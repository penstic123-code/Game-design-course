Chapter 14

Randomness & Probability

Randomness is a seasoning: in the right amount it delights, too much and it is all luck.

Learning goals

about 40 min
  • Tell input randomness from output randomness, and price a random reward with expected value.
  • Explain why people’s probability intuition so often goes wrong (the gambler’s fallacy, small samples, base-rate neglect), and why pity systems and shuffle bags were invented.
  • Say why the sum of several dice is bell-shaped and how the standard deviation changes with the number of dice, and use that to decide whether a random quantity should be split into several rolls or merged into one.
1

What it isWhere do you put the randomness?

Two kinds of randomness can both be “up to luck,” yet one is thrilling and the other makes people want to throw the controller. The difference is whether the randomness happens before the decision or after it, and it has little to do with how large the probability is.

In Tetris, which piece comes next is random, but the player sees it first and then decides where to put it, so cleverness still pays. A swing with a “75% hit chance” is different: the decision is already made, and all that is left is to wait for the dice.

🎴 Input randomness

A random “problem” comes first, and then the player works out how to solve it. Examples: what the next Tetris piece is, which cards you draw in an opening hand, how a map is generated. The player’s decisions still determine the result, and the randomness only makes every game different.

🎯 Output randomness

After the player has made their decision, the “outcome” is randomly determined. Examples: an attack with a 75% hit chance, a critical hit, opening a chest. The player can influence the probability, but the result is out of their control, which is why it is exciting and also easily makes people feel “cheated.”

The next-piece preview in Tetris: which piece comes is random, but the player sees the next piece before dropping the one in hand. Judgment still decides success or failure, and the randomness just keeps setting new problems.
An attack with a 75% hit chance: which target to pick and which skill to use are the player’s decisions. Whether the blow connects or misses is up to the dice. One miss in four is normal, and exciting too.
Showing 95% and still MISSing: this is where output randomness most easily backfires. Players feel “cheated,” yet one in twenty is bound to miss, and people are especially intolerant of failures that were “almost certain.”
Rule of thumb: to make the player feel “I won because I’m smart,” use more input randomness. For tense, thrilling bursts, add a little output randomness, but don’t let it rule who wins.
2

How it was inventedA gambling match that was interrupted

How should the stakes be split to be fair?

In seventeenth-century France, the gaming table was a social place. A gambling-mad knight, the Chevalier de Méré (real name Antoine Gombaud), ran into a nagging problem and went to ask the mathematician Pascal.

The situation: A and B each stake 50 gold coins, agreeing that whoever first wins 3 rounds takes all 100. When A leads 2 to 1, the match is forced to stop and can’t go on. How should the 100 gold coins be split to be fair?

This problem had troubled people for nearly two hundred years by then, and nobody had given an answer that satisfied both sides. Guess first: how much should A get?

1494 The Italian Luca Pacioli first discussed this problem, in a mathematics book, and his answer was to split by rounds already won: 2 to 1, so A gets two thirds. It sounds reasonable, but has a loophole: if A had won 1 round and B none, this split would give A everything, even though B still has a chance of coming back.
1654 Pascal and Fermat discussed the problem in a series of letters. Their turning point: don’t look back at who has won how many rounds, look forward and list all the possible endings from here, and work out each by its probability. Fermat counted with combinations and Pascal worked backward step by step, and they reached the same answer: A 75, B 25. Historians often treat these letters as the starting point of probability theory.
1657 The Dutchman Christiaan Huygens heard of the correspondence and wrote On Reasoning in Games of Chance, the first systematic account of the method. The concept of “expectation” also took shape in the book: what you can expect to get is the probability-weighted average of the possible outcomes.
1713 Jacob Bernoulli’s Ars Conjectandi (published after his death) proves that when the same thing is repeated enough times, the actual average gets closer and closer to the theoretical value. This is the law of large numbers, and it also explains why expected value can be used to price random rewards.

3

What it isExpected value: how to price randomness

What Pascal and Fermat computed is the expected value (EV): for each outcome, probability × value, then add them up. It answers: if this is repeated a great many times, what is the average per try?

Concept card

Expected Value

Expected value, EV for short

Pascal and Fermat · the method was laid down in their 1654 correspondence; Christiaan Huygens · his 1657 On Reasoning in Games of Chance gave “expectation” its shape

Multiply each outcome’s probability by its value and add them all up. Repeated many times, you get about this much per try.

ExampleA chest gives 50 coins with 10%, 10 coins with 30%, and 0 with 60%: expected value = 0.1×50 + 0.3×10 = 8 coins.

Example: opening a chest

10% gives 50 gold, 30% gives 10 gold, 60% gives 0.
EV = 0.1×50 + 0.3×10 + 0.6×0 = 8 gold.

Example: a six-sided die

EV = (1+2+3+4+5+6)/6 = 3.5.
You can never roll 3.5, but roll many times and the average will come close to it.

In design the most common use is “pricing”: if a “20% chance to crit for ×2 damage,” the average damage rises by 1 + 0.2 = 1.2 times, and a numbers designer can put it on the same scale as “a flat +20% damage.”

Opening a chest: three outcomes each with a probability, and the expectation works out to 8 gold. Designers use it to decide how costly a chest should be and whether it is worth opening.
Buying a lottery ticket: the expected value of each ticket is the average of all the prizes weighted by probability. The lottery operator has to make money, so the average payout is always less than what you spend. You are buying a chance to dream, not a return.
Same expectation, different feel: one chest “always gives a steady 8,” another “gives 80 only once in ten,” and both have an expectation of 8. The first feels solid, the second is either wild joy or empty hands. Expected value cares only about the average and says nothing about the experience. That calls for the variance below.

Try itA dice distribution simulator

Variance (and its square root, the standard deviation) describes how “scattered” the outcomes are. With the same average, play with large variance feels more like gambling, with occasional ecstasy and occasional collapse; play with small variance feels more like a game of skill, with stable and predictable results.

Dice are the best tool for seeing this. 1d12 (roll one 12-sided die) makes every number equally likely. 2d6 (roll two six-sided dice and add) mostly lands in the middle, in a bell shape. Pick one, roll many times, and see how far the red dots (theory) and the bars (your rolls) differ.

Try it: first pick 1d12 and roll 1000 times or more, then switch to 2d6 and roll 1000 times or more, and compare the shapes of the two charts. Which one has rarer extremes?

The bars are the frequencies from your simulation, and the red dots are the theoretical probabilities. With few rolls the bars look “ragged,” and only with many do they come close to theory. For 2d6, the theoretical probability of a 7 = 6/36 ≈ 16.7%, and for 1d12 each number is 1/12 ≈ 8.3%.

Why it worksWhy does adding several dice turn it into a bell shape?

2d6 is more “concentrated” than 1d12, and this is a general rule: when many small independent random amounts are added, the shape of the total approaches a bell. The intuition is not hard. For the total to be very large, every single die has to be large at the same time, and such “everything lines up” combinations get rarer and rarer. Combinations where some are high and some low and they cancel out are far more numerous.

The central limit theorem (CLT)

One six-sided die has a mean of 3.5 and a standard deviation of about 1.708. The sum of n independent dice has a mean of 3.5n and a standard deviation √n times that of one die: 2 times at n=4, 4 times at n=16. The total itself is about n times bigger, so the relative spread shrinks as 1/√n.

Advanced: how the standard deviation is computed

One six-sided die has a variance of 35/12 ≈ 2.917 and a standard deviation of ≈ 1.708. For independent random amounts the variances add, so the sum of n dice has variance 35n/12 and standard deviation 1.708×√n. The standard deviation of the average roll is 1.708/√n, which narrows as you keep rolling.

When the conditions fit (the terms are independent, have finite variance, and no single term dominates the total), the distribution of the sum of many random amounts looks more and more like a normal distribution.

In a game: small random events such as a “20% chance to crit” average out over a long fight, while a one-off big chest does not. To make players feel “steady most of the time, with the occasional surprise,” split one big random event into many small ones. For the thrill of “one roll decides everything,” merge them into a single big one. Best-of-several matches let the stronger side win more reliably for the same reason.

Source: a classic result of probability theory. The early normal approximation to the binomial distribution is usually credited to de Moivre (1733) and Laplace, and was later made rigorous by Lyapunov, Lindeberg and others.

The Central Limit Theorem

Central limit theorem, CLT for short

When many small independent random amounts are added, the distribution of the total looks more and more like a bell (the normal distribution).

What it isThe traps of probability intuition: isn’t it red’s turn?

On August 18, 1913, at a roulette table in the Monte Carlo casino in Monaco, the ball fell into black 26 times in a row. The gamblers, watching the long string of black on the scoreboard, felt more and more that “it’s unscientific for it to stay black any longer, it must be red this time,” and piled their chips onto red.

That night the casino made a fortune. The roulette wheel has no memory, and every spin is independent: whether this spin lands on black or red has nothing to do with how many blacks came before. This judgment error is the gambler’s fallacy, which is also called the “Monte Carlo fallacy” because of that event.

Concept card

The Gambler’s Fallacy

Gambler’s fallacy (also called the Monte Carlo fallacy)

The 1913 roulette run at the Monte Carlo casino is the best-known case; Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman · in 1971 they studied the “law of small numbers” systematically in psychology

Believing an independent event will “make up for” earlier results. After several blacks in a row, the next one feels due to be red.

ExampleA coin lands heads 5 times in a row, and the next flip is still 50/50, because the coin has no memory.

🪙 The gambler’s fallacy

“Five heads in a row, next time must be tails!” Wrong. Independent events have no memory, and the next flip is still 50–50. Randomness will not “make it up to you.”

🔬 Small samples can deceive

In 10 flips, getting 70% heads is not at all unusual; only with 1000 flips does it settle near 50%. A player plays only a few dozen times in a lifetime, so what they experience is always the “ragged small sample.”

See for yourself: flip a fair coin in each group.

Heuristics: the brain’s “low-effort algorithms”

The brain can’t do an exact calculation every time, so it uses shortcuts to estimate quickly. These shortcuts are heuristics. They are good enough most of the time, but on probability questions they err in a consistent direction.

  • Representativeness heuristic: HTHTTH “looks more random,” so it feels more likely, and HHHHHH “doesn’t look random,” so it feels impossible. With a fair coin, any specific sequence of 6 flips has probability (1/2)6 = 1/64.
    The gambler’s fallacy and too much trust in a small sample both come from here. Tversky and Kahneman called it the “law of small numbers” in 1971: people expect a small sample to look as even as the whole population.
    Ignoring base rates has the same root (question 4, base rate neglect).
  • Availability heuristic: forums are full of screenshots of “two golds in a ten-pull,” because people who didn’t get it don’t post, so its frequency gets overestimated.
  • Misjudging accumulated small probabilities: “only 1.5% each time, so ten tries should be fine” (question 2), “0.5% drop rate, and 200 runs should give me even odds” (question 5), and in the birthday problem people badly underestimate the chance that “some pair collides” (question 6).

Design implication: players feel probability and don’t compute it. There is always a gap between the “displayed probability” and the “probability the player feels,” and mechanisms like pity timers and shuffle bags, which adjust for how it feels, exist because of that gap.

Heuristics and Biases

Heuristics and biases

Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman · 1974 Science paper “Judgment under uncertainty: Heuristics and biases”

People estimate probability with rules of thumb. They work well enough most of the time, and on probability questions they err in a consistent direction.

ExampleForums are full of “two golds in a ten-pull” screenshots, and players who didn’t get one don’t post, so players overestimate how common the drop is (availability heuristic).

The “wrongful accusations” of an anti-cheat system: the picture from question 4. Real cheaters are only 1%, and however accurate the system is, it will sweep in a batch of innocent people, and most of those flagged are innocent. Saying “how many out of 1000 people” is far easier to understand than saying “95%.”
The “lucky ones” on forums: those who post screenshots are the ones who got the drop, and those who didn’t don’t post. You see only the successful samples, and so overestimate how common “getting the drop” is.
“A near-miss”: drawing cards without replacement: three aces have been drawn from a deck, so there are fewer aces left, and the probability that the next card is an ace really has changed. “Having memory” is correct here. The gambler’s fallacy applies only where each event is independent (coins, dice, roulette).

People disagree about these shortcuts. Gerd Gigerenzer and others argue that they are quite “smart” in real environments, and that people do much better when a problem is stated in frequency format (“how many out of 1,000 people”) (Gigerenzer and Hoffrage, 1995). Both claims have experiments behind them. For designers the conclusion is the same: don’t expect players to calculate probability correctly.

How it was inventedThe person who got burned by luck for a hundred pulls

The “random numbers” in a computer come from an algorithm and are pseudorandom, which is good enough for games. The designer’s real trouble is not whether the randomness is “random enough” but that pure randomness can be too cruel.

Take an item with a 1% drop rate. Many players’ gut says, “after 100 pulls, one must have dropped.” Guess first: of everyone who pulls 100 times, how many still get nothing?

These people quit the game, swear, and write long posts on forums. Designers found that “the probability is fair” alone couldn’t persuade them, so they came up with several ways to hold up “the unluckiest people”:

Early on Many early games were purely random: every roll independent. In Tetris this could mean waiting dozens of pieces in a row for the long bar, and the player feels “this game has it in for me.”
Later In the modern Tetris guideline, pieces come from a “shuffle bag”: seven kinds of piece go in a bag, are shuffled and taken out one by one, and when it’s empty another bag is filled. Each kind appears exactly once in every seven pieces, and at most 12 pieces separate two long bars.
Another road In Dota 2, effects like crits use a “pseudo-random distribution”: each time it doesn’t trigger, the chance of triggering next time rises a little, and resets to zero after a trigger. For a 20% crit, with its published constant C ≈ 0.0557, the probability on the nth attempt is 5.57% × n, the long-run average is still 20%, but it can go at most 17 attempts in a row without triggering.

What it isPity and shuffle bags

🎰

Pure randomness

Each roll is judged independently by probability. Simple, but possibly extremely unlucky.

🛡️

Pity

Once consecutive misses reach the cap, the next one is a guaranteed hit. It cuts off the “unluckiest” tail.

🎒

Shuffle bag

Shuffle 100 outcomes (including 1 winner) and draw them one by one, reshuffling when empty. Guarantees exactly 1 win in every bag.

A deck of playing cards: shuffled and dealt one by one, and through the whole deck each card appears exactly once. A shuffle bag is this method: lock down in advance how many times each outcome appears, and make only the order random.
The 90-pull pity in gacha: many mobile games state openly “after 90 consecutive pulls without the top rarity, you are guaranteed one” (the exact number varies by game). Even the unluckiest person gets 90 pulls at most, and it is not a bottomless pit.
“A near-miss”: a bag too small: if the bag holds only two slips, one win and one blank, then once the player draws “win” they can be sure the next is blank. The smaller the bag, the more predictable it is, and the surprise is gone. The size of the bag is a trade-off between “guarding against bad luck” and “keeping suspense.”

Common misconceptions

  • “At a 1% drop rate, 100 pulls is guaranteed to get it”: wrong, about 63.4% get it at least once, and about 36.6% end up with nothing.
  • “Same expected value, same experience”: wrong. Expected value cares only about the average, and variance decides what you actually feel.
  • “Shuffle bags and pity are cheating, no longer random”: they are still random, only no longer independent from one draw to the next, and that is a deliberate design trade-off.
  • “Drawing cards is also an independent event, so drawing the same card repeatedly means ‘something’s fishy’”: drawing without replacement has memory; only with replacement is it independent. Tell them apart before judging.
  • “The central limit theorem says everything becomes a bell”: it requires the terms to be independent, to have finite variance, and for no single term to dominate.

Reflection and debate

  • Are heuristics necessarily “errors”? Kahneman and Tversky stress the systematic bias they cause, while Gigerenzer and others stress their reasonableness in real environments. These are different views in the field, and both have experimental support.
  • Pity and shuffle bags make players feel better, but players may also learn to “count the pity”: stop playing until they’re near the pity, and walk away once they get it. This is a trade-off a designer has to weigh.
  • Is it fair to exploit players’ misjudgment of probability to raise spending, for example showing only the “highest probability” and not the combined probability? This is a question of design ethics.

Key points

  • Input randomness comes before the decision and adds variety; output randomness comes after the decision and adds tension but weakens the sense of control.
  • Expected value = the sum of probability × value, born of “how to split the money when a match is interrupted.” It prices random rewards, and doesn’t describe the experience of a single game.
  • Independent events have no memory (the gambler’s fallacy), while players experience small samples and can see very extreme luck.
  • The sum of several dice forms a bell curve, with the standard deviation growing as √n and the fluctuation of the average shrinking as 1/√n. Splitting one big random event into many small ones makes the experience steadier.
  • People estimate probability with heuristics, easily swayed by “how much it looks like” and “how many come to mind”: the probability a player feels ≠ the true probability.
  • Use pity and shuffle bags to cut off the “unluckiest” tail, while watching their effect on the average and on surprise.
Randomness isn’t better the more “real” it is; it has to fit human intuition: know that people misjudge probability, then price with expected value, and rein in extremes with pity and splitting.
