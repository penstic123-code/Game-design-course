Chapter 20

Genre Design Patterns

A genre is a contract made of “a loop plus a promise.”

Learning goals

about 45 min
  • Explain the “core loop + player promise” of each of five common genres, and tell how the “genre” label grew.
  • Spot survivorship bias, explain why “what successful games have in common” is not the same as “the reasons for success,” and know which data to look at in addition.
  • Use players’ schemas and expectations to judge whether “breaking a genre convention” will bring delight or confusion, and say why games of the same kind always cluster together.
1

What it isGenre = core loop + player promise

You see the word “roguelike” on a store page, and without opening a single screenshot you have already guessed a good deal: every run will be a bit different, dying will hurt, and you will probably be unable to resist one more run. Nobody said any of this; the word itself carried it.

So to a designer a genre is more like a contract. By choosing this genre a player is expecting a certain core loop (from Chapter 2, the set of actions players repeat again and again), and also expecting certain player promises, that is, “you will get this kind of experience.”

🔁 Core loop

The set of actions players repeat over and over: for example “jump → land → move forward,” or “draw a card → play a card → win → get a new card.”

🤝 Player promise

The experience this genre guarantees by default: platformers promise “failure is my execution’s fault”; roguelikes promise “every run is different”; puzzles promise “the moment it clicks feels great.”

Platformers: you buy one to get steadily more precise with your controls. As long as forgiving rules like “coyote time” (you can still jump for a moment after running off an edge) are kept in order, dying makes you think “my bad, one more try.”
Puzzle: the promise is “I can work it out by thinking.” With enough clues but no direct answer, you get that “aha.”
Walking into a Sichuan restaurant: you assume “it will be spicy.” Opening a horror film, you assume “I’ll be scared.” Cuisines and film genres alike are contracts nobody wrote down.
Breaking the promise: the marketing says “precision platforming,” but the collision is fuzzy, and you clearly land on a platform and still fall. Players don’t mind that it’s hard; they feel cheated, because the promise that “failure is my fault” was not kept.
2

What it isDesign patterns of five genres

Each promise differs, and so does the way of keeping it. Each of the five genres below grew up around its own promise.

🏃 Platformer: precise control + level teaching

Promise: a fair challenge, and when I die it’s my fault.

Feel is the foundation: jump height, acceleration and air control must be tuned to be very smooth. Common forgiving tricks include coyote time and “input buffering” (pressing jump slightly early still counts).

The level itself is the tutorial: learn one move in a safe place first, then add danger, and finally combine (covered in Chapter 11). Checkpoints should be dense and restarts quick, so that “try again” costs almost nothing.

💀 Roguelike: procedural generation + permadeath + two kinds of growth

Promise: every run is a new story. The name comes from the old game Rogue of around 1980.

Procedural generation re-assembles maps, enemies and rewards every run, so players can’t memorize a routine. Permadeath gives every decision weight, because dying means starting over.

In-run growth works only within this run and is gone when you die; meta-progression is kept after death. Works with meta-progression are often classed as roguelites.

🃏 Deckbuilder: deck + random draws + synergy

Promise: my cleverness builds a powerful deck.

The player first builds the deck (choosing which cards to add) and then draws at random from it in battle. The strategy is in the building, and the tension comes from the drawing.

Synergy is the heart of the fun: put a few cards together and the effect exceeds the sum of the parts, for example “stack armor” with “more armor means more damage.” A good design leaves several synergy routes instead of only one best solution. Adding cards is always more tempting than cutting them, but the thicker the deck, the harder it is to draw the core combination, so “trimming the deck” is itself an interesting choice.

🏰 Strategy / 4X: the triangle of economy, expansion and military

Promise: my long-term planning makes an empire grow. 4X stands for four verbs: explore, expand, exploit, exterminate.

The three hold each other in check: only developing the economy gets you attacked, only building an army leaves you broke, and only expanding stretches your front too long. The player must keep weighing “invest now” against “be safe now.”

Compounding is the core: early economic investment snowballs (covered by the faucets and sinks of Chapter 15), so “sinks” like war and upkeep are needed to keep the snowball from going too far. Turns are long and information plentiful, and the interface and hints are almost as important as the rules.

🧩 Puzzle: insight + clues

Promise: I can work it out by thinking. The core experience is insight: you are stuck, and suddenly you see the rule and it all clears.

There must be enough clues, but the answer must not be spelled out: too few and it is guessing, too many and there is no insight. Start from one simple rule, add variations level by level, and let the player learn in small examples before using it in big problems.

You also have to guard against “brute-force trial and error”: if clicking every option in turn gets you through, the insight is gone. A player stuck too long needs hints, but it should be the player who decides whether to take one.

What it isTwo kinds of growth in roguelikes: in-run and meta

A roguelike has a contradiction: permadeath makes people tense, but if one death wipes everything, players find it hard to accept. Its solution is to split growth into two layers.

Schematic: each rising line is the growth of power within one run, and ✖ is death. On the left every run restarts from the same starting point; on the right failure leaves a permanent bonus and the starting point gets higher and higher.

In-run growth

Each run starts from zero, bringing immediate choices and surprises. What is lost is lost when you die, so decisions carry weight.

Meta-progression

It lets failure accumulate something, reducing frustration; but too much lets newcomers “grind” instead of “learn,” so it needs restraint.

Vampire Survivors: gold earned each run buys permanent upgrades, a typical meta-progression that lets even the worst run “earn something.”
Hades: death is part of the story, you return to the start after dying and upgrade permanent abilities with what you saved, while the rooms and blessing combinations differ every run.
Working out: each workout’s state is “in-run,” and the fitness left by months of persistence is “meta.” With meta-progression, “a poor workout today” isn’t wasted.
A near miss: meta upgrades given too generously. After a few runs the character is strong enough not to need thought, the challenge is ground flat, and the player’s fun changes from “how to win” to “grind enough and you win.” Meta-progression should cushion frustration, not replace skill.
4

How it was inventedHow the word “genre” grew

Nobody announced one day that “from now on there are roguelikes in the world.” A genre is first a batch of similar games, then someone wanting to give them a common name, and only after the name is chosen do arguments about its borders begin. Chapter 1 saw the same thing: the word “game” has no agreed definition, only family resemblance. Genres are the same, only younger and quarrelsome.

From Rogue to “roguelike”

Around 1980 the students Michael Toy and Glenn Wichman at the University of California, Santa Cruz made Rogue, a game that drew dungeons in characters. The map was shown on the terminal through the curses library written by Ken Arnold. It spread with BSD UNIX and was in 4.2BSD in 1983, but without source code. Not until 4.3BSD in 1986 was the source available. Anyone who wanted to play “something like it” had to start from scratch.

So hobbyists made similar games on their own: Hack, Moria, Angband. But players were scattered among different Usenet newsgroups and found it hard to reach each other.

In 1993 someone proposed merging these discussions under one name. According to Wikipedia’s account, it was argued for about three weeks and in the end rec.games.roguelike.* was chosen, on the grounds that Rogue was the oldest of this kind of game, making it “the least bad option.” One word, and that is how a batch of games was framed as “a genre.”

Once the genre had a name, its borders began to drift. In 2008 the International Roguelike Development Conference met in Berlin, and the community drafted the “Berlin Interpretation.” What it lists is a set of factors, and it gives no definition. The high-weight factors include random environment generation, permadeath, turn-based play and grid movement.

It takes ADOM, Angband, Linley’s Dungeon Crawl, NetHack and Rogue as benchmarks to measure “how roguelike a game is.” The same year the indie game Spelunky appeared. It met only some of the factors yet was counted as a roguelike by many, and so terms like roguelite came along too.

Around 1980 Rogue (Toy, Wichman, using Arnold’s curses library): a dungeon and an adventurer represented by an @ drawn in characters.
1983 / 1986 4.2BSD carried Rogue but without source; the source became available only with 4.3BSD. People who wanted to make similar games had to remake it from scratch, so a batch of “look-alike” games appeared.
1993 Several Usenet newsgroups merge and are named rec.games.roguelike.*. One name framed a batch of games as a genre.
2008 The Berlin Interpretation: a list of factors, not a definition.
From 2008 Works like Spelunky that meet only some factors become popular, terms like roguelite appear, and the boundary keeps being argued over.

How it was inventedWald’s airplane: “who is missing” is the key

Where on the plane should armor go?

During the Second World War the US military wanted to add armor to bombers, but armor is heavy and can’t cover the whole plane. Where should it go? Researchers examined planes that had returned, and the wings and fuselage were riddled with bullet holes, with few near the engines. The intuition came naturally: “reinforce where the holes are.”

Abraham Wald, working with Columbia University’s Statistical Research Group (SRG), saw the other side: these were the planes that made it back. The places with few holes were exactly the places where “if hit, it doesn’t come back,” and those planes were not in the sample at all. What needed reinforcing was the places on returning planes with no bullet holes.

Wald’s set of memoranda from 1943 dealt with exactly how to estimate the survival probability of different parts of an aircraft when only the survivors can be seen. One of them, titled “A Method of Estimating Plane Vulnerability Based on Damage of Survivors,” was reprinted by the Center for Naval Analyses in 1980. Mangel and Samaniego gave a popular introduction in the Journal of the American Statistical Association in 1984.

The details of the much-retold “bullet-hole diagram” differ across retellings (who drew the diagram, the exact data), so the widely accepted main point is taken here: who is missing from the analyzed sample. The planes and numbers below are illustrative, not historical data.

Why it worksLooking only at winners misleads: survivorship bias

You want to make “the next hit,” so you study the 20 most successful indie games and find that “70% are pixel art” and “about a third have roguelike elements.” The conclusion seems obvious: make a pixel-art roguelike!

But among the several thousand that failed, how many are pixel art? If it is also 70%, this “discovery” carries no information at all. Wald’s planes are the same trap.

Survivorship bias

The principle: observe only the samples that survived a filter, and you take “features the filter kept” for “the cause of success.” To test whether “feature X is the cause of success,” you must look at three more things: the base rate (how much of all games has X to begin with), the failed samples (is the success rate of games with X higher than without X?), and the sample size (with 20 winners, how big is the random fluctuation?).

What you see is “how much of the successes is pixel art,” and what you want to know is “how much of the pixel-art games succeeded.” These are two different questions, unless you also know what share of all games is pixel art and what share of all games succeeded.

Advanced: writing it in symbols

What you see is P(pixel art | success), and what you want to know is P(success | pixel art). P(A | B) is read “the probability of A given B.” The two are equal only if you also know P(pixel art) and P(success).

Sources: Wald · 1943 · wrote the memoranda on aircraft survivability at the Statistical Research Group · had to estimate the planes that did not return. Mangel and Samaniego · 1984 · presented Wald’s work (JASA). “Survivorship bias” is a common concept in statistics and behavioral science, and the difference between conditional probabilities is basic probability theory.

Looking only at the samples that were selected to remain makes you mistake the traits of the survivors for the cause of success. Ask: what about those that did not remain?

ExampleReturning planes had bullet holes concentrated on the wings and fuselage, yet what needed reinforcing was the area near the engines, which had the fewest holes: planes hit there did not come back and were not in the sample.

“Hit games all have online play, so make it online”: if 40% of all games had online play to begin with, 40% of winners having it says nothing. What to compare is the success rate with online play and without.
“So-and-so dropped out, founded a company and became a billionaire, so dropping out helps you succeed”: we don’t see the far more numerous people who dropped out and stayed unknown.
“Old houses are all sturdy, and today’s houses are poor quality”: the badly built old houses collapsed or were torn down long ago, and we see only the ones that survived.
A right example: run a control. Randomly split players into two groups, give a feature to only one, and compare retention. With no “selection,” a difference can be due to that feature.

Why it worksA genre is a psychological expectation: delight and confusion when breaking convention

You open a game labeled “platformer,” and your fingers are already ready for “hold to jump higher.” If this time the key press must wait half a second before the jump, it is irritating. But if on the tenth minute the game lets you discover that “the jump button is also a grappling hook,” you laugh. Both break your expectations, yet the feelings are poles apart.

Schema and expectation: the psychology of the genre promise

The principle: in psychology a schema is the brain’s ready-made framework for “what things of this kind are usually like.” The idea is often traced to Bartlett (1932).

He had British subjects retell an unfamiliar North American Indian folk tale (“The War of the Ghosts”) and found that, in retelling, people rewrote the parts that did not fit their own culture’s expectations into something that “made sense.” Expectation reshapes how people understand new information. How strong the conclusions of this kind of early memory research are has also been much discussed since.

When players see a genre name, a schema starts up: how to operate, what to expect, what counts as fair. If the game fits the schema, players get started fast and with little effort. If it goes against it, players have to relearn, the cost being confusion and the gain possibly delight. A common view is: things that break expectations but make sense afterwards are the likeliest to feel like a delight. Breaking expectations and not making sense (inaccurate collision, going against the genre’s core promise) makes people feel cheated.

Sources: Frederic Bartlett · 1932 · Remembering · introduced the concept of schema and applied it to memory research. “Expectation and delight” is common design experience in the games industry. The distinction between “surface / core” below is a design judgment, and in reality depends on execution.

A near miss: a “roguelike” that is the same every run. A near miss: a puzzle you can brute-force. The game doesn’t punish errors, and clicking every button in turn clears it. The promise “I can work it out by thinking” falls through, and the player is bored.

Why it worksWhy do games of the same kind always cluster? Local optima

Once a genre takes off, the games everyone makes grow more and more alike: the same core loop, the same upgrade tree, the same art style. Nobody ordered it; each person is just “making what already works a bit better.”

It is like a climber looking for the highest point in thick fog: he can see only what is underfoot, and goes wherever it is higher. When every direction around him slopes down, he stops. But that peak may be only a low hill, and the higher one lies on the other side of the valley.

Local optima and “hill climbing”

On a “landscape,” improving only by small steps easily leaves you stuck at a local optimum: the highest point in the surrounding area but not the highest overall. To reach a higher peak you have to walk downhill first, that is, get worse for a while before getting better. The evolutionary biologist Sewall Wright first drew this kind of “fitness landscape” in 1932, and the metaphor has since been borrowed widely in optimization, product design and elsewhere. Seeing “genre clustering” as hill climbing is an analogy.

Source: Sewall Wright, “The roles of mutation, inbreeding, crossbreeding and selection in evolution,” Proceedings of the Sixth International Congress of Genetics, 1932.

Ask these four questions when you meet a “summary of success stories”

  1. How much of all games of this kind had this trait to begin with? (base rate)
  2. Is the success rate of games with it higher than of those without? (failed samples)
  3. How large is the sample? Would it change with another batch? (random fluctuation)
  4. Are other factors (publishing, timing, luck) at work at the same time? (confounding)

Common misconceptions

  • “Successful works all have X, so making X will succeed.” It ignores that failed works have plenty of X too (the base rate), and factors like luck, publishing and timing.
  • “Mixing two hot genres must be even hotter.” The promises of two genres may conflict (for example the precise control of “failure is my fault” against the uncertainty of “random every time”), so check the promises first.
  • “Genre conventions exist to be broken.” Conventions are a shortcut that saves players effort. Breaking one or two on purpose brings delight; break them all and you have a game nobody can read.
  • “A genre has one official definition.” It is agreed by the community and it drifts, as the draw-the-line interactive already showed you firsthand.

Reflection and debate

Survivorship bias warns against over-reading successful cases. Successful cases still have reference value: combined with base rates and failed samples, and with a control, you can still learn from them. Details of Wald’s story differ between retellings, and what is taken here is the widely accepted core.

Whether something is “delight or confusion” differs by player group: veterans want freshness, while new players may need familiarity, and the judgment in the text is only design experience.

Local optima have another side: a low peak may mean a stable play style that people buy into, and treating every one as a “trap to escape” is one-sided.

“Genres are agreed upon by the community” is a common view, and some think genres correspond to stable structures of play. Think about it: are genres more “fact” or “convention”?

Key points

  • Genre = core loop + player promise. Think clearly about what experience you promise the player first, and then decide which genre to use.
  • Platformers rely on feel and level teaching; puzzles on clues and insight; deckbuilders put strategy in building and tension in drawing; the core of 4X is the mutual check of economy, expansion and military; roguelikes rely on procedural generation + permadeath, with in-run growth bringing drama and meta-progression cushioning frustration.
  • A genre is first a batch of similar games and then someone naming it (as when a newsgroup was named roguelike in 1993); the boundary is agreed on by the community and drifts.
  • Survivorship bias (Wald’s plane): when analyzing a sample, first ask “who is missing”; look at the base rate, failed samples and sample size.
  • A genre is the player’s psychological expectation: breaking a surface convention is low risk, while breaking a core promise needs new fun as compensation; improving only in small steps easily leaves you stuck at a local optimum, which is why games of one kind cluster.
A genre is a contract of expectations written to the player: use it to save effort, break it on purpose now and then, but don’t take “what winners have in common” as a recipe.
