Chapter 18

Multiplayer Design

Once there are other people, a game gains something new: relationships.

Learning goals

  • Judge whether the players in a multiplayer game are cooperating, competing or a mix of both, say whether it is zero-sum or non-zero-sum, and why a bad experience like “playing a co-op game alone” appears.
  • Explain why the Elo rating had to be invented, how it turns the gap between “expected” and “result” into points, and then use positive and negative feedback to tune the suspense of a match.
  • Explain why strangers are willing to cooperate (reciprocity, reputation), why a division of labor benefits both sides, and why communities so easily become winner-take-all.
about 45 min
1

What it isStart with the relationship: cooperation, competition, or both

You join a four-person dungeon in a room full of strangers, and no one on the team is someone you know. Halfway through the boss fight, your health is down to a sliver, and the team’s healer throws you a big heal, with his own mana scraped to the bottom. Why would he help you? He will never meet you again, and helping you does him no good at all.

To answer that, you first have to be clear what the relationship between the players actually is. “Four people playing together” can be quite different things, and each relationship sets the designer a completely different problem.

🤝

Cooperation

Everyone works against the system (the level, the boss). The hard part is division of labor and communication; the worst case is a strong player “finishing it for everyone else.”

⚔️

Competition

Players are each other’s opponents. It comes down to skill and strategy; the worst case is a newcomer getting crushed, and the loser soon leaving.

🎭

Mixed

Team against team, or “cooperation with betrayal mixed in.” It relies on trust and game theory, and is the likeliest to produce stories.

There is a separate axis too: whether the players are symmetric. In chess both sides have exactly the same rules and resources, which is fair and easy to understand, and the result depends entirely on skill. A one-versus-many hunting game is asymmetric: different roles and different play, a rich experience, but hard to balance, needing repeated testing with real people until each role’s win rate seems acceptable.

2

What it isZero-sum, non-zero-sum, and “everyone is clever and everyone loses”

Zero-sum

What one side gains is what the other loses, and the total is fixed. Chess and poker are like this: if I win, you lose, and the players’ interests are fully opposed.

Non-zero-sum

The total can change, so both can win, or both can lose. Trade and cooperative missions are like this. Only here is there “the possibility of cooperation,” and also “the temptation to betray.”

The most troublesome kind of non-zero-sum is the social dilemma: everyone makes the choice that is smartest for themselves, and together everyone loses. You will play one yourself later. First, a few concrete examples to see what these words look like in real games.

Pandemic: a cooperative board game designed by Matt Leacock and published by Z-Man Games in 2008. Everyone fights a disease that spreads on its own, winning together or losing together. This is pure cooperation, non-zero-sum: the opponent is the system, not each other.
Chess: if I take one of your pieces, you have one fewer; after a game the winner is +1, the loser −1, and the total is always 0. This is pure competition, zero-sum.
Werewolf / Mafia: reportedly created by Dmitry Davidoff in the psychology department of Moscow State University (he remembers the first game as the spring of 1987). The good guys must cooperate to find the bad guys, and the bad guys must lie together. Cooperation and betrayal in the same room, so every game is a story.
The ancestor of Monopoly: The Landlord’s Game: Elizabeth Magie patented it in 1904 and gave the same board two sets of rules. Under the “Monopolist” rules, whoever collects the most rent wins and the others go bankrupt; under the “Prosperity” rules, when someone buys land everyone benefits, and the game ends when the poorest player’s wealth has doubled, so everyone wins. One board, one zero-sum rule set and one non-zero-sum. Thirty years later the Parker Brothers bought her patent, and only the first set remained in what we know.
Counterexample: a co-op game turned into a one-person game: nominally cooperative, but someone knows everything and grabs the commands, and the others can only follow, or only watch. The relationship was designed as “cooperation,” yet the experience is “one player and a few spectators.” What is missing is a decision in every player’s hands that only they can make.
3

What it isFeedback loops: the snowball and catch-up mechanics

In the second half of a Monopoly game, everyone knows how it will end: someone owns half the street, and someone pays rent at every step but must keep rolling the dice anyway. This loop of “whoever leads finds it easier to keep leading” is called positive feedback. Every multiplayer game runs into it, because players compare themselves with each other, and where there is comparison there is “leading.”

The remedy is to build another loop the other way: restrain the leader a bit and help the trailer a bit, and the gap is pulled back. This is negative feedback, popularly the catch-up mechanic. The design goal is not to wipe out one of the two but to tune the knob: reward the winner, but don’t make the reward so large that a comeback is impossible.

Items in racing games: the Mario Kart series hands out items by rank, and players further behind are more likely to get a powerful item, so the last lap often turns around. This is negative feedback, and the suspense is preserved.
The snowball and Monopoly: give a push at the top of the hill and the snowball grows as it rolls. Monopoly is the same: more property collects more rent, and more rent buys more. This is positive feedback, and a small early edge is magnified into a foregone conclusion.
Counterexample: catch-up tuned too far: the leading car is chased and pelted by items, the player in front feels their skill is being punished, and those behind can catch up without effort. If negative feedback is too strong, “doing well” earns nothing.
4

What it isHow to match strong with weak: the Elo rating

A catch-up mechanic evens things out within one game. An earlier step is to not seat badly mismatched people at the same table: strong with strong, newcomers with newcomers. That needs a score everyone can compare. The classic approach is Elo, used in chess, and the ranked systems of many games were inspired by it, though each implements it differently.

Expected score: EA = 1 / (1 + 10(RB − RA)/400)
New rating: R'A = RA + K × (S − EA) (win S=1, draw 0.5, loss 0)

The reading is plain: first predict A’s score from the rating gap of the two players (with a gap of 400, the stronger is expected to score about 91%), and after the game see how much the actual result beat the prediction, and adjust by that difference. K is the sensitivity: a large K makes scores rise and fall fast, suited to newcomers; a small K keeps scores steady. In each game one side gains and the other loses the same amount, so the total stays the same.

The curve is drawn from the formula (K doesn’t affect the curve, only how much each adjustment is).

An upset: a 1200-rated small player beats an 1800-rated big one. His expected score was only about 0.03, with K=32 he gains 32×(1−0.03)≈31 points, and the big one loses the same 31. If the big one wins, he gains only about 1 point: winning against someone he should have beaten, the system learns almost nothing.
A new account’s first games: many rating systems give newcomers a larger K, so scores swing wildly in the first few games and quickly approach true level, and then K shrinks and the score settles.
Counterexample: an expert on a fresh account: a rating is an estimate from “past games.” A new account has no past, its score still says “beginner” though the skill is expert, and so it keeps winning in newcomer rooms. Elo is not wrong here, it simply has no data.
5

How it was inventedThere is no universal ruler: how ratings were invented

Is an 1800 at club A the same as an 1800 at club B?

Chess tournament organizers were once troubled by a plain question: how do you know how strong someone really is? Each club had its own ranking, but it was only the order inside the club and couldn’t be converted elsewhere. To put players from different cities into one tournament, you needed a ruler everyone accepted.

Around 1950 the United States Chess Federation (USCF) used a rating method designed by Kenneth Harkness, which for the first time made ratings something used nationwide. But Arpad Elo, later asked to examine it, thought its results were unreliable in some cases.

Elo was a Hungarian-born American physics professor, and also a chess player. He changed the angle: the point of a rating is not ranking but prediction. Compare two players’ ratings and you should be able to compute each one’s chance of winning; whether the real result is higher or lower than predicted, and by how much, is the amount to correct by. The bigger the surprise, the bigger the change. If you have read the story of dopamine and “prediction error,” this will look familiar: learning runs on the gap between prediction and result.

Around 1950 The problem: each place computed its own, and scores couldn’t be compared. The USCF adopted Harkness’s rating and for the first time gave players numbers across the country. But Elo later judged that in some cases its results were unreliable.
1960 The USCF adopted Elo’s method at its annual meeting in St. Louis. The key insight: the rating difference should convert into a win probability, and the gap between actual result and prediction should drive the adjustment.
1970 The International Chess Federation (FIDE) adopted the Elo rating. One ruler, used everywhere: players from different countries could be compared in one table for the first time.
2006 In online games a match is often two teams of several players, with only one team result but a score to give each person. Herbrich, Minka and Graepel of Microsoft Research published TrueSkill, extending Elo’s idea to multiplayer teams and explicitly recording “how uncertain the system is about a person’s skill.” They evaluated it on Halo 2 test data and used it on Xbox Live.
Sources: for when Elo’s rating was adopted and the background of the Harkness rating, see the World Chess Hall of Fame’s introduction to Elo and Wikipedia’s “Elo rating system”; TrueSkill: Herbrich, Minka & Graepel, “TrueSkill: A Bayesian Skill Rating System”, NIPS 2006.

Why it worksWhy are strangers willing to cooperate? Reciprocity, reputation and punishment

Back to that healer at the start. If everyone counted only their own gains and losses, “not giving” would always pay better, everyone would withhold, and the team would collapse. But in reality, strangers cooperate at a rate far higher than this predicts.

The first clean study of this contradiction came in 1950. At the RAND Corporation, Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher designed a two-player game and had colleagues play a hundred rounds to see whether people would betray each other. Later Albert Tucker told it as a story of two prisoners, which is today’s prisoner’s dilemma.

For cooperation to hold, something has to realign “good for the individual” and “good for everyone.” Researchers found several things.

Reciprocity, reputation, conditional cooperation and punishment

  • Reciprocity: if you will meet again, today’s kindness is repaid tomorrow. “Tit for tat” in the repeated prisoner’s dilemma is this logic.
  • Indirect reciprocity and reputation: even if I never meet you again, others are watching whether I am reliable. Public ratings, achievements and trust scores let cooperation continue among strangers.
  • Conditional cooperators: many people look at how much others put in and then decide how much to put in themselves, which rests on social comparison.
  • Punishment: people willing to pay a cost to punish free-riders make “freeloading” unprofitable. But punishment has costs and side effects.
Sources: Flood & Dresher’s 1950 RAND experiment is in Flood (1952), and Tucker’s prison story is in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry “Prisoner’s Dilemma” (the name “prisoner’s dilemma” first appeared in print in Luce & Raiffa, 1957, and the attribution is disputed); Trivers, “reciprocal altruism” (1971); Nowak & Sigmund, “image scoring” and indirect reciprocity (Nature, 1998); Fischbacher, Gächter & Fehr, “Are people conditionally cooperative?” (Economics Letters, 2001), where about half of people are conditional cooperators and about a third free-ride; Fehr & Gächter on punishment in public-goods games (2000, 2002); social comparison: Festinger (1954).

Words alone won’t do, so here is a small experiment to check it. The rules: groups of 4, each person has 10 tokens each round and decides how much to put into a fund; the tokens in the fund are doubled and shared equally among the 4. Your payoff in one round is 10 − your contribution + 0.5 × (the total of the 4 contributions).

Work it out: if you put in 1 more, you get back only 0.5 yourself, a loss of 0.5; but each teammate gains 0.5, so the team gains 2 more in all. The individually best choice is to put in nothing and the collectively best is to put in everything, which is the public-goods version of the social dilemma.

A public-goods mini game (illustrative)

You and 3 virtual players, 10 rounds in all. The virtual players are all “conditional cooperators”: next round’s contribution ≈ a reference value ± a random fluctuation of 1. The reference value: when anonymous, only the fund total is visible, and the reference is the average contribution of all 4 last round; when reputation is visible, each person’s contribution is visible, the reference is the other 3’s average last round, and because they are seen themselves, each puts in about 1 more (this is an assumption we set, and the size in real experiments varies with the situation). Punishment: each virtual player who put in at least 3 more than you pays 1 to make you lose 3 (this requires “reputation visible” first, or you can’t tell who). These are simplified illustrative rules, not real experimental data.

The conclusion of the experiments is not “the heavier the punishment the better.” In a 2008 paper in Science, Herrmann, Thöni and Gächter repeated the public-goods experiment in 16 groups of participants around the world, and found that in some societies it was the cooperators who put in a lot who got punished (antisocial punishment), strongly enough to cancel the cooperation-promoting effect of punishment. This relates to the local norms of civic cooperation and the strength of the rule of law. So “punishment promotes cooperation” is not a conclusion that holds everywhere.

Why it worksWhy do division of labor and trade make both sides better off? Comparative advantage

Cooperation depends not only on goodwill but on arithmetic. The strongest player on your team deals more damage than anyone and also gathers faster than anyone. Should he do everything himself?

Intuitively yes, but the result is the other way round. Time is limited, and doing A means not doing B. As long as the other person is “relatively not losing out” on some task, letting them do it increases the team’s total output.

This is what Ricardo worked out in the early 19th century: what matters is not who is stronger, but who gives up less.

Comparative advantage

The amount of one thing you give up to get a unit of another is called the opportunity cost. The side with the lower opportunity cost has a comparative advantage in that thing. Each does the thing with the lower opportunity cost and they swap at a price between the two opportunity costs; total output grows, and both sides get a share of the benefit.

You see it everywhere in games: role division in a party, gathering and crafting on an exchange, “who should do what” in co-op games. Trade turns the situation non-zero-sum; how to divide the extra cake is still a game.

Sources: Adam Smith on the division of labor (The Wealth of Nations, 1776); David Ricardo used the example of wine and cloth to set out comparative advantage in On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation (1817).

Why it worksWhy do communities so easily become “winner-take-all”?

When new players log in, they always crowd toward the servers and guilds with many people, because more people means easier matching and more life, and nobody wants to go somewhere empty.

But if every newcomer chooses this way, the crowded ones get more crowded and the sparse ones sparser. This is the social version of the earlier snowball: the probability of being chosen is proportional to existing size (or even stronger), called preferential attachment.

Remember the power law in the economy chapter? A few giants take most of it: the same shape, from another mechanism.

Network effects and “the rich get richer”

As long as “more people means more value” and newcomers follow the crowd, the system produces winner-take-all. Design remedies include splitting or merging servers, cross-server matching, giving small communities distinct features, or letting newcomers into a “newcomer pool” first, rather than simply choosing the largest.

Sources: Barabási and Albert (1999, Science) used a “preferential attachment” model to explain power laws in networks; the sociologist Merton (1968) called the way strong players in science accumulate advantage “the Matthew effect.” The simulation below is a highly simplified illustration.

Another classic scenario is the stag hunt: two people must work together to hunt a stag (a high payoff), while each going off to catch a rabbit is safe but pays little. Its problem is not “the temptation to betray” but “do I dare trust you?” Designs like this are solved by communication, commitment and visible signals.

Key points

  • First look at the relationship between players: cooperation, competition or mixed; zero-sum (fixed total) leaves no room for cooperation, while non-zero-sum has both cooperation and betrayal.
  • Social dilemma: individual best ≠ group best. Co-op games must guard against a “one-man show,” and competitive games against newcomers getting crushed.
  • Positive feedback widens the gap and negative feedback narrows it; design is tuning the knob between “reward the strong” and “keep the suspense,” and a catch-up mechanic that is too strong leaves doing well without reward.
  • Elo treats a rating as a prediction: new rating = old rating + K×(actual − expected); an upset earns a lot; new accounts and teams need correction (which is where TrueSkill comes from).
  • Strangers cooperate through reciprocity, reputation and conditional cooperation; punishment helps but has a cost; making “who did what” visible is an important lever in multiplayer design.
  • Comparative advantage: each does what has the lower opportunity cost and then they trade, and total output grows; the price must fall between the two opportunity costs.
  • “More people is better” makes communities winner-take-all (preferential attachment), which calls for splitting traffic and a newcomer pool.
The core of multiplayer games is relationship design: make cooperation pay better than betrayal, make division of labor mutually beneficial, and keep “more people attract more people” and “the strong get stronger” from squeezing the experience into one-sidedness.

Common misconceptions

  • “People are selfish, so co-op games must fall apart”: in experiments many people are conditional cooperators; the problem is often that the mechanism makes free-riding invisible and cost-free.
  • “The heavier the punishment the better”: heavy punishment provokes retaliation, makes players afraid to try, and easily hurts the innocent; before punishing, first make contributions visible and give positive reputation.
  • “I’m stronger than the other, so it’s better if I do everything”: this ignores opportunity cost. Comparative advantage is about being “relatively” good, not “absolutely” good.
  • “Elo is a player’s skill”: it is only an estimate of win probability under a particular match structure, and needs correction for multiplayer teams, different roles and new accounts.

Reflection and debate

  • How universal are the conclusions of public-goods experiments? The experiments mostly come from particular groups and money settings; levels of cooperation and the effect of punishment differ greatly across cultures (see the 2008 study above).
  • The other side of reputation systems: public ratings can bring one-upmanship, pile-ons and score-farming. This is a common worry, and a designer has to weigh it.
  • Is comparative advantage a cure-all? It assumes free trade, no transaction costs, and no loss of skill growth from specializing. In a game, “you’ll only ever be the healer” may feel dull to a player. Some argue that game design should give every role a “fun” part, not just the “optimal” one.
  • The mini games here are all illustrative: the virtual players’ rules are ones we set, the results depend on those settings, and they cannot be taken as predictions of real player behavior.
