Chapter 18

Multiplayer Design

Once there are other people, a game gains something new: relationships.

Learning goals

  • Tell whether the relationship between players in a multiplayer game is cooperative, competitive or mixed, say whether it is zero-sum or non-zero-sum, and why a bad experience like “playing a co-op game alone” appears.
  • Explain why Elo rating had to be invented, how it turns the gap between “expected” and “result” into points, and how positive and negative feedback can adjust the suspense of a match.
  • Explain why strangers are willing to cooperate (reciprocity, reputation), why a division of labor benefits both sides, and why communities so easily become winner-take-all.
about 45 min
1

What it isStart with the relationship: cooperation, competition, or both

You join a four-person dungeon with a team of strangers, nobody you know. Halfway through the boss fight, your health is down to a sliver and the team’s healer throws you a big heal while their own mana hits bottom. Why would they help? He will never meet you again, and helping gets him nothing.

To answer that, you first have to be clear what the relationship between the players actually is. “Four people playing together” can be quite different things, and each relationship sets the designer a completely different problem.

🤝

Cooperation

Everyone works together against the system (levels, bosses). The hard parts are division of labor and communication, and the worst case is the strongest player “finishing the fight for everyone else.”

⚔️

Competition

The players are each other’s opponents and compete on skill and strategy. The worst risk is newcomers getting crushed, so the losers leave quickly.

🎭

Mixed

Team against team, or “cooperation with betrayal mixed in.” It relies on trust and game theory, and is the likeliest to produce stories.

There is one more independent axis: whether the players are symmetric. In chess both sides have exactly the same rules and resources, which is fair and easy to grasp, and winning or losing comes down to skill. A one-versus-many hunting game is asymmetric: different roles, different ways to play, a richer experience, but hard to balance, and it takes repeated tests with real people until every role’s win rate is acceptable.

2

What it isZero-sum, non-zero-sum, and “everyone is clever and everyone loses”

Zero-sum

What one side gains is what the other loses, and the total is fixed. Chess and poker are like this: if I win, you lose, and the players’ interests are fully opposed.

Non-zero-sum

The total can change, so both can win, or both can lose. Trade and cooperative missions are like this. Only here is there “the possibility of cooperation,” and also “the temptation to betray.”

The trickiest kind of non-zero-sum is the social dilemma: everybody makes the choice that is smartest for themselves, and together all of them lose. You will play one by hand further on.

Concept card

Social Dilemma

Social dilemma

Robyn Dawes · 1980 review “Social dilemmas” (Annual Review of Psychology 31)

Each person picks what pays best for themselves, and together it is bad for everyone. The best choice for the individual is not the best choice for the group.

ExampleA public fund for a team of four: put in 1 more token and you get back only 0.5, but the team gets 1 more in total. Everyone putting in nothing pays best for each person, and the whole team loses.

The examples below show these terms in real games.

Pandemic: a cooperative board game designed by Matt Leacock and published by Z-Man Games in 2008. Everyone fights a disease that spreads on its own, winning together or losing together. This is pure cooperation, non-zero-sum: the opponent is the system, not each other.
Chess: if I capture one of your pieces, you have one fewer. Over a game, the winner gets +1 and the loser −1, and the total is always 0. This is pure competition, zero-sum.
Werewolf / Mafia: created by Dmitry Davidoff in the psychology department of Moscow State University, who remembers the first game as spring 1987. The good players must cooperate to find the bad ones, and the bad ones must lie together. Cooperation and betrayal in the same room make every game a story.
The ancestor of Monopoly: The Landlord’s Game: Elizabeth Magie took out a patent in 1904 that gave the same board two sets of rules. Under the “monopolist” rules, whoever collects the most rent wins and everyone else goes bankrupt. Under the “prosperity” rules, when someone buys land everyone benefits, and the game ends when the poorest player’s wealth has doubled, so everyone wins. One board, one zero-sum rule set and one non-zero-sum. Thirty years later the Parker Brothers bought her patent, and only the first set remains in the game we know.
Counterexample: a co-op game turned into a one-person game: nominally cooperative, but someone knows everything and grabs the commands, and the others can only follow, or only watch. The relationship was designed as “cooperation,” yet the experience is “one player and a few spectators.” What is missing is a decision in every player’s hands that only they can make.
3

What it isFeedback loops: the snowball and catch-up mechanics

In the second half of a Monopoly game everyone knows how it will end: one player owns half a street, another pays rent on every step and still has to keep rolling. “Whoever is ahead finds it easier to stay ahead” is a loop called positive feedback. Multiplayer games always run into it, because players compare themselves with each other, and comparison creates a “leader.”

The remedy is to build a loop the other way: put some limit on the leader and give the trailing player some help, and the gap is pulled back. This loop is negative feedback, usually called a catch-up mechanic in games. Keep both kinds of loop in the design and adjust only the dials: reward the winner, but not so much that a comeback is impossible.

Items in racing games: the Mario Kart series hands out items by rank, and players further behind are more likely to get a powerful item, so the last lap often turns around. This is negative feedback, and the suspense is preserved.
The snowball and Monopoly: give a push at the top of the hill and the snowball grows as it rolls. Monopoly is the same: more property collects more rent, and more rent buys more. This is positive feedback, and a small early edge is magnified into a foregone conclusion.
Counterexample: catch-up tuned too far: the leading car is chased and pelted by items, the player in front feels their skill is being punished, and those behind can catch up without effort. If negative feedback is too strong, “doing well” earns nothing.
4

What it isHow to match strong with weak: the Elo rating

A catch-up mechanic evens things out inside a single match. An earlier step is not to seat very unequal players at the same table: strong players meet strong players and newcomers meet newcomers. That calls for a score everyone can be compared by. The classic method is Elo. Chess uses it, and the ranking systems of many games were inspired by it, though each implements it differently.

Concept card

Elo Rating

Elo rating system

Arpad Elo · adopted by the United States Chess Federation (USCF) in 1960; published The Rating of Chessplayers, Past and Present in 1978

Treat a rating as a prediction: compare two ratings and each side’s chance of winning can be computed. The amount by which the real result beat or fell short of the prediction is used to adjust the scores.

ExampleA 1200-rated underdog beats an 1800-rated favorite and gains about 31 points; if the favorite wins, he gains only about 1 point.

Advanced: the Elo formula

Expected score: EA = 1 / (1 + 10(RB − RA)/400)
New rating: R'A = RA + K × (S − EA) (win S=1, draw 0.5, loss 0). RA and RB are the two players’ ratings.

The method is straightforward: predict A’s score from the rating difference (with a gap of 400, the stronger player is expected to score about 91%), then after the game see how far the real result beat or missed the prediction, and adjust by that gap. K is the sensitivity: with a large K, ratings rise and fall fast, which suits newcomers, and with a small K they stay stable. After each game one side gains what the other loses, so the total is unchanged.

The curve is drawn from the formula (K doesn’t affect the curve, only how much each adjustment is).

An upset: the 1200-rated underdog beats the 1800-rated favorite. His expected score was only about 0.03, K=32, so he gains 32×(1−0.03)≈31 points, and the favorite loses the same 31. If the favorite had won, he would gain only about 1 point. When the person who should win does win, the system learns almost nothing.
A new account’s first games: many rating systems give newcomers a larger K, so scores swing wildly in the first few games and quickly approach true level, and then K shrinks and the score settles.
Counterexample: a master on a smurf account: a rating is an estimate from “past games.” A new account has no past, so its score still sits at “beginner” while its skill is that of an expert, and it keeps winning in beginner rooms. Elo isn’t wrong here; it just has no data to use.
5

How it was inventedThere is no universal ruler: how ratings were invented

Is an 1800 at club A the same as an 1800 at club B?

Chess tournament organizers were once troubled by a plain question: how do you know how strong someone really is? Each club had its own ranking, but it was only the order inside the club and couldn’t be converted elsewhere. To put players from different cities into one tournament, you needed a ruler everyone accepted.

Around 1950 the United States Chess Federation (USCF) used a rating method designed by Kenneth Harkness, which for the first time made ratings something used nationwide. But Arpad Elo, later asked to examine it, thought its results were unreliable in some cases.

Elo was a Hungarian-born American physics professor, and also a chess player. He changed the angle: the point of a rating is prediction, not ranking. Compare two people’s ratings and you should be able to compute each one’s chance of winning. Whether the real result came out higher or lower than predicted, and by how much, is used to correct the rating. The bigger the surprise, the larger the correction. Prediction error, covered in Chapter 2, works the same way: learning comes from the gap between prediction and result.

A 1200-rated small player beats an 1800-rated big one (K=32). How many points should he gain from this game?

Around 1950 The problem was that every region calculated on its own and the scores could not be compared. The USCF adopted the Harkness rating, which for the first time gave players numbers nationwide. But Elo later judged that in some cases its results were unreliable.
1960 The USCF adopted Elo’s method at its annual meeting in St. Louis. The key insight: the rating difference should convert into a win probability, and the gap between actual result and prediction should drive the adjustment.
1970 The International Chess Federation (FIDE) adopted the Elo rating. One ruler, used everywhere: players from different countries could be compared in one table for the first time.
2006 In online games a match is often two teams of several players, with only one team result but a score to give each person. Herbrich, Minka and Graepel of Microsoft Research published TrueSkill, extending Elo’s idea to multiplayer teams and explicitly recording “how uncertain the system is about a person’s skill.” They evaluated it on Halo 2 test data and used it on Xbox Live.
Sources: for when Elo’s rating was adopted and the background of the Harkness rating, see the World Chess Hall of Fame’s introduction to Elo and Wikipedia’s “Elo rating system”; TrueSkill: Herbrich, Minka & Graepel, “TrueSkill: A Bayesian Skill Rating System”, NIPS 2006.

Why it worksWhy are strangers willing to cooperate? Reciprocity, reputation and punishment

Back to the treatment at the start: if everyone counts only their own gains and losses, “not giving” always pays better, nobody gives, and the team collapses. In real life, though, strangers cooperate far more often than that predicts.

This contradiction was first studied cleanly in 1950. Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher at RAND designed a two-player game and had colleagues play it for a hundred rounds. They wanted to see whether people would betray each other. Later Albert Tucker told it as a story of two prisoners, and it became today’s prisoner’s dilemma.

For cooperation to hold, something has to realign “good for the individual” and “good for everyone.” Researchers found several things.

Reciprocity, reputation, conditional cooperation and punishment

  • Reciprocity: if you will meet again, today’s kindness is repaid tomorrow. “Tit for tat” in the repeated prisoner’s dilemma is this logic.
  • Indirect reciprocity and reputation: even if I will never meet this person again, others are watching whether I am reliable. Public ratings, achievements and trust scores let cooperation continue between strangers.
  • Conditional cooperators: many people look at how much others put in and then decide how much to put in themselves, which rests on social comparison.
  • Punishment: people willing to pay a cost to punish free-riders make “freeloading” unprofitable. But punishment has costs and side effects.

Words alone are not enough, so here is a small experiment. The rules: groups of 4; each round every person has 10 tokens and decides how many to put into the fund; the tokens in the fund are doubled and shared equally among the 4. A round’s payoff is 10 − your contribution + 0.5 × (the sum of all 4 contributions).

Work it out: put in 1 more and you get back only 0.5, a loss of 0.5; each of the 3 teammates gains 0.5, +1.5 in all, so the whole team gains 1 more in total. The best choice for the individual is to put in nothing, and the best for the group is for everyone to put in all. This is the social dilemma in its public-goods version.

A public-goods mini game (illustrative)

The player and 3 virtual players play 10 rounds in total. The virtual players are all “conditional cooperators”: next round’s contribution ≈ the reference value ± a random swing of 1.

Reference value: when anonymous, only the fund total is visible, and the reference is the 4-player average contribution of the previous round; when reputation is visible, each person’s contribution can be seen, the reference is the other 3 players’ average contribution in the previous round, and since each is also being watched, each puts in about 1 more (this is an assumption built into the setup; the size in real experiments varies with context).

Punishment: any virtual player who put in at least 3 more than the player pays 1 point to make the player lose 3 (this needs “reputation visible” first, otherwise nobody can tell who did it). This is a simplified illustrative rule, not real experimental data.

Harsher punishment is not always better either. In their 2008 paper in Science, Herrmann, Thöni and Gächter repeated the public-goods experiment in 16 groups of participants around the world.

They found that in some societies the ones punished were actually the generous cooperators (antisocial punishment), strong enough to cancel the cooperation that punishment promotes. It is tied to the local norms of civic cooperation and the strength of the rule of law.

Why it worksWhy do division of labor and trade make both sides better off? Comparative advantage

Cooperation depends not only on goodwill but also on doing the sums. The strongest player on the team is better than anyone at dealing damage and faster than anyone at gathering. Shouldn’t he just do everything himself?

Intuition says yes, but the result is the other way around. Time is limited, and doing A means he cannot do B. As long as the other person is “relatively less badly off” doing a certain task, letting them do it raises the whole team’s total output.

This is something David Ricardo worked out in the early 19th century: who gives up less matters more than who is stronger.

Comparative advantage

The amount of one thing given up to get one unit of another is the opportunity cost. The side with the lower opportunity cost has the comparative advantage in that task. If each does the task with the lower opportunity cost and they trade at a price between the two opportunity costs, total output rises and both sides share the gain.

You see it everywhere in games: role division on a team, gathering and crafting on an exchange, “who should do what” in a co-op game. Trade turns the situation into non-zero-sum. How the extra cake gets divided is still a game of its own.

Sources: Adam Smith · 1776 · The Wealth of Nations · on the division of labor. David Ricardo · 1817 · On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation · explained comparative advantage with the example of wine and cloth.

Comparative advantage

The side with the lower opportunity cost has the comparative advantage in that task. If each does the task with the lower opportunity cost and they then trade, total output rises.

Why it worksWhy do communities so easily become “winner-take-all”?

When new players log in, they always crowd toward the servers and guilds with many people, because more people means easier matching and more life, and nobody wants to go somewhere empty.

But if every newcomer chooses this way, the crowded places get more crowded and the empty ones emptier. This is the social version of the snowball above: the chance of being chosen is proportional to existing size (or even stronger), and the mechanism is preferential attachment.

Chapter 15 covered power laws: a few big ones take most of it. This is the same shape, coming from a different mechanism.

Network effects and “the rich get richer”

As long as “the more people, the more value” holds, and newcomers follow where the crowd is, the system produces winner-take-all. Design remedies include splitting or merging servers, cross-server matching, giving small communities distinctive features, or letting newcomers into a “rookie pool” first instead of simply choosing the biggest.

Sources: Barabási and Albert · 1999 · used the “preferential attachment” model to explain power laws in networks (Science). Merton · 1968 · called the accumulation of advantage by the strong in science the “Matthew effect.” The simulation below is a highly simplified illustration.

Another classic situation is the stag hunt: two people must work together to bring down a stag (high payoff), while each chasing a rabbit alone is safe but pays little. Key points

  • First look at the relationship between players: cooperation, competition or mixed; zero-sum (fixed total) leaves no room for cooperation, while non-zero-sum has both cooperation and betrayal.
  • Social dilemma: individual best ≠ group best. Co-op games must guard against a “one-man show,” and competitive games against newcomers getting crushed.
  • Positive feedback widens the gap and negative feedback narrows it; design is tuning the knob between “reward the strong” and “keep the suspense,” and a catch-up mechanic that is too strong leaves doing well without reward.
  • Elo treats a rating as a prediction: new rating = old rating + K×(actual − expected). Upsets add more. New accounts and teams need adjustment (which is what TrueSkill came from).
  • Strangers cooperate through reciprocity, reputation and conditional cooperation; punishment works but has a cost. Making “who did what” visible is an important lever in multiplayer design.
  • Comparative advantage: each does what has the lower opportunity cost and then they trade, and total output grows; the price must fall between the two opportunity costs.
  • “More people is better” makes communities winner-take-all (preferential attachment), which calls for splitting traffic and a newcomer pool.
The core of multiplayer games is relationship design: make cooperation pay better than betrayal, make division of labor mutually beneficial, and keep “more people attract more people” and “the strong get stronger” from squeezing the experience into one-sidedness.

Common misconceptions

  • “Human nature is selfish, so cooperative games must collapse”: in experiments many people are conditional cooperators. The problem is that the mechanism makes free riding invisible and free of cost.
  • “The harsher the punishment the better”: heavy punishment invites retaliation, makes players afraid to try things, and easily hits the innocent. Rather than punishing, first make contributions visible and give positive reputation.
  • “I’m stronger than the other, so it’s better if I do everything”: this ignores opportunity cost. Comparative advantage is about being “relatively” good, not “absolutely” good.
  • “Elo is a player’s skill”: it is only an estimate of win probability under a particular match structure, and needs correction for multiplayer teams, different roles and new accounts.

Reflection and debate

  • How universal are the conclusions of public-goods experiments? The experiments mostly come from particular groups and money settings; levels of cooperation and the effect of punishment differ greatly across cultures (see the 2008 study above).
  • The other side of reputation systems: public ratings can bring one-upmanship, pile-ons and score-farming. This is a common worry, and a designer has to weigh it.
  • Is comparative advantage a cure-all? It assumes free trade, no transaction costs, and that division of labor does not cost anyone skill growth. In games, “only letting the player be the healer” may feel boring. Some argue that game design should give every role a part that is “fun,” not just “optimal.”
  • The mini-games here are all illustrative: the virtual players’ rules are set by assumption, the results depend on those assumptions, and they can’t be taken as predictions of real players’ behavior.
