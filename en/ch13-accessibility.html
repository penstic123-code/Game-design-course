Chapter 13

Accessibility

Not “adding an option for a few people,” but letting more people into your game.

Learning goals

  • Tell permanent, temporary and situational barriers apart, and explain why “accessibility” isn’t only about a few people.
  • Explain the curb-cut effect and multi-channel redundancy, and check an interface with WCAG’s contrast and flash thresholds.
  • Hunt down by hand the information in a game interface that is conveyed “by color, sound or long presses alone,” and give a fix.
about 40 min
1

What it isA barrier is not “a kind of person” but “a kind of mismatch”

First a little experiment: play a game that needs two hands with just one, say with a coffee in your left and the right hand on the controls. In a few minutes you’ll find that many games assume you have “two hands, fully nimble fingers, good eyesight, good hearing and quick reactions.”

Accessibility (often abbreviated a11y) asks: who has your design shut the door on? A “barrier” here is not a label on a person but a mismatch between the person and the design. The same person at different moments meets different mismatches.

This view has a name, the social model of disability: the British disabled people’s organization UPIAS argued it in its 1976 pamphlet Fundamental Principles of Disability, and Mike Oliver’s 1983 book Social Work with Disabled People brought it to more professionals. It moves the question from “what’s wrong with this person” to “who has this design shut out.”

The usual split is into three kinds, and this “permanent, temporary, situational” framework is often attributed to Microsoft’s Inclusive Design toolkit (led by Kat Holmes):

♾️

Permanent

Long-term: blindness or low vision, deafness or hearing loss, having only one hand or arm, dyslexia.

⏳

Temporary

It will get better: an arm in a cast, dilated pupils after an eye exam, an ear infection, scattered attention after a sleepless night.

📍

Situational

Caused by the environment: can’t see the screen in sunlight, can’t play sound on the subway, holding a child so only one hand is free, being interrupted all the time.

Combined with “which ability is limited,” barriers are often sorted into four groups: vision, hearing, motor (including fine control and endurance), and cognition (including reading, memory, attention and processing speed). The figure below puts the two dimensions together:

Read down a column: the fixes needed are often the same. Captions help deaf players and also players muted on the subway; a button layout that works one-handed helps someone with one hand, someone with a broken arm, and someone holding a child.

Permanent: a player was born with only one hand. For him, “hold the direction key, jump, dash and attack at once” is not difficulty but a physical wall: however much he wants to play, the number of fingers is simply there.
Temporary: your right hand is in a cast for three weeks. You still want to play, only to find that the game allows nothing but WASD plus a mouse. Three weeks later you’re better, but that wall has been there for others all along.
Situational: you’re playing in a bedroom late at night, the child has just fallen asleep, and you don’t dare turn the sound on. If the key cues depend entirely on sound, you have become “temporarily deaf.” You have no impairment at all; the environment made one for you.
Counterexample (a near-miss): “We have a colorblind mode,” but all the mode does is swap red and green for more vivid red and green. It looks done, yet it hasn’t solved the root problem of “telling things apart by color alone,” as you can test yourself in the barrier simulator below.

What it isCommon practice in games, and “multi-channel redundancy”

Games are tougher than ordinary software: they demand perception, judgment, operation and time pressure all at once. So the game world commonly uses a set of options to “loosen” each of these steps. Sorted into the four groups:

👁 Vision

  • Colorblind mode, or better: shapes, patterns and text that also tell things apart
  • Text scaling, bold, adjustable caption size and background
  • High-contrast mode, adjustable HUD opacity and size
  • Screen reading / voice navigation, audio cues (for blind and low-vision players)

👂 Hearing

  • Captions, with speaker labels and descriptions of key sounds (such as “[footsteps · back left]”)
  • Separate volume for each channel (dialogue / music / effects)
  • Visual cues for sound (direction indicators, hit edges)
  • Controller vibration as a haptic substitute

✋ Motor

  • Remappable buttons, with support for one-handed layouts
  • Hold changed to toggle, button mashing changed to hold
  • Auto-aim, aim assist, simplified quick-time events
  • Pause and save anytime, with timing relaxed or turned off

🧠 Cognitive

  • Difficulty and assist options adjustable at any time (without penalizing the choice)
  • Quest / goal hints, tutorials you can revisit
  • Reduced motion, flashing and screen shake; camera sway that can be switched off
  • Clear, consistent icons and terms

Behind these options is a shared principle: multi-channel redundancy: important information shouldn’t travel by only one road.

Note that “redundancy” is not “repeating yourself.” Its cost is usually small (drawing one more arrow), but in return, when any one channel fails because of a barrier, the environment or the device, the game can still be played.

In games: an enemy approaches from behind-left; an arrow appears at the left edge of the screen, left-channel footsteps play in the headphones, and the left side of the controller vibrates lightly. Those who can’t hear look at the arrow, those who can’t see well listen, and those for whom neither is convenient still have the vibration.
In daily life: a pedestrian crossing signal makes a beeping sound as the green walking figure lights up. People who can’t see cross by sound, people who can’t hear look at the light, and someone wearing headphones doesn’t miss it either.
Counterexample: low health is flagged only by “red flashing at the screen edge.” Players with different color vision may not tell it apart, and those who turned on “reduce motion” never see the flashing; once this single channel breaks, the warning disappears entirely.

How it was inventedHow these things came to be “invented”

Story one: a curb that blocked a whole group of people

Imagine you use a wheelchair and want to go from home to the corner shop. The road is flat until the intersection, where between sidewalk and road there is a curb a dozen centimeters high. For people on foot it barely exists; for a wheelchair user it is a wall. You can only go a long way around, or ask someone to lift you.

What happened in history

After WWII In Kalamazoo, Michigan, a veteran persuaded the city government to cut ramps into the sidewalks at four downtown corners. This is an early attempt often mentioned, but it did not spread into a nationwide practice.
1960s Ed Roberts, a polio survivor who lived by means of an iron lung. In 1962 the University of California, Berkeley at first refused him, and later he enrolled and lived in a room in the campus hospital. With other disabled students he formed the “Rolling Quads” and began to fight for disabled people’s civil rights.
1971 On September 28, the Berkeley City Council unanimously passes a policy to build curb ramps at once at 15 designated corners. This is seen as one of the earlier curb-ramp projects at scale.
1977–1990 Disabled people protest and occupy federal buildings, demanding enforcement of protections; in 1990 the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) is signed, requiring accessibility in public places, transportation and employment.
2017 Angela Glover Blackwell publishes “The Curb-Cut Effect” in the Stanford Social Innovation Review, spreading the observation that “design done for those at the margins ends up benefiting everyone.”

The key insight: the barrier is in the environment, not in the person; changing the environment is much cheaper than making everyone “overcome” it themselves, and it benefits more people. This is also the core of “universal design.”

Sources: the 99% Invisible episode “Curb Cuts” (99percentinvisible.org/episode/curb-cuts/, the Kalamazoo and Berkeley timelines); Blackwell (2017), Stanford Social Innovation Review. Sources differ on “where the earliest curb ramp was,” and a conservative wording is used above.

Story two: why captions have to be “switchable”

Deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers watching television have only the picture, without what is heard. The most direct fix was to burn captions into the picture, called open captions. But then every viewer sees them, and commercial broadcasters worried that people who didn’t need them would be annoyed and drive viewers away. So the question became: can they be shown only to those who need them?

1972 The US public broadcaster PBS begins regularly airing The French Chef with open captions.
1979 The National Captioning Institute is founded in the US, one of its goals being to win the cooperation of the commercial networks.
1980-03-16 The first regularly scheduled programs with closed captions air in the US: the caption signal is hidden inside the TV signal and shown only with a decoder: those who want it turn it on, those who don’t turn it off.
The 1990 Television Decoder Circuitry Act requires analog television sets of 13 inches and larger to have built-in decoding, effective July 1, 1993. The decoder changed from “a device you buy separately” to “in every television.”

Later captions came to be used heavily by people who aren’t hard of hearing: in noisy bars, quiet libraries, when learning a foreign language, late at night when you don’t want to wake the family. Captions in games are the same story: “switchable” lets those who need them use them without disturbing everyone else.

Sources: Wikipedia’s entry “Closed captioning,” which organizes the dates above; the entry words the date of passage of the act and the year in its name slightly differently, so only its requirements (13 inches and larger, July 1, 1993) are taken here.

Story three: how the games world began to take it seriously

2004 Mark Barlet founds AbleGamers, because a good friend of his, owing to multiple sclerosis, gradually couldn’t operate a mouse but still wanted to play games. The nonprofit later committed itself to assessing player needs and providing equipment and consulting.
2012 The Game Accessibility Guidelines are published (reported by Game Informer in September 2012). Written jointly by a group of studios, experts and scholars, they sort recommendations into basic, intermediate and advanced tiers by reach, impact and cost to implement, so developers know where to start.
2018 Microsoft announces the Xbox Adaptive Controller in May, and it goes on sale on September 4. The idea: rather than choosing the way of controlling for the player, provide large buttons and many ports, so players can plug in their own peripherals and build a “controller” that suits them.
2020 The Last of Us Part II ships with over 60 accessibility settings, including features for blind and low-vision players; the development team brought in the blind consultant Brandon Cole very early. It became an often-cited example in the industry’s discussion of “how deep even a big title can go.”

These threads all point to one thing: accessibility is not an after-the-fact patch; the earlier you ask “who has this design shut out,” the lower the cost. Microsoft also offers its own Xbox Accessibility Guidelines (learn.microsoft.com/gaming/accessibility), organizing similar checks into a checklist to compare against.

Sources: AbleGamers’ “Mark Barlet” page (ablegamers.org/mark-barlet/); Game Informer, 2012-09-04, “Game Accessibility Guidelines Published,” and the site notes at gameaccessibilityguidelines.com; Microsoft Xbox News, 2018-09-04; the article on TLOU2 accessibility features from Naughty Dog / PlayStation Blog, 2020-06. No judgment is made here about “who is best”; these are only recorded as milestones.

Why it worksUniversal design, contrast, flashing and color vision

① Design made for a few often benefits everyone

This is the curb-cut effect. The engineering thinking behind it is called universal design:

The counterparts in games: captions (for muted settings), remappable buttons (for left-handers, different keyboard layouts, one-handed play), text scaling (small phone screens, handhelds, big TVs from a distance), pause and autosave (for anyone interrupted).

Curb ramps: a gentle slope made for wheelchairs is just as smooth for people pushing strollers and dragging suitcases, and even delivery hand trucks benefit.
Captions: captions made for the hearing impaired became a feature used every day in noisy bars, quiet libraries and by people learning languages.
A near-miss: the ramp smoothed away the curb’s “edge,” and a person with a white cane lost a cue for judging where the roadside is. Later, tactile paving with little dots was added at the ramp mouth to make up for it. Design has to be tested with the affected people, and not assumed.

② Contrast: turning “can you see it clearly” into a number

The W3C’s Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) say that for normal text, level AA requires a contrast ratio with its background of at least 4.5:1; for large text (at least 18 pt, or 14 pt bold, about 24 px / 18.5 px), at least 3:1. The threshold is a floor: a computed 4.499 doesn’t pass.

Try it: pick two colors and see the ratio and whether it passes. Try the “gray text on a dark background” and “yellow text on white” that are common in games.

Whose view, and what view

The contrast formula comes from W3C’s WCAG (the 2.1 rules are used here): first turn each sRGB channel into a linear value, then compute relative luminance as 0.2126R + 0.7152G + 0.0722B, and contrast = (lighter + 0.05) ÷ (darker + 0.05). This figure is a starting point and can’t replace testing with real people: font weight, anti-aliasing, screen brightness and the user’s eyesight all affect actual legibility.

Source: W3C, “Understanding SC 1.4.3: Contrast (Minimum),” www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html. For non-text elements in game interfaces (such as health-bar borders), the 3:1 practice is often followed.

③ Flashing: don’t let the picture hurt anyone

A small number of people are sensitive to flickering pictures, which can trigger photosensitive epilepsy. The floor set by WCAG 2.3.1 (level A) is: no more than 3 general flashes, and no more than 3 red flashes, in any one second; or the flashing area is small enough (the standard defines this by an area such as “0.006 steradians within a 10-degree field of view”). A “general flash” here means a pair of opposing changes in relative luminance, with an amplitude of at least 10% of the maximum luminance, where the darker side is below 0.80.

What this means for game designers: explosion white-outs, strobe effects and rapid light-dark switching need care, and a “reduce flashing / motion” option should be provided.

Source: W3C, “Understanding SC 2.3.1: Three Flashes or Below Threshold,” www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/three-flashes-or-below-threshold.html. This standard is for the web, so for games it is a reference, not a legal requirement; game platforms and the industry may have their own testing procedures.

④ Color vision differences: not a minority of a minority

Red–green color vision deficiency varies in rate between populations. According to Wikipedia’s summary of the literature, among people of northern European descent, about 8% of men and about 0.5% of women have red–green deficiency; the most common is deuteranomaly (about 5% of men); blue–yellow (tritan) and total color blindness (achromatopsia) are both very rare (blue–yellow below 0.01%).

In other words: out of every 12 male players, on average about 1 has “trouble telling red from green.” So designs like red and blue teams or red and green health are not a corner case.

Source: Wikipedia, “Color blindness” (the epidemiology section, citing multiple population studies); the proportions vary with population and measurement method, so this is only an order-of-magnitude guide.

⑤ Cognitive load: accessibility and “good design” are the same thing

Remember why a tutorial should teach just one thing at a time? Because human working memory is like a very small table, with no room for more than a few things at once. George Miller (1956) summed it up as “7±2,” and Nelson Cowan (2001) later reworked it, arguing that the pure capacity is more like about 4 “chunks,” though the exact figure is still disputed.

John Sweller (1988) applied the idea to learning and proposed cognitive load theory: when the task is too messy and information too scattered, the table fills up and anything more falls straight off. Games add time pressure on top: map, health bar, enemies, hints all have to be watched, and buttons pressed at the same time.

For people with dyslexia, with attention that is hard to hold, or just off a night shift, the table is even smaller. Ways to lower the load, such as clear icons, teaching one thing at a time, hints you can revisit and not forcing quick reactions, help players with cognitive barriers, and also tired, distracted and first-time players. This is the cognitive version of the curb-cut effect.

Sources: Miller (1956), “The magical number seven, plus or minus two,” Psychological Review 63; Cowan (2001), “The magical number 4 in short-term memory,” Behavioral and Brain Sciences 24; Sweller (1988), “Cognitive load during problem solving,” Cognitive Science 12.

⑥ Fairness: designing from behind the “veil of ignorance”

A thought experiment: you have to design a game, but must decide without knowing which player you will turn out to be. You might be a nimble-handed veteran, or have only one hand, or see color differently from others. In such a position, you would probably quite naturally make the options generous.

The philosopher John Rawls used exactly this “veil of ignorance” in A Theory of Justice (1971): fair rules are those set when you don’t know your own position. He was discussing social institutions, so moving it to design is only an analogy, but it answers “why design for a few”: because those who design can’t guarantee they will never be among that few.

The corresponding term in the design world is universal design, which the architect Ron Mace is said to have put forward around 1985, arguing for making things usable by as many people as possible from the start rather than patching afterward.

Sources: John Rawls, A Theory of Justice (1971); for Ron Mace and the origins of universal design see the introduction from NC State’s Center for Universal Design (sources differ slightly on the year, and a conservative wording is used here).

    🧰

    A starter checklist

    Start from the “basic” tier of the Game Accessibility Guidelines: broad in reach and cheap to implement, such as adjustable captions, not relying on color alone, and key remapping.

    🤝

    Do it with users

    Users with barriers are experts, not “objects to be served.” Inviting them early is much cheaper than changing things after launch.

    An example from daily life: the braille keys in an elevator are necessary for blind people, and also a clue for someone fumbling in the dark; a voice assistant is an entry point for people with motor barriers, and also lets someone driving look at the screen one time less.
    Counterexample: a game puts all its accessibility options in settings “unlocked only after hard mode,” or locks achievements once you turn on an assist mode. An option that exists but punishes you for using it is as good as none.

    Common misconceptions

    • “Accessibility = easy mode.” Difficulty is just one of many options; what many people need is another way to perceive / operate, with the challenge unchanged (such as remappable buttons and captions).
    • “Only a very few players need it.” Besides permanent barriers there are temporary and situational ones; red–green color vision deficiency isn’t rare among men either.
    • “Adding a colorblind mode is enough.” If it only swaps one set of colors, it still relies on color; steadier is to add shapes, patterns and text beyond color.
    • “Having the options means it’s done.” Options have to be findable (settable on first launch), previewable, and free of penalty for users.
    • “Accessibility ruins the design.” Good practice often makes the design clearer; where there is a real conflict, it needs a specific trade-off rather than a blanket rule.

    Reflection and debate

    Limits. The simulator and the contrast calculation here are both simplified: people’s degrees of barrier, assistive technology and habits vary enormously, and “meets the standard” doesn’t mean “works well.” WCAG was also made for web design, and carrying it into games takes judgment.

    Debate: should difficulty also be an accessibility option? One view holds that lowering difficulty, skipping combat and so on are themselves important accessible means and shouldn’t be stigmatized; another holds that the core challenge of some games is exactly what they want to express, and casually bypassing it changes the work’s intent. There is no standard answer here; a common compromise is: offer fine-grained assists (slowed time, aim assist, skippable sections) and put the choice in the player’s hands, instead of a “one-size-fits-all easy mode.”

    Debate: cost and priority. A small team has limited resources, so what should come first? The Game Accessibility Guidelines’ ranking by “reach, impact and cost” offers one line of thought, though it varies with the type of game.

    Something that may change your view. If you used to think “accessibility is looking after a few people,” this chapter would like to swap in another view: it is more like raising a design’s tolerance for error, so that the game still holds up in more environments, more bodies and more moods.

    Key points

    • A barrier is a mismatch between a person and a design, of three kinds, permanent, temporary and situational; abilities fall into four groups, vision, hearing, motor and cognitive.
    • Multi-channel redundancy: important information shouldn’t travel by color alone, sound alone or long presses alone.
    • The curb-cut effect: design made for those at the margins often benefits everyone; the barrier is in the environment, not in the person.
    • Quantifiable floors: normal text contrast ≥ 4.5:1, large text ≥ 3:1; flashes no more than 3 in any one second (WCAG).
    • Do accessibility early, do it with users, and verify it with real-person testing.
    A barrier is a mismatch between a person and a design, not a defect in the person: open a few more roads for important information, and the door stays open to more people.
