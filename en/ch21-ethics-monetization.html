Chapter 21

Ethics & Monetization

A game has to make money, but not off its players’ weaknesses.

Learning goals

  • Tell four business models apart, say how each in turn shapes game design, and read what a retention curve is telling you.
  • Explain why free-to-play with in-app purchases appeared, and why variable rewards, supernormal stimuli, loss aversion and defaults make people want to buy more and find it harder to stop.
  • Check a paid design against the list of “transparent, optional, exit-able, protecting vulnerable users,” and tell legitimate marketing from manipulation.
about 45 min
1

What it isWho pays? Four business models

You didn’t really feel like playing anymore, yet because of the “12-day login streak” on the screen you crawled out of bed at midnight and tapped once. That little reluctance has nothing to do with willpower; someone put something in the design. To understand what, we first have to know where the money comes from, because the way money is collected shapes the game itself.

💳 Buy-to-play

The player pays once, so the design must make the player believe before buying and feel satisfied after buying. The experience can be wrapped up completely, with no need to stretch it out on purpose.

📅 Subscription

Paying monthly or yearly, the player must keep feeling it is “worth it.” The design leans toward steady content updates, so people don’t want to cancel.

🆓 Free + in-app purchases

Download for free first, then have some players spend money (cosmetics, convenience, content packs). The user base is large, but the design must serve both paying and non-paying players at once.

📺 Ads

Money comes from showing ads, and the more people watch and the longer, the better. Ads interrupt the experience, so a common approach is to let players watch voluntarily in exchange for a reward.

In practice the models are often mixed, such as “buy-to-play plus paid expansions,” or “free plus in-app purchases plus optional ads.” The five examples below show which direction each model pushes the design.

A buy-to-play indie game: the player watches videos and reads reviews on the store page to decide whether to buy. The designer’s pressure is on “making people believe before buying,” and the game can end beautifully at the credits, with no need to pad it to stretch the playtime.
A monthly-subscription online world: every month the player decides again “is it still worth it,” so the designer must keep updating content and tending the community. The reason to pay is “this world keeps being open.”
Free + in-app purchases: everyone can come in, and a few paying players keep everyone’s servers running. The designer must look after both paying and non-paying players, and non-paying players are part of the atmosphere too.
Watching an ad for a reward, by choice: when a player dies, they can choose to watch a 15-second ad to revive. The ad becomes one of the player’s options, not an interruption forced in.
Counterexample: pay to decide who wins: free + in-app purchases itself is fine; the bad part is that bought stats directly crush those who don’t pay, making them “sparring partners.” Free players become props for paying players’ experience, and what is missing is “fair competition.”

What it isRetention: do players come back?

Whichever model is used, the team has to answer one question: after a player’s first visit, will they come back? The answer is retention, and is usually read at three points:

1️⃣

Day-1 retention

Of those who came on day 1, the share who came back on day 2. It reflects the first impression and the onboarding.

7️⃣

Day-7 retention

It reflects whether a habit has formed: are there enough goals and variety?

3️⃣0️⃣

Day-30 retention

It reflects long-term value: depth of content, social ties, the growth system.

This is a schematic: the curve of most games has the shape “steep fall, then flat,” and the exact numbers vary with the genre and channel.

Onboarding decides day 1: someone who gets through the tutorial smoothly on the first day and gets a first small achievement is more willing to come back tomorrow. Day-1 retention is mostly decided at this step.
Retention in daily life: a gym annual pass: the moment you sign up looks like “conversion achieved,” but real retention is how many times you show up in the next thirty days. Having a card doesn’t mean you go often, and having downloaded a game doesn’t mean you play often.
Counterexample: retention held up by streak-break penalties: to keep a 12-day login streak from resetting, players crawl out of bed at midnight to tap once. The retention number looks good, but the players are not “wanting to play,” they are “afraid to lose.” This kind of retention doesn’t last, and below you will see how it hurts trust.

What it isDark patterns: pushing players toward “what they didn’t want to do”

The term dark patterns was coined in 2010 by the British user-experience designer Harry Brignull It describes designs that use the interface or mechanics to induce people to do what they didn’t want to (spend more money, spend more time, get trapped in a subscription). The point of knowing them is to avoid using them yourself, and to recognize them as a player.

Concept card

Dark Patterns

Dark patterns

Harry Brignull · registered darkpatterns.org and coined the term in July 2010

Designs that use the interface or mechanics to induce people to do what they did not want to do: spend more money, spend more time, or be unable to leave a subscription.

ExampleA bundle page shows a “23:59:59” countdown that resets at midnight every day, so the same bundle returns tomorrow; subscribing takes one step, while unsubscribing takes several rounds of “Are you sure?”.

⏰ FOMO (fear of missing out)

FOMO: limited time, limited stock, “gone if you don’t buy now.” Real scarcity is legitimate information, but a fake countdown is deception.

😨 Loss framing

“Miss a day and it resets to zero,” “your win streak is about to be lost”: describing “not doing it” as “losing,” which exploits the fact that people are more sensitive to losses.

⚓ Sunk cost

“I’ve already put so much into this…” This is the sunk cost effect: continuing to pay so as not to waste earlier payments, even when you are no longer happy.

♾️ No stopping point

There is no natural stopping point, so the player must make an active decision to stop, and is never given a designed “this segment is over.”

Concept card

Sunk Cost Effect

Sunk cost effect

Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer · 1985 paper “The psychology of sunk cost”

To avoid wasting an investment that has already been made and cannot be recovered, people keep investing, even when it is no longer worth it.

Example“I’ve already played 30 hours”: those 30 hours will not come back, so the only question is whether the next 30 hours are worth it.

These tricks share a flaw: once they are treated as “ways to push a number up,” they reinforce themselves. The diagram below is a typical loop.

FOMO: the resetting countdown: the gift-pack page shows “23:59:59,” but it resets at midnight every day and the same pack comes back tomorrow. The urgency is faked, and the player is tricked into deciding quickly.
Easy to subscribe, hard to unsubscribe: subscribing takes one step, while unsubscribing takes you through “Are you sure?” “Really?” “Think again.” The door in and the door out are not the same width. This burns the player’s patience instead of winning their consent.
Sunk cost: “I’ve played 30 hours already”: those 30 hours are gone, and whether to continue should look only at whether the next 30 hours are worth it. But “not playing would be a waste” makes people continue even when they are not happy.
Counterexample: clearly priced cosmetics: three cosmetics each with a price, and the game plays fully without buying. The player sees clearly, can decline, and is not pushed to decide. It is still “selling things,” but it lacks exactly the deception and coercion of dark patterns.

What it isThe floor of design ethics, and what has already happened in reality

🧒

Protecting minors

Spending limits, parental controls, double confirmation of payment, play-time reminders.

🔍

Transparent odds

Random paid rewards must publish the odds and the pity rules.

🧘

No manufactured anxiety

No fake countdowns, shaming copy or punishing check-ins to push players. Let players leave without worry.

Besides, canceling a subscription should be as easy as subscribing, prices and content should be spelled out, and paying should not turn non-paying players into “sparring partners.” This is not just a gentleman’s agreement, and regulators in many places are moving this way, with fast change. Here are a few things that have already happened (laws differ by place; follow current local rules):

  • China: from May 1, 2017, online game operators must publish the probabilities of random-draw items; in August 2021 the National Press and Publication Administration set that online game services for minors are limited to 20:00–21:00 on Fridays, Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays, one hour in all.
  • Belgium: in April 2018 the Gaming Commission ruled that the loot boxes in FIFA 18, Counter-Strike: Global Offensive and Overwatch violated gambling law; Star Wars Battlefront II, after adjustment by EA, was not so ruled.
  • United States: in December 2022 the Federal Trade Commission reached a settlement with Epic Games, of which 245 million dollars goes to refunds. The charges included using dark patterns to induce players (including children) to buy by mistake, such as a button layout that charged on a single accidental tap (order finalized March 2023). In January 2025 the commission proposed a 20 million dollar settlement with Cognosphere, the developer of Genshin Impact: charging that it misled players about gacha odds and made the virtual-currency purchase flow confusing, and requiring that loot boxes not be sold to people under 16 without parental consent. The company said it disagreed with some of the allegations but agreed to settle.
Sources: Harry Brignull registered darkpatterns.org and coined the term in July 2010 (see the author introduction at deceptive.design); China: the Ministry of Culture’s rules on publishing the odds of random-draw items (in force from May 1, 2017), and the National Press and Publication Administration’s notice of August 30, 2021 on further strict management to effectively prevent minors from becoming addicted to online games (reported by Xinhua the same day); the Belgian Gaming Commission’s April 2018 findings (media reports); FTC press releases of December 2022 (Epic Games) and January 17, 2025 (Cognosphere).

How it was inventedHow did games learn to charge “for free”?

Each way of charging was invented to solve the trouble the last one left

Looking back over this history, one clear line runs through it: every new way of charging appeared because the old one had an intolerable flaw. In the arcade, one coin per play, and the reason to pay was perfectly plain. But once games moved home, players had to buy the whole box at once without being able to try it first, and anyone who bought a dud could only accept their bad luck.

So came the first remedy: let players play first. Later worlds grew bigger and servers cost money every month, a one-off purchase could not keep up with the continuing costs, and it changed to monthly subscription. Later still came the phone, where having to pay before downloading a game was too high a barrier, and “free to download, sell inside” became the new way. Each step solved an old problem and brought a new one.

🤔 Guess first, then reveal

You want to sell a new game nobody has heard of, and players can’t try it before buying. How will you charge?

The arcade era One coin, one play, with payment directly tied to fun, clear to all. But it charged per play, which wouldn’t work once moved into the living room: a home game had to be bought whole, and one could not tell before buying whether it was any good.
1993 In December, id Software released the first episode of Doom (nine levels) as free shareware, to be copied freely, with the other two episodes by mail order. The key insight: let players play first, then decide whether to pay. It spread extremely fast, reportedly jamming university servers as soon as it was uploaded.
2004 World of Warcraft launched in November with a monthly subscription (the monthly card then about 14.99 dollars, the boxed game’s suggested retail price 49.99 dollars including one month). An online world has to be open all the time and its costs are continuing, so the charge also became continuing. This brought a new problem: each month the player decides again whether it is worth it, and designers had to keep holding people with content.
2009 In October Apple allowed free apps to use in-app purchases too. Before that only paid apps could sell in-app purchases, and developers had to make a separate free “Lite version.” From then on “free to download, sell inside” became the mainstream approach for mobile games, and brought new pressure. Only some people pay, so a team has to find ways to get more people to part with money.
2012 A kind of draw that required “collecting a whole set to redeem a grand prize” (known as kompu gacha) became popular in Japanese social games: random card draws, with the last card slow to arrive. Complaints rose noticeably, the Consumer Affairs Agency said the practice might be illegal, and six big companies then announced they would drop it. Random paid mechanics were watched by regulators for the first time.
2017—2018 From May 1, 2017, China required the odds of random items to be published; in April 2018 Belgium ruled the loot boxes of some games to violate gambling law. From “is it fun” to “is it fair,” the rules began to be written into law.
Sources: Doom shareware release and upload on December 10, 1993: Doom’s history (DoomWiki, Wikipedia “Doom (1993 video game)”); World of Warcraft price and launch date: Blizzard’s November 4, 2004 press release and reports of the time; Apple’s email to developers on October 15, 2009 (reported by TechCrunch and Macworld); the Japanese kompu gacha affair: industry and media reports of May 2012 (The Next Web and others). For national regulatory details see the sources in the previous section.

Why it worksWhy does “you can’t tell when it will hit” make it harder to stop? Reinforcement schedules

Some games make you want to close them and yet “try one more time.” Psychologists observed something like this long ago in experiment boxes.

B. F. Skinner and his collaborators had animals press a lever for food and then changed the rule for “when to give the reward.” A reward after every N presses is a fixed ratio; a reward on average every N presses, with which one unpredictable, is a variable ratio. Both rules keep the animals pressing, but the latter is usually steadier, and fades more slowly when the rewards are stopped.

Random drops and gacha pulls in games look a lot like this.

Reinforcement schedules and the designer’s responsibility

Dopamine came up in Chapter 2. Schultz, Dayan and Montague summarized in their 1997 paper that the dopamine neurons of primates seem to encode “prediction error”: the response is strongest to outcomes better than expected.

A variable reward makes every pull “uncertain,” and every pull carries some prediction error, a common explanation of why it is hard to stop. The direct evidence comes mainly from animals and controlled experiments, and no conclusion can be drawn directly about any particular player.

Knowing “how to make behavior last longer” means having the power to shape players’ behavior, so responsibility matters. The power itself is neither right nor wrong, but at least three things must be kept apart:

① can ≠ should: using it to make fights more surprising and using it to make people spend and unable to stop are two different things.

② Take double care when tied to real money: random rewards plus payment already have the shape of gambling, and need published odds and spending caps.

③ Mind who it is for: the same mechanism affects adults and minors, and impulsive people, differently.

Sources: Skinner, The Behavior of Organisms (1938); Ferster & Skinner, Schedules of Reinforcement (1957); Schultz, Dayan & Montague, “A Neural Substrate of Prediction and Reward”, Science 275 (1997); for research on slot-machine design see Natasha Dow Schüll, Addiction by Design (2012). Conclusions from animal experiments cannot be equated directly with effects on people; their ideas are borrowed here to discuss design responsibility.

Why it worksMore tempting than nature: the supernormal stimulus

Junk food is sweeter and oilier than fruit and tastes “more intense,” yet it does the body no more good. Human desires evolved in a natural environment, and when they meet an exaggerated artificial stimulus they get pulled further than “what is truly good for you.”

The ethologist Niko Tinbergen and colleagues saw this in seabirds. Herring gull chicks peck at a red spot on the parent’s bill to beg for food, and a red stick with white thin rings drew even more pecking than a realistic model of a bird’s head. Offered dummy eggs, birds often prefer to sit on one larger and more brightly colored than their own.

Tinbergen called an artificial stimulus stronger than the natural range, one that may be more attractive than the real thing, a supernormal stimulus.

The supernormal stimulus

Connecting to games: some people use this analogy to discuss games, short videos and processed food. Do ever-amplified visuals, sound, reward pop-ups and number inflation let “wanting” run ahead of “satisfaction”? This is only an analogy, not a conclusion: it does not equal “games = addiction,” and most people play games with no problem. It only reminds designers: turning up the stimulus is easy, but “more stimulating” does not mean “more satisfying”.

Sources: Tinbergen, The Study of Instinct (1951), The Herring Gull’s World (1953); for popular accounts extending the concept to human behavior see Deirdre Barrett, Supernormal Stimuli (2010), an analogical application that the field has not fully accepted. The “innate releasing mechanism” that Tinbergen and Lorenz used to explain these phenomena is no longer widely accepted today, but the observed phenomena themselves still stand. A related but different theory is Berridge and Robinson’s distinction between “wanting” and “liking” (from the 1990s).

Why it worksFearing loss is stronger than wanting gain: loss aversion, scarcity and default options

Finding 100 yuan is pleasant, losing 100 yuan is painful, and the pain lasts longer. What promotional copy likes to do most is take the same product and put it differently: not “what you will get,” but “what you will lose,” or “only a few left.”

Behind this is a famous finding. Kahneman and Tversky’s prospect theory (1979) points out that people feel gains and losses relative to a reference point. A loss of a given size often matters more than a gain of the same size, and this tendency is called loss aversion.

Another common principle of persuasion is scarcity: the harder to get, the more valuable it looks.

Concept card

Scarcity Principle

Scarcity principle

Robert Cialdini · Influence, 1984

The rarer, harder to get or more time-limited a thing is, the more valuable people find it and the more eagerly they want it.

ExampleA real “3 days only” event is legitimate information; a fake countdown that resets at midnight every day is fabrication.

An illustrative curve: its shape borrows the parameters estimated by Tversky and Kahneman (1992) (exponent about 0.88, loss weight about 2.25; reportedly the estimate came from the choices of 25 graduate students). This multiple varies a lot across studies.

Loss aversion, scarcity and “where is the line”

These principles are not evil in themselves: a real limited-time event and a real rare item are legitimate information. What crosses the line is the following: is the information true (fake countdowns, fake stock), can players give up without penalty (does “don’t buy and it resets” count as coercion), and does it exploit player weakness (low moods, minors).

There is one more quiet but powerful thing: the default option (covered in Chapter 10). In a 2003 paper in Science, Johnson and Goldstein compared the organ-donation systems of European countries: in countries where the default is “not a donor” and you must opt in (such as Germany, about 12%), the registered consent rate is far lower than in countries where the default is “donor” and you must opt out (such as Austria, about 99.98%). This is the rate of registered consent, not the actual donation rate.

Most people won’t change the default, so whoever sets the default is deciding for everyone. Using defaults to steer people toward what is good for them is the “nudge” that Thaler and Sunstein discuss in Nudge (2008). The same technique, used on a pre-ticked “auto-renew,” becomes a dark pattern.

Kahneman and Tversky · 1979 · prospect theory (“Prospect Theory,” Econometrica); Tversky and Kahneman · 1992 · cumulative prospect theory.
Cialdini · 1984 · Influence · scarcity and other principles of persuasion.

Thaler and Sunstein · 2008 · Nudge.
Researchers have also recently questioned how universal loss aversion is: for example Gal and Rucker (2018) argue that existing evidence does not support “losses generally loom larger than gains,” so “a loss is about twice a gain” should not be taken as a law.

This list is a practical self-check put together for this course, not legal text, and does not guarantee compliance; regions have different laws on odds disclosure, minors’ spending, subscription cancellation and so on, so check local rules.

Key points

  • Four common business models: buy-to-play, subscription, free + in-app purchases, ads; how you make money shapes the design in return.
  • Retention is read at day 1, day 7 and day 30: day 1 shows the first impression, day 7 the habit, day 30 the long-term value. Retention held up by streak-break penalties is brittle.
  • Ways of charging evolved step by step, each fixing the last one’s flaw: try-first (shareware), monthly subscription, free plus in-app purchases, until random paid mechanics drew regulators.
  • Dark patterns: fake countdowns, cancellation mazes, sunk cost, loss framing, no stopping point. Know them in order to avoid abusing them.
  • Why they work: variable rewards last longer, supernormal stimuli let “wanting” run ahead of “satisfaction,” loss aversion and scarcity make people fear loss, and default options quietly decide for people. Effective does not mean it should be used: look at whether it is true, whether one can give it up, and whether it targets the vulnerable.
  • The ethical floor: protect minors, transparent odds, no manufactured anxiety; self-check: if the player were fully informed, would they still find it fair?
Psychology tells us how to “make people want more”; ethics requires doing so only when it is true, optional, and players can leave without worry.

Common misconceptions

  • “Supernormal stimulus = games will make people addicted.” This is only an analogy: most players have no problem playing games; what it reminds us of is that “stronger stimulus is not more satisfying.”
  • “Once the odds are published, there is no problem.” Transparency is only one floor; you also have to look at spending caps, protection of minors, and whether anything beyond the pity tempts overspending.
  • “As long as players are willing to pay, it is voluntary.” If the willingness is manufactured by fake countdowns, punishing resets or pushes sent at emotional lows, “voluntary” is discounted.
  • “Limited-time and scarcity are bad in themselves.” Real limited-time events and rare items are legitimate; what crosses the line is faking and coercion.

Reflection and debate

① Animal reinforcement experiments and research on supernormal stimuli are extended to human players only by analogy. In 2019 the World Health Organization listed “gaming disorder” in the eleventh edition of the International Classification of Diseases (in force from 1 January 2022). But game-industry bodies objected, and critics think the evidence is not yet enough. Attitudes also differ from country to country.

② The “line” itself is contested too: some (often some industry practitioners) think players are adults and should take responsibility for themselves; others stress that sellers hold more information and psychological tools, so their responsibility is greater. These are two different positions.

③ The business reality is also real: a team has to survive, and free games need revenue. What ethical design asks is not to make money from players’ weaknesses; it does not ask to make no money.

④ The strength of loss aversion has been questioned by some researchers in recent years. When a mechanism “works” but leaves players regretting it afterwards, does it count as a success?
