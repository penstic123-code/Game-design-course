Chapter 12

Audio & Visual Design

Mute a game, then paint it as a pure black silhouette, and see what is left.

Learning goals

  • Name the four functions of sound in games, and use “envelope, pitch, duration” to design a sound effect that is understood at once.
  • Explain why adaptive music was invented, and tell “horizontal switching” from “vertical layering.”
  • Use color, shape, silhouette and easing to check whether a picture “reads at a glance and feels right,” and say why exaggeration and grouping grab attention.
about 45 min
1

What it isSound is not background; it is a second screen

Try a little experiment: play a small game you know well on your phone, and this time turn the sound off. You will find yourself dulled: you can’t tell whether you picked up the coin, whether someone is behind you, or even whether “that hit landed.”

Sound isn’t only there to be “pleasant”; it has been quietly passing along information for you all along. With the picture already so busy, why open another channel? Because eyes can look in only one direction, while ears can take in messages from all around at once without using up your line of sight.

Sound in a game broadly serves four functions, a split commonly used in the industry:

Feedback: the “ding” of collecting a coin in Super Mario. Short, bright, almost impossible to mishear. When we talked about game feel, we said sound often accounts for half of it.
Information: in many shooters, footsteps tell you whether the enemy is upstairs or downstairs, even which side of a wall. You haven’t seen him, yet you already know where to hide.
Daily life: the microwave’s “ding” is feedback, an ambulance siren is information, a violin tremolo in a film is emotion, and hearing, eyes closed, that someone is walking in from the left is space. You have long been a skilled “listener designer.”
Counterexample: everything makes a sound. Every button click plays a gorgeous 3-second effect: cool the first time, annoying the tenth, and by the fiftieth you want to mute. The sound has become noise, the player suffers “listening fatigue,” and the sound that really matters is drowned out.
2

What it isSome principles of sound-effect design, and the “envelope”

Designing a sound effect is really turning a few knobs. The most critical is the envelope, that is, “how the sound starts and how it falls away.” With the same pitch, whether it starts fast or slow makes two entirely different things to the ear:

A very short attack with no sustain is the crisp of a plucked string, a knock or a coin; a slow attack with a long release is the soft of a string pad or a magic incantation. This four-stage description comes from synthesizers, and the sound chips of early home machines handed you ADSR knobs directly.

The two notes have the same pitch, with only the envelope changed. Press either one first.
Try it: listen to both, then close your eyes and think: which one is more like “picking up a coin,” and which is more like “charging a spell”?

A few more design rules of thumb, common practice in the industry rather than laws:

🎚️

Layering

An “explosion” is often built from a low-frequency thump, a mid-frequency burst and high-frequency debris stacked together. Each layer handles one band of frequencies, so it doesn’t turn muddy.

👂

Distinguishable

Important sounds should be set apart from others in frequency, timbre or rhythm. “Getting hit” and “picking up” must never sound alike.

⏱️

Short

Feedback sounds that occur often mostly end within a fraction of a second. Short, so that they don’t cover the next one.

📈

Pitch varies with intensity

The higher the combo count, the more the pickup sound rises a semitone each time, so the player “hears” themselves climbing a slope.

🔀

Variation

Change the pitch of the same footstep slightly each time, or alternate several recordings, to avoid “machine-gun” repetition.

🙉

Leave gaps

A picture where everything makes a sound is a picture where nothing can be heard. Making way for the important sound is commonly called “ducking.”

3

What it isMusic: looping, switching and silence

Music has an inborn difficulty: the player’s time is uncertain. A film score can be written to a timeline; in a game you don’t know whether the player will stay in this room for 5 seconds or 5 minutes, nor on which beat a fight will break out. There are three common answers:

  • Looping: a piece of music plays over and over, end joined to start. The simplest, and the difficulty is keeping people from noticing the seam and the repetition.
  • Horizontal switching: when the state changes (from exploring to fighting), switch to a different piece of music, cutting in on a beat or a bar line.
  • Vertical layering: one piece is split into several synchronized tracks (drums, bass, melody…), and when danger rises a layer is “pushed in,” without changing the piece.

The last often underrated tool is silence. The brain filters out something that is always sounding; if the music is suddenly pulled before a boss appears, leaving only breathing, the tension is actually greatest. Treat “quiet” as a deliberately designed “timbre,” not a blank.

Silence as reward: in some puzzle and exploration games, big areas have only wind and water, and music comes in only when you find the key object, so the music itself becomes the signal “you got it right.”
In daily life: horror films. What really scares is often not the screaming strings but the second the music stops: something you have been listening to suddenly vanishes, and the body goes on alert before the mind.
Counterexample: forgetting that people stay a long time. A 90-second loop plays for 40 minutes in a single level, restarting from the top each time and clashing with the rhythm of the footsteps. Nine times in ten the player goes into settings and turns the music off.
4

What it isVisuals (part 1): the language of color and shape

The first job of visual design is not “looking nice” but letting the player tell within 0.5 seconds: what is this, can I touch it, where should I look first. The two most powerful tools are color and shape.

Color: hue, contrast and saturation

Hues often bring associations: red is danger and health, green is safety and healing, blue is calm and magic, yellow is attention and coins, purple is rare. But these associations vary with culture and genre, and the more reliable use is: be consistent within one game, so the player learns it once.

Two common tools: contrast, so that what you want the player to see stands apart from the background in lightness, saturation or hue; and saturation is a limited budget: if the whole screen is highly saturated there is no focus, so keep backgrounds low in saturation and save high saturation for things that are “interactive and important.”

The language of shape

There is a famous experiment in psychology: show people a rounded shape and a spiky one and ask which is “bouba” and which is “kiki,” and the great majority say the round one is bouba. Designers have taken over this intuition that “shapes have personalities”:

This is design experience plus a little psychological support. Super Mario has round yet dangerous enemies and pointy yet cute characters, and breaking expectations is sometimes exactly the joke.

Round and sharp: in many games the enemies have pointed corners and asymmetric outlines, while the hero is rounder, with friendly proportions. The player hasn’t read a word, yet already knows whom to approach and whom to avoid.
In daily life: road signs. A triangle is a warning, a circle is a command or prohibition, a square gives information. The shape is itself a code, and a driver reads it at a glance from far off with no need to read.
Counterexample: red doing two jobs. Good pickups are red, while red also marks an enemy’s attack range. The player can only rely on memorizing text, not “knowing at a glance.”
5

What it isVisuals (part 2): hierarchy, silhouette and motion

Visual hierarchy decides where the player looks first: themselves first, then threats and goals, then interactive objects, and the background last. A homemade way to check: squint, or blur or desaturate the picture, and see whether the focus can still be made out.

Character design has a similar test, called silhouette readability. Fill the character in solid black, and if you can still tell who it is, the outline is a success. In fast combat the player has no time to make out the patterns on clothes and can only recognize characters by outline.

Conversely, if a row of characters differ only in color and have exactly the same outline, filled in black they become five black blobs, and no pretty palette can save that.

Motion: why should movement “have expression”?

For a square moving from A to B, constant-speed sliding is like a machine, and speeding up then slowing down is like something with weight. Behind this are principles summed up by animators, and the ones most relevant to games are:

  • Anticipation: crouch a little before the jump. It also gives the player a signal that “it’s coming.”
  • Squash and stretch: stretched on takeoff, flattened on landing, with the volume unchanged, and the object “has weight.”
  • Easing (slow in & slow out): slower at the start and end, fast in the middle. Ease-out is the most comfortable for pop-ups, and a touch of “bounce” makes them look elastic.
Squash and stretch: in many side-scrolling action games the character stretches on takeoff and drops into a slight crouch on landing, kicking up dust. You don’t need to look at any numbers to know “it landed,” and you feel the character has some weight.
In daily life: pop-ups and elevator doors. A pop-up that “springs” out on a phone is friendlier than one that appears with a snap; an elevator door starts slowly, moves fast in the middle, and stops slowly. All easing, you just never noticed.
Counterexample: health that drains at a constant rate. A health bar falls from full to 0 at constant speed, and every hit feels like “falling into a spreadsheet.” Add a small delay and a bounce, and the same number immediately has impact.

Style, consistency and the performance trade-off

There is no “best” art style, only the “most suitable.” Pixel art, low-poly, hand-drawn and realistic each have costs: realism needs a lot of assets and performance, while a stylized look can reach a unified feel with very few resources. Consistency matters more than fineness: one set of lines, one palette and one set of proportions feels more like “one world” than a few top-class assets used together. Indie developers often start from “limits,” a fixed palette (say 16 colors) and a fixed resolution; the limit itself is the style.

How it was inventedLimits create style: from 3 voices to music that “breathes”

Problem one: you have only three “mouths,” yet the player may interrupt you at any time

In the early-1980s home computers and consoles, the sound chip was a fixed piece of hardware, not a player. It had only a handful of voices, each able to sound only one note at a time. You had to fit melody, bass and rhythm into those few voices, and keep one for sound effects. On top of that came another difficulty: a game is not a film, and the player decides what happens and when.

Now be the designer first. The little instrument below is looping “exploration” music, and imagine a fight suddenly breaks out: how would you make the music keep up? Try all three approaches and hear the difference (sound plays only after you click).

Press “Start” first, then press “Enter combat” at any moment. Press each button a few times and try different timings.
Try it: use each of the three “enter combat” methods once (press “Back to exploring” after each to switch to the next), and notice which one makes you feel “the music is listening to me.”

The differences you just heard are the wall that programmers and composers hit in history. A hard cut chops the phrase off mid-sentence, and the keys may even clash; waiting for the bar line is clean, but you lag half a beat; layering is instant and unbroken, provided the piece was written from the start to be “taken apart.”

First came the limits of the hardware, and the first move musicians thought of was to turn the limits into a style: carefully distributing melody, bass and drum beats among those few voices. But as games grew longer and less predictable, “writing cleverly” was no longer enough, and music had to learn to follow the game.

1981–1982 Bob Yannes at MOS Technology leads a small team designing the SID sound chip (6581), put into the Commodore 64. It has 3 voices, each with four waveforms (triangle, sawtooth, variable pulse width, noise), its own ADSR envelope, and a programmable filter. It is said they originally wanted more voices, and under time pressure copied a single oscillator three times.
1980s The Ricoh 2A03 in Famicom-class consoles has 5 channels: 2 pulse waves, 1 triangle wave, 1 noise and 1 DPCM sample channel. Musicians could only distribute melody, bass and drums among those few “mouths,” and this limit gradually became a highly recognizable “chiptune” style.
1991 Michael Land and Peter McConnell, composers at LucasArts, dissatisfied with the audio systems of the day, create iMUSE (Interactive Music Streaming Engine). It lets music transition seamlessly with the action on screen, and was first used in Monkey Island 2. They later filed a patent for it (US 5,315,057).
Afterward Horizontal switching and vertical layering become common tools, and audio middleware such as Wwise and FMOD make “control music by game parameters” standard: a designer pushes a “tension” fader and the drums and bass come in by themselves.

Key insight: music is no longer a recorded timeline, but a system that responds to the game state. The limit hasn’t gone away; it has just moved from “a few voices” to “how to synchronize and transition.”

Sources: the Wikipedia entries on iMUSE and the MOS 6581, and the Land/McConnell interviews they cite; accounts of the patent date differ a little, so only the patent number is given here. The Famicom channel count is from the Ricoh 2A03 entry.

Problem two: how do you make something drawn on paper “look like it has weight”?

Animators faced a different limit: every frame had to be drawn by hand, and as soon as the audience felt it was “fake,” they wouldn’t believe the thing in the picture was alive. If a little ball falls from a height and bounces, and moves the same distance every frame, it looks like a button sliding along a rail.

Many people find C the most natural, B like accelerating in a fall, and A like a machine. Over the course of making feature films, Disney’s animators gradually discovered this kind of lesson that makes motion “alive”: speeding up as it falls, flattening on landing, stretching as it springs up, and slowing a little at the top.

From the 1930s In the course of making feature films, Disney’s animators gradually sum up a set of lessons for making motion “alive” (anticipation, squash and stretch, slow in and slow out…), later organized into the “twelve principles.”
1981 Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston publish The Illusion of Life: Disney Animation, writing down these twelve systematically, the one they valued most being “squash and stretch.” Game and UI motion design have borrowed from it ever since.

The key insight: the “weight” and “life” an audience feels lie not in how finely each frame is drawn but in how the speed changes. Jumps, hits and pop-ups in games today all repeat this discovery.

Sources: Wikipedia, “Twelve basic principles of animation,” and the details of Thomas and Johnston’s 1981 book.

Why it worksWhy do exaggerated things grab us more: supernormal stimuli

We have said a lot of “design experience”: pointy feels like a threat, red feels like danger, a landing should squash. Is there a more basic reason? There is one lead from ethology that many designers bring up.

A herring gull chick, from the moment it hatches, pecks at the red spot on its parent’s beak, and the parent coughs up food to feed it. The Dutch zoologist Niko Tinbergen wanted to know: is the chick drawn by the “beak,” or by the “red spot”?

He and his colleague Perdeck made a batch of models. The result was odd: a simple red rod with three white rings painted on it drew more pecks than a realistic model of a gull’s head. The real thing was not the most attractive; something that exaggerates the key feature past the limit was stronger. Tinbergen called such things “supernormal stimuli.”

This is what comics, mascots and game effects do: draw big eyes bigger, make a coin burst fuller, hold the frame of a hit longer. What is exaggerated is not everything, but the feature that lets you recognize it.

Supernormal stimulus

The principle: an organism’s response to certain key features can be triggered more strongly by a model that is “more exaggerated” than the natural object. The counterpart in design is to “amplify the one feature you want the player to perceive,” not to draw everything more realistically.

Sources: Tinbergen & Perdeck (1950), “On the stimulus situation releasing the begging response in the newly hatched herring gull chick,” Behaviour 3: 1–39; Deirdre Barrett, Supernormal Stimuli (2010). Controversy: John Whitfield pointed out in Nature in 2009 that the data of these classic experiments are not as clean as textbooks say, and whether the red spot really is the strongest stimulus is still doubtful; “supernormal stimulus” is a handy metaphor in design, not a law.

Why it worksWhy do eyes “group” by themselves: Gestalt

Why does visual hierarchy work? Because your brain never reads a picture as a heap of pixels; it automatically sorts things into gangs. Dots close together are a group, dots that look alike are a group, and dots moving the same way are a group.

In the early twentieth century, the German psychologist Max Wertheimer set these regularities out and opened the “Gestalt” school. What lets a health bar, skill bar and minimap in a game interface be “seen as one block” at a glance is exactly this: put related things close together and make them look the same.

Gestalt grouping (proximity, similarity)

The principle: perception has its own organizing rules, and things close together tend to be seen as a group, as do things that look alike. In design it decides “what goes together,” with no need to draw a frame line.

Source: Wertheimer (1923), “Untersuchungen zur Lehre von der Gestalt II,” Psychologische Forschung 4: 301–350. Later researchers differ on exactly how many laws there are, and some think proximity, common fate and others are all special cases of “similarity.”

Why it worksAre ears faster? Do shapes have personality? (And how to check)

Now two more claims that are often cited, to see how far the research actually supports them.

1) Hearing reacts faster than vision: say it carefully

The common claim: “People react to sound faster than to a picture.” Some simple reaction-time studies support this: Shelton and Kumar (2010, Neuroscience & Medicine) had only 14 participants, measured about 331 ms on average for vision and about 284 ms for hearing, a gap of about 47 ms; later literature often cites “hearing is about 20–40 ms faster.”

The key points: the sample is small, the gap is on the order of “tens of milliseconds,” and it is simple reaction (press a key on seeing or hearing). Don’t stretch it to “sound matters more than picture.” For games the conclusion is: for events needing the fastest feedback, pair sound and picture, and any mismatch between them is hard to bear.

2) Round and sharp: bouba/kiki

Wolfgang Köhler (1929) had people match words to shapes with “takete / baluba,” and they tended to pair the spiky shape with takete. In 2001 Ramachandran and Hubbard redid it with “bouba/kiki,” and about 95–98% of American undergraduates and Tamil speakers in India called the round one bouba. A 2022 study of about 900 people across 25 languages also found a cross-language tendency, but its strength varies by language.

This suggests that the “shape and sound” association has a cross-cultural part, but it isn’t the same for every person and every language. In design it is “borrowing a common intuition,” not “proof.”

Common misconceptions

  • “The louder and more gorgeous the sound effects, the better.” The most common result is listening fatigue and a volume war. A good mix makes trade-offs: the important sounds loud, the minor ones stepping back.
  • “Colors have fixed meanings.” Red isn’t necessarily danger and green isn’t necessarily safe; they mean something only within your game’s rules. Consistency matters more than “convention.”
  • “The finer the picture, the better.” What players need is to see clearly. Too much detail drowns the focus and eats performance.
  • “Hearing is faster than vision, so sound is more important.” The gap is tens of milliseconds, from small experiments, and doesn’t support “more important.”
  • “Music is just background.” It also conveys state and emotion; and some players play with the sound off, in public places for instance, or because they can’t hear, so key information can’t rely on sound alone.

Reflection and debate

Culture and individuals. Color associations depend heavily on culture and context: red is festive in many East Asian contexts and dangerous in many Western games. Shape associations (bouba/kiki) show a cross-language trend, but with differences in strength too. Treat them as starting points, not conclusions.

Style versus legibility. A cool “dark, low-contrast” picture may be unreadable for players with low vision; a game that can be played only with the music on is unfriendly to deaf and hard-of-hearing players. Good audio-visual design gives players adjustable options: separate volume tracks, subtitles, a colorblind mode, reduced motion.

“Pleasant to hear” and “useful” don’t always agree. The loveliest music may actually be distracting, such as a melody in a puzzle game taking up the room for thinking. There is no standard answer here, and it has to be verified with real playtesters.

Something that may change your mind. Design is not “making sound and picture pretty” but translating rules into sensory signals: so that a player who never reads the manual can still “hear the rules and see the rules.”

Key points

  • Sound has four functions: feedback, information, emotion, space; sound effects are designed through envelope, pitch, duration and timbre, and should be short, distinguishable, layered and fatigue-free.
  • The difficulty of music is that the player’s time is uncertain: looping, horizontal switching, vertical layering; systems like iMUSE make music a “system that responds to the game state”; silence is also a tool.
  • Visuals first serve legibility: color (consistent, contrasting, not color alone), the language of shape, hierarchy, the silhouette test.
  • Motion borrows the twelve principles of animation: anticipation, squash and stretch, easing, which decide “feel” and “weight.”
  • Underneath are two leads: supernormal stimuli (exaggerate the key feature) and Gestalt grouping (proximity, similarity); research gives “tendencies,” not laws. Matching sound and picture matters more than any single technique.
Sound and picture are the language through which a game translates rules into sensory signals: be short, be clear, be consistent, and never let one channel carry all the information alone.
