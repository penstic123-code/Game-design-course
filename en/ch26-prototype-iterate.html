Chapter 26

Prototyping & Iteration

Don’t think a year ahead. Make a paper version you can play today.

⏱ about 50 min

Learning goals

  • Explain why iterative design appeared: what the risk of finalizing in one go is, and why a game’s experience can only be “found by trying.”
  • Explain why small improvements get stuck on a “good enough” hilltop, how exploration and exploitation should be mixed, and why the faster the feedback the better.
  • String the previous twenty-five chapters into one line, and generate your own one-page design.
1

What it isDesign is “found by trying”

Imagine sitting at a desk with a page of rules written out: the goal, how pieces move, how points are scored, every item weighed, the whole thing looking seamless. Then a friend picks it up, plays for ten minutes, and does something you never thought of: he finds that by running into a corner he can win forever.

Whether the designer is dim has nothing to do with it. What you write down is rules, and what players get is an experience, and between them lies a layer of “how players will actually play.” How rules collide and how people find loopholes can’t be fully worked out in your head; only real people playing can show it.

So professional teams don’t rely on thinking it through once. They rely on a circle that keeps turning: make a prototype, find people to play it, see how they play, change a little, and play again. Turning the circle round after round is iteration.

2

What it isWhy prototypes should be rough, and early

Prototypes are rough because rough is cheap. Changing a rule on paper takes one pen. Discover a problem in the rules after the art, levels and code are all done, and you have to tear it down and start over. The cost of one change rises the further the project goes.

This is an illustrative curve to build intuition, not measured data; the curve’s shape differs between projects.

The first playtest of Magic: The Gathering: the designer Richard Garfield himself recounts that the earliest Alpha playtest had 120 cards, dealt at random to two players, and they just started playing. Several rounds of changes followed, Beta and Gamma, and by Gamma the cost of many creatures had been raised, and the cards that “steal an opponent’s creature permanently” had already become rare and were later dropped altogether. Problems that show only when the cards are together were found by playing round after round.
The paper stage of Armello: the indie team said in an interview that, although they knew it would end up a digital game, they first iterated with paper prototypes for about eight months. During playtests two “hosts” handled the hidden rules by hand, in effect simulating the computer with human labor.
Cooking a new dish: nobody gets the salt right the first time. Add a little, taste, add more. “Tasting” is the cheapest playtest. Showing friends the outline before writing an essay is the same idea.
A “complete version” made behind closed doors for a year (a hypothetical, not a specific project): beautiful art and twenty levels all done, and only after release does it turn out players get stuck in level one. What then needs changing is the whole level and the teaching; one rule is nowhere near enough. A paper prototype would have exposed this in an afternoon.

Sources: Garfield, The Creation of Magic: The Gathering (Wizards of the Coast site, 2013 reprint of a 1994 article); Armello developer interview: PAX AUS 2015 interview (the developer’s own account; defer to it for the exact length).

How it was inventedHow was iteration thought up

First try it yourself. Suppose you are the project lead, and the client only vaguely said “about this level,” with the real requirement hidden at some unknown position. There are two ways to work: finalize in one go, with a single chance; or split it into four rounds, where after each delivery the players tell you which way you are off and by how much. Play a few games of each and see the results.

People in history ran into the same wall

Software keeps getting larger, and needs are hard to state clearly at the start. If the work is lined up in a straight line, finishing all the requirements first, then design, then coding, then testing, it is like the “one go” above. The first sight of real reactions comes on delivery day, when it is too late to change.

The software world was not the first to think of this. According to Larman and Basili’s historical survey, its source can be traced to the quality-improvement work of Walter Shewhart at Bell Labs in the 1930s: plan, do, check, act, round and round, later popularized by Deming.

In the 1950s the X-15 hypersonic aircraft project already used iterative, incremental methods. The NASA Project Mercury software team used very short time-boxed iterations in the early 1960s (sources differ slightly on the years).

The sequential approach owes its reputation to a 1970 paper by Winston Royce. In “Managing the Development of Large Software Systems” he drew a sequential flow chart of “requirements → design → coding → testing → operations,” which later came to be called the “waterfall model.” But Royce himself never used the word “waterfall”; according to several sources it may first have appeared in a 1976 paper by Bell and Thayer.

Royce’s paper in fact points out that this simplest approach is very risky, and recommends adding iteration and early trial builds. Scholars read the paper differently on how far it “opposes” the approach.

Later people moved step by step to another idea: don’t line up “thinking it through” and “making it” as two steps in sequence; make a small piece, try it, and decide the next step from that.

From 1986 to 1988 Barry Boehm proposed the “spiral model”: advance in rounds driven by risk, each round first using a prototype to test the biggest uncertainty. In February 2001, 17 software practitioners at the Snowbird ski resort in Utah wrote the Agile Manifesto, which puts “responding to change” before “following a plan.”

For games, this approach is especially important. In Rules of Play (2003), Salen and Zimmerman call game design a “second-order design problem”: what you design is rules, but the experience is played out by the player, so you can’t design the experience directly, only make something playable and see.

In Play as Research: The Iterative Design Process, Eric Zimmerman used three projects, SiSSYFiGHT 2000, LOOP and LEGO Junkbot, to show this approach: build a playable prototype as soon as possible, playtest it, and decide where the game should grow according to how the test felt.

Why it worksWith many rules, working it out stops working

“Trying it out” sounds like designers being lazy, but there are three very solid reasons. The first is mathematical: combinations of rules grow much faster than the rules themselves.

Complex systems are unpredictable: the design space is too large to work out

With 20 rules, pairing gives 190 pairs, not counting cases where three or more act together. Each pair may add up, cancel or produce new play. Chapter 5 covered emergence: these interactions grow overall behavior the designer never wrote down. This is what Salen and Zimmerman call the “second-order design problem.” So even the most experienced can only form hypotheses first, and let the prototype verify them.

Each rule added brings a whole new patch of interactions to think through.

The Gamma version of Magic: a card that “steals an opponent’s creature permanently” looks cool alone, and the problem shows only when it is placed among the interactions of a whole deck.
Adding a one-way street in a city: it looks reasonable locally, yet may jam the next street. Changes in a system spread along its connections.
“A 50-page design doc is watertight, so no playtest is needed”: a document can only describe the rules, and cannot describe what players will do once the rules collide.

Source of the principle: Salen and Zimmerman · 2003 · Rules of Play (MIT Press) · proposes the “second-order design problem.” The number of pairs among 20 rules is 20×19÷2=190.

Why it worksWhy small-step improvement gets stuck at “OK”

Once you know to iterate, the next question is: in which direction to change each round? The most natural way is small-step improvement. Raise it a bit in this version; if it’s bad, step back; if good, keep it. Each version a bit better than the last sounds foolproof.

But think of the climber in the fog: with each step he goes higher, and at some point everything around is downhill, so he stops, thinking he is at the top. In fact across a valley stands a much higher mountain he has never seen.

A point that is “better than its neighbors but not the best” belongs, in optimization, to what is called a local optimum.

Explore and exploit: polish most of the time, leap boldly now and then

In decision theory this dilemma is exploration versus exploitation: next to you is a slot machine that pays decently, so do you keep pulling it, or spend a few pulls trying others? The classic “multi-armed bandit” problem asks exactly this. All exploitation leaves you stuck at a local optimum. All exploration burns your time on things that never get polished. The organization scholar James March pointed out in a 1991 paper that an organization that only polishes known practices does well in the short term and is dragged down in the long term.

Optimization algorithms have a kindred method: in 1983 Kirkpatrick, Gelatt and Vecchi proposed “simulated annealing,” which at first allows an occasional step in a worse direction, and grows more conservative as time passes. The lesson for design: leap boldly early, polish late. There is no standard ratio; it depends on time and risk.

Exploration and Exploitation

Exploration and exploitation

Keep using a method known to be pretty good (exploitation), or pay a cost to try something not yet tried (exploration). All exploitation leaves you stuck at a local optimum; all exploration never gets anything polished.

ExampleA designer tunes a tower’s damage through ten versions, each a little better, while the real problem is that the map has only one path and its structure has to change.

A tower defense game (hypothetical): the designer tunes the towers’ damage ten versions in a row, each a little better. The real problem is “the map has only one road,” which tuning numbers can never fix; the map structure has to change.
Picking a restaurant: choose only among the few near home, and you always pick “the least bad one.” Only an occasional visit to a place you’ve never been may find something better.
Revising a résumé: ten versions of rewording, each a bit smoother, yet the direction you send it in is wrong. Small-step improvement can’t touch a problem like “direction.”
Always starting over: the other extreme. Each time a change of direction, no version polished to a playable state, forever exploring and not exploiting at all.
6

Why it worksThe faster the feedback, the faster the learning

The last reason is the plainest: in the same 12 weeks, rounds of 2 weeks give only 6 times a “guess is checked against reality,” and rounds of 2 days give 42. The number of checks is the number of lessons.

Fast feedback

It is generally held that timely feedback helps learning and correction. In memory research, delayed feedback sometimes serves long-term retention better (the “desirable difficulties” proposed by Schmidt & Bjork). That concerns a learner’s memory, while here we are talking about how fast a designer gets information, and the two situations differ. Fast is not the same as careless: if the people playing are wrong for the purpose, fast only means reaching a wrong conclusion sooner.

Source of the principle: the industry consensus on iterative development and rapid prototyping; Schmidt & Bjork (1992), Psychological Science 3(4), on feedback and retention in training.
Tuning difficulty in a web mini game: drag a slider and see the difficulty change at once, ten sets of parameters in a minute. If you had to repackage and publish each time, you might try one set a day.
Practicing free throws: a coach says “a bit left” on the spot and the next ball can be corrected; get a note a week later, and that week was spent practicing on the wrong feel.
Every round fast, but the testers are always yourself and colleagues: colleagues know the game too well and can’t see a newcomer’s sticking points. You got a wrong conclusion quickly.

ReviewLooking back: the structure of the previous twenty-five chapters

Having come this far, the chapters you have read can be strung into a line. One key point per chapter, plus a line of “why.” Click a chapter title to go back and reread it.

String these five parts into one sentence: first know what games are, then understand the player’s experience, then master the systems behind it, then settle your own stance, and finally make it with cases, and learn to improve it round by round.

Common misconceptions

  • “Iteration means keep adding things”: often iteration is cutting, simplifying and merging.
  • “The more the prototype looks like the finished thing, the better”: the more it looks finished, the harder to bring yourself to change it, and the easier to put attention on art rather than play.
  • “Do whatever players say they want”: players are good at pointing out what is wrong, but the designer still has to find how to fix it.
  • “Keep improving in small steps and you will reach the best”: you may only have climbed the nearest little hill.
  • “The waterfall model is what Royce recommended”: Royce’s paper didn’t use that word and was discussing the risks of this approach.
  • “The hill-climbing chart, cost curve and speed chart above are real data”: all are illustrative, to build intuition.

Reflection and debate

Think back on the last piece of work you “changed again and again” (a game, an essay, a slide deck, anything): were you polishing in small steps, or boldly changing direction? Looking back now, which “small hilltop” might you have been stuck on?

Agile and iteration are often held up as the standard answer, but for engineering with very clear needs and extremely costly changes (a rocket that can’t be test-fired again and again, say), doing the design thoroughly first also makes sense. Iteration is strongest when “uncertainty is large and trial and error is cheap.”

Key points

  • Iteration = make a small piece → playtest → observe → revise, repeatedly. The earlier, rougher and faster, the smaller the cost of change.
  • It is not an invention unique to games: its source can be traced to quality improvement and the engineering practice of the 1950s–60s. “Waterfall” is a later, simplified name for Royce’s paper.
  • Games are “second-order design”: the experience is played out by players, and with many rules it can’t be worked out in full, so you must make something playable and look.
  • Small-step improvement easily falls into a local optimum and needs an occasional bold leap. Rapid iteration presupposes that the people playing and the records kept are reliable.
Nobody can know on paper whether it will be fun: systems are too complex, it is easy to get stuck at a local optimum, and the faster the feedback the faster the learning. So make it rough first, test it quickly, change it often, and leap now and then.
