Chapter 9

Narrative Design

A good game story is one the player helps tell with their own hands.

Learning goals

about 50 min
  • Rank storytelling methods by “player involvement,” and say why what can be told through play should not be told in a cutscene.
  • Explain what problems branching, merging and false choices appeared to solve, and build a structure with 4 endings within a fixed content budget.
  • After reading, you can check a story with causal links, the information gap and the peak–end rule: why it flows, why it makes people want to keep going, and why the ending matters more than the middle.
1

What it isThe same “this world is poor,” told two ways

Suppose two games both want players to believe “this world is short of supplies.” The first opens with a two-minute narration, a hoarse voice telling how war ruined the granaries. The second says nothing and gives only a 6-slot backpack.

Three hours later the narrator’s lines are forgotten, but the player still remembers that night: hesitating a long time between two weapons and three days of rations, and finally throwing the axe away by the roadside. The second game made the player feel scarcity; the first only told them about it.

That is what is special about storytelling in games. Narrative is not only the lines written in a script: players use their hands, and every action of the hands can be a sentence. So the means of storytelling can be lined up from low to high by how much the player takes part.

🎬

Cutscene narrative

Control is taken away for a while, like watching a film. Good at emotion and big events; too long and it breaks the pacing.

🗣️

Embedded narrative

The story is “put into” the game: dialogue, logs, recordings, quest text. Players can read at their own pace.

🏚️

Environmental narrative

Scenes hint at what happened: an overturned chair, an unfinished meal, scratches on the wall. It can be told without a single word.

🎮

Gameplay narrative

The play itself is the story: the player’s actions, choices and changes in the rules let them experience it personally, not be told.

Gameplay storytelling: a 6-slot backpack. Nobody says “supplies are scarce,” but every item picked up means dropping another. The player isn’t told; they live it.
Environmental storytelling: a room. An overturned chair, a smashed cup, scratches on the door. Not a line of dialogue, yet the player has worked out that a fight happened here and something wanted to break in.
Environmental storytelling in daily life: a stranger’s room. Three empty coffee cups on the desk, sticky notes all over the wall. Without a word from him you can read that he stayed up late last night rushing work.
Counterexample: a ten-minute cutscene every ten steps. The story can be good, but the player has the controls taken away again and again, the controller lies in their lap, and the pacing is chopped to pieces. Cutscenes are not the fault. The fault is using them too often.
Core principle: whatever can be told through gameplay, don’t tell through a cutscene; whatever the player can “do,” don’t make them “watch.” There is also a kind of story that nobody wrote at all: the rules sit there, and the players play out a legend themselves, like emergence. Simulation and sandbox games often have this “emergent narrative.”
2

What it isAgency: why doing it yourself weighs more than watching

A film viewer sits in the audience; a game player stands on the stage. The satisfying power of taking meaningful action and seeing your decisions bring results is, for the scholar Janet Murray, agency. It has two halves: the player acts, and the world responds. Without the second half, it is only pressing buttons.

Concept card

Agency

Agency

Janet Murray · her 1997 book Hamlet on the Holodeck

The satisfaction of taking meaningful action and seeing the world respond to it. Both the action and the visible consequence are needed.

ExampleIn a game with a 6-slot backpack, picking up one thing means dropping another; the player makes the trade-off by hand and sees the result.

Agency has an awkward flip side: when story and gameplay say different things, players trust their hands more. In an October 2007 blog post analyzing BioShock, the designer Clint Hocking called this ludonarrative dissonance.

The story prizes selflessness, while the gameplay rewards the player for sacrificing the “Little Sisters” for upgrade materials, and the player remembers the gameplay. The term has since been overused. Here it serves only as a checking tool: does the action the player repeats most in this game match the theme the story wants?

Concept card

Ludonarrative dissonance

Ludonarrative dissonance

Clint Hocking · his October 2007 blog post “Ludonarrative Dissonance in Bioshock”

The values the game’s story conveys contradict the behavior the gameplay encourages. Players trust their hands, not the lines.

ExampleThe story keeps saying “I don’t want to hurt anyone,” while the gameplay pops up +100 for every enemy knocked down.

❌ Dissonant

Plot: the hero cherishes life and keeps saying “I don’t want to hurt anyone.”
Gameplay: the whole game runs on killing enemies, and the more you kill the more you’re rewarded.
What the player remembers is the gameplay, not the lines.

✔ Consonant

Plot: the hero is a lonely wanderer who trusts no one.
Gameplay: supplies suffice for one person only, and bringing a teammate stretches resources thinner.
The gameplay itself “acts out” this personality.

Dissonance: says no killing, kills nonstop The hero with a halo says again and again “I don’t want to hurt anyone,” yet each enemy downed pops up +100. However moving the lines, what the player will do is what the reward points to.
Harmony: a horror game with no weapons The hero is empty-handed, and the player really can only run and hide. The fear is created by the rule “no weapons,” with no need for lines.
Dissonance in daily life: “people first” on the wall yet the performance board honors whoever worked latest. What the employees remember is the board, not the slogan. This is the same structure as “saying kindness while the play rewards killing.”
Nearly right: having combat is not dissonance The story says “cherish every life,” and in the play the reward for sneaking past guards (+200) is higher than for killing (+50), so the play also encourages not killing, and there is no dissonance here. What matters is where the reward points, and it has nothing to do with whether there is combat.

3

What it isBranching narrative: four structures, four costs

Once players can choose, the story is no longer a single line. The practical problem is that every extra branch multiplies the content to write and build. Designers gradually worked out several structures for keeping the cost under control:

Linear

One path from start to finish is the easiest to control and the most powerful narratively, but the player lacks the feeling of “I changed the story.”

String of pearls

The main line is like a thread strung with a few optional side-quest “beads,” all returning to the main line. Costs stay manageable.

Branching tree

Every choice forks, and the endings explode in number. The most freedom and the highest cost; few ever build one in full.

Bottleneck (fork and merge)

Fork first, merge at a “bottleneck,” then fork again. The player feels they are choosing, while the designer writes only a limited amount of content.

Merging is not deceit. What matters is whether the choice leaves consequences. Even if the plot merges, as long as earlier choices changed relationships, resources or later lines, the player still feels “this is my story.”
String of pearls: a main line threaded with side quests. The main quest goes straight ahead, with a few optional side quests along the way, and finishing them leads back to the main line. Side quests enrich the world without throwing the main line into disorder.
Bottleneck: two routes, one big scene Each route has a few unique scenes, then they merge at the same big battle, and afterward split again into different endings by the earlier choices. The merged scene need only be written once.
A bottleneck in daily life: the supermarket Three shelf aisles to wander freely, but in the end everyone passes the same checkout. The route is the customer’s choice, and the destination is the store’s.
Counterexample: every option leads to all-new plot. A novice team wanted every dialogue option to lead to a completely different story, and at the fourth layer found the content had multiplied several times over, so they had to cut a great deal. The final experience was more fragmented than a carefully designed bottleneck.
4

How it was inventedWhat forced branching and the three-act structure into being?

Story A: a page is a line, but the reader wants to decide for the hero

A printed book is linear by nature: after page 1 there is always page 2. But readers always want to interject: “What if he went left?” Literature had this thought early; Borges’s 1941 short story “The Garden of Forking Paths” imagined a world where time keeps forking. Still, it was only a story, and the reader couldn’t act.

The person who really made forking into a book you can flip through was Edward Packard. He worked out a way of writing that let the reader decide how the hero goes, and in 1969–1970 several big publishers rejected the manuscript. In 1976, Vermont Crossroads Press published his Sugarcane Island. In 1979, Bantam launched the “Choose Your Own Adventure” series, with the first volume his The Cave of Time.

Here the problem arrived: every extra choice the author offered meant writing twice as many pages. The pages of a book are fixed, and so is the budget. In the same period, text adventures on computers took another road, where players could type commands to steer the hero, but the ledger behind it was the same: every extra possibility meant writing another passage.

1976 Packard’s Sugarcane Island is published by Vermont Crossroads Press; at a fork the reader has to turn to a specified page number.
Mid to late 1970s Will Crowther writes Colossal Cave Adventure, which Don Woods expands and which spreads widely on mainframes, one of the starting points of text adventures. Then MIT students make Zork, which understands whole sentences, and in 1978 Scott Adams makes Adventureland on a home computer.
1979 Bantam makes this kind of book into a series, with every volume having to fit several routes and several endings into a fixed page count.
1982 Steve Jackson and Ian Livingstone’s The Warlock of Firetop Mountain is published by Puffin, starting the Fighting Fantasy solo gamebook series, with dice and combat added to the books.

So the remedy is plain: with a limited content budget, branches have to be controlled. Let branches merge later, or fork for real at only a few nodes, or use “false choices” that add no content to create a sense of taking part. Work out the cost for yourself first.

Story B: was the three-act structure invented by Aristotle?

Guess first, then open

Answer: no. In the Poetics, Aristotle says only that a complete action has a beginning, a middle and an end; he did not lay down “three acts.” The popularity of the modern three-act structure owes more to Syd Field’s 1979 screenwriting textbook Screenplay.

Imagine writing a two-hour script, about 120 pages. At page 50 you start to panic: has the pace collapsed? Where should the turn go? Nothing says what should roughly be happening now. A long story needs a locator.

The ancient Greeks were the first to notice that a complete story has a beginning and an end (Aristotle’s Poetics), but he spoke only of the whole and said nothing about page counts. In 1863 the German writer Gustav Freytag drew a five-part shape in Die Technik des Dramas: exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, catastrophe, later known as “Freytag’s pyramid.” It was drawn from stage plays, though. In 1949 Joseph Campbell, in The Hero with a Thousand Faces, distilled the “hero’s journey” from myths around the world: leaving the ordinary world, accepting the call, passing trials, the deepest crisis, and returning changed.

Finally Field broke a film script into “setup—confrontation—resolution,” about 30 / 60 / 30 pages, with a plot point at the end of the first act and the end of the second. This account is easy to teach and use, became standard in screenwriting classes, and game people later borrowed it to arrange the pacing of levels and flow.

Concept card

Three-act structure

Three-act structure

Syd Field · his 1979 screenwriting textbook Screenplay made it popular

A story is split into setup, confrontation and resolution, taking roughly 1/4, 1/2 and 1/4, with a turning point at the end of the first act and the end of the second.

ExampleThe three phases of a boss fight: probing, escalation, and a counterattack with your back to the wall.

4th century BCE Aristotle’s Poetics: a complete plot has a beginning, a middle and an end.
1863 Freytag’s Technique of the Drama: a five-part scheme of exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, catastrophe.
1949 Campbell’s The Hero with a Thousand Faces: the “hero’s journey” drawn from myths, later simplified by screenwriters into five or six steps.
1979 Syd Field’s Screenplay: boils scripts down to three acts and popularizes the idea.

The three acts are a checklist compiled by later writers, used to see whether a story has ups and downs and whether the turning points sit where the reader is about to tire.

In a game: the three phases of a boss fight. Probing, escalation and a desperate counterattack are three acts in miniature.
In daily life: a football match. The cautious feeling-out at the start, the stalemate and the turn in midfield, a winning goal in the last ten minutes. The audience’s tension rises and falls just like that.
Counterexample: the biggest climax at the very start. The giant boss and the full-screen explosions are used up right away, nothing is left to upgrade afterward, and the player’s tension can only slide downhill.

Why it worksWhy do people naturally read stories “along cause and effect”?

Look at these four sentences first: she went out without the keys, so she went back to the café, but the door was already locked, and the clerk, through the glass, held up a key to her. You understand with almost no effort. Now scramble the order and it feels awkward at once. The same information, just because causation broke, goes from a story back to scattered news.

Psychologists have found that people automatically string events into causal chains and then fill in intentions for the characters. In 1944, Fritz Heider and Marianne Simmel showed an animation of nothing but two triangles and a small circle moving in and around a box. Almost all the participants told stories of chasing, fighting and hiding. The shapes have no intentions; the viewers’ minds added them.

Causal chains, narrative and memory

① Causal understanding: Trabasso and van den Broek’s 1985 study found that the more causal links an event has to those before and after, the easier readers remember it and the more they treat it as the backbone of the story.
② Stories help memory: In 1969 Gordon Bower and Michael Clark had one group weave 10 unrelated words into a story while another group just memorized them. On the later test, those who made up a story remembered far more words (the often-cited figures are medians of 93% versus 13%). Later replications don’t always get a gap this wide, but the direction, “remembered better when linked by cause,” hasn’t changed.
③ Narrative transportation: Green and Brock proposed in 2000 that when a reader is “carried into” a story and temporarily forgets their surroundings, they are more likely to accept the views in the story.

Sources:
Heider and Simmel · 1944 · showed people an animation of two triangles and a circle · people told stories of chasing, fighting and hiding (“An experimental study of apparent behavior,” American Journal of Psychology 57).
Trabasso and van den Broek · 1985 · events with more causal connections are remembered better (Journal of Memory and Language 24).
Bower and Clark · 1969 · people who made up a story to learn words remembered far more (Psychonomic Science 14: 181–182).
Green and Brock · 2000 · proposed narrative transportation (Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 79).
Most of these findings come from text and film, so for games that are both read and played they can serve only as analogies.

A widely shared saying in writing circles goes: two adjacent scenes in a good story should be joinable with “therefore” or “but,” not “and then… and then…” (a craft rule of thumb, often credited to the creators of South Park). The same holds in games. What the player did, and how the world changed because of it, must both be visible. Quest logs, story recaps and changes in the scenery all help maintain this causal chain for the player.

In the game: a quest log that states cause and effect “Because the village was burned, go and find the blacksmith.” What to do next needs no guessing.
In daily life: a news report. “Because of the rainstorm, flights were canceled, so travelers were stranded” is easier to remember than three isolated items.
Daily life: a short film of only shapes There are no people on screen, yet viewers still tell a story of “bullying” and “escaping.” People supply causes and intentions automatically.
Counterexample: a string of “and then.” Take a quest, kill monsters, return to town, take another quest: with no “therefore” or “but” between the events, players can’t remember them and don’t care.

Why it worksWhy does “to be continued” make it so hard to stop?

Most people have had a night like this: sleepy, the series stopped at its tensest point, and still you tap “next episode,” because a few unanswered questions are hanging in your head.

In 1994 the behavioral economist George Loewenstein proposed information-gap theory: curiosity comes from “knowing a little, and knowing that you do not know the rest.” Know nothing and there is nothing to be curious about; understand nearly everything and curiosity fades too. The gap pulls hardest when it is the right size.

A hanging state is uncomfortable, and people want a definite answer and want to settle things. This tendency is the need for cognitive closure.

Popular accounts often lump it with the Zeigarnik effect, but the original claim of that effect, “unfinished tasks are remembered better,” has not replicated consistently. What stands on firmer ground is that unfinished goals keep occupying the mind, and that this nagging eases once a plan for finishing has been made.

Information-gap theory

Information-gap theory of curiosity

ExampleAt the end of a level a locked great door appears with light seeping through the crack, and the player plays another level carrying the question “what is in there?”

In a game: the door that won’t open. At the end of a level a locked great door appears, with light seeping through the crack. Carrying the question “what’s inside?”, the player is willing to play another level.
Daily life: the next-episode preview The preview of a mystery series is always cut to the most crucial second, giving only the question and not the answer, and so at half past midnight you click the next episode.
Daily life: a friend who says half a sentence “I’ll tell you something… never mind, next time.” That half sentence can hang you up all day.
Counterexample: a “shocking secret” that never pays off. The plot hints again and again at a great secret, hangs it out for a dozen chapters, and at the end gives only “it’s a mystery.” The reader’s curiosity turns into anger at being played.

Why the ending matters more than the middle: the peak–end rule

The peak–end rule came up in Chapter 6. Applied to narrative, its conclusion is slightly uncomfortable for designers: when readers recall an experience, they remember almost only the most intense moment and the last moment, while how long it lasted is easily overlooked.

The peak–end rule in narrative

Remember the cold-water experiment? In 1993 Kahneman and colleagues had participants hold a hand in 14°C water. In another trial they kept it there 30 seconds longer, while the water was quietly warmed a little (to about 15°C). Asked afterward which they would repeat, most chose the longer one, because it ended less painfully. In 1996 Redelmeier and Kahneman saw a similar pattern in colonoscopy patients: the later rating followed mainly the most painful moment and the last moment.

It concerns the evaluation held in memory. If the experience is too bad, the player quits on the spot and never reaches the ending. These studies mostly involve pain and short films, so for a game of dozens of hours they can serve only as an analogy. They are enough to make one point: put the hook where the reader is about to leave, and take care over the ending.

Sources:
Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber, Redelmeier · 1993 · cold-water hand experiment · most people chose to repeat the trial that ended less painfully (“When more pain is preferred to less,” Psychological Science 4(6): 401–405).

False choices are useful too: they let the player “act out” the character’s personality and make people feel respected. The problem is dressing a false choice up as a fate-changing event. Once seen through, the player feels cheated, and agency breaks at the point where “consequences are visible.”

Common misconceptions

  • “The longer the story, the deeper.” Length is not depth. What matters is clear causes, rising and falling tension, and gameplay matching the theme.
  • “More branches mean more agency.” Agency comes from visible consequences, not the number of forks.
  • “Suspense means hanging without answering.” Suspense that isn’t paid off turns into frustration. The gap has to be filled, ideally better than imagined.
  • “The three-act structure is a rule laid down by Aristotle.” It is a summary by later people, especially twentieth-century screenwriting textbooks, and only a checklist.

Reflection and debate

  • Is a game a narrative at all? Scholars argued over this for a long time: the narratologists see games as a new medium for storytelling, while the ludologists (who stress rules and play) hold that looking at games through a narrative frame misses what is essential about them. There is still no settled answer.
  • “People are born lovers of stories.” There is plenty of research supporting causal and intentional understanding; “stories are an adaptation humans evolved” is a view.
  • Most of the experimental material is text and film: research on narrative transportation, causal understanding and the peak–end rule cannot be carried over unchanged to players who both read and act.
  • The limits of the hook: using suspense to stretch play time may be good design, and may also become a means of “keeping people from sleeping.” Each designer must draw this line themselves.

Key points

  • Storytelling methods ranked by involvement: cutscene < embedded < environmental < gameplay storytelling. Let the player “do” first and let them “watch” less.
  • Agency is “action + visible consequence”; when what the plot says and what the gameplay encourages disagree, that is ludonarrative dissonance.
  • Content cost gave rise to the string of pearls, the bottleneck and false choices in branching narrative; consequences matter more than the number of forks.
  • Three acts are a “locator” for long stories, a tool later people summed up, not a rule from the ancients.
  • People understand stories through causal chains; suspense comes from information gaps and has to be paid off; what readers remember deepest are the strongest moment and the last moment.
Good game narrative: clear cause and effect, a moderate measure of suspense, the player making the story with their own hands, and the ending kept for the most unforgettable moment.
