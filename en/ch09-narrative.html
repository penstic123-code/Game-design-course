Chapter 9

Narrative Design

A game is not a “movie with controls”: the best stories are told with the player’s own hands.

Learning goals

about 50 min
  • Rank storytelling methods by “how much the player takes part,” and say why anything that can be told through play shouldn’t be told through a cutscene.
  • Explain what problems branching, merging and false choices appeared to solve, and build a structure with 4 endings within a fixed content budget.
  • Use causal chains, information gaps and the peak–end rule to check a piece of story: why it flows, why it makes people want the next chapter, and why the ending matters more than the middle.
1

What it isThe same “this world is poor,” told two ways

Suppose two games both want you to believe “this world is short on supplies.” The first opens with a two-minute narration, a hoarse voice telling how war ruined the granaries. The second says nothing at all and just gives you a 6-slot backpack.

Three hours later, you have probably forgotten the narrator’s lines, but you still remember that evening: you agonized between two weapons and three days of rations, and finally left the axe by the roadside. The second game let you feel scarcity, while the first only told you.

That is what is special about storytelling in games. Narrative is not only the lines written in a script: players use their hands, and every action of the hands can be a sentence. So the means of storytelling can be lined up from low to high by how much the player takes part.

🎬

Cutscene narrative

Control is taken away for a while, like watching a film. Good at emotion and big events; too long and it breaks the pacing.

🗣️

Embedded narrative

The story is “put into” the game: dialogue, logs, recordings, quest text. Players can read at their own pace.

🏚️

Environmental narrative

Scenes hint at what happened: an overturned chair, an unfinished meal, scratches on the wall. It can be told without a single word.

🎮

Gameplay narrative

The gameplay is itself the story: your actions, your choices and changes in the rules let players experience rather than be told.

Gameplay narrative: the 6-slot backpack. Nobody tells you “supplies are scarce,” but every time you pick something up you have to drop something else. You weren’t told; you are living it.
Environmental narrative: a room. An overturned chair, a smashed cup, claw marks on the door. Without a single line, you have worked out for yourself that there was a fight here and something wanted to get in.
Environmental narrative in daily life: a stranger’s room. Three empty coffee cups on the desk, sticky notes all over the wall. Without him saying a word, you read that he was up late last night rushing a deadline.
Counterexample: a ten-minute cutscene every ten steps. The story may be good, but the player has control taken away again and again, the controller lies on the floor, and the pacing is chopped to pieces. The cutscene itself isn’t wrong; the fault is using it too often.
Core principle: whatever can be told through gameplay, don’t tell through a cutscene; whatever the player can “do,” don’t make them “watch.” There is also a kind of story that nobody wrote at all: the rules sit there, and the players play out a legend themselves, like emergence. Simulation and sandbox games often have this “emergent narrative.”
2

What it isAgency: why doing it yourself weighs more than watching

A film viewer sits in the audience, and a game player stands on the stage. The scholar Janet Murray calls this feeling agency: the satisfying power of taking meaningful action and seeing that your decisions and choices have brought results. Note it has two halves: you did something, and the world responded. Without the second half, you are just pressing buttons.

Agency has an awkward flip side. When story and gameplay say different things, players trust their hands more. The designer Clint Hocking, analyzing BioShock on his blog in October 2007, named this phenomenon ludonarrative dissonance. The story celebrates selflessness, while the gameplay rewards you for sacrificing the “Little Sisters” for upgrade materials, and what players remember is the gameplay. The term was later overused, so we treat it only as a checking tool: does the action players repeat most in this game match the theme the story wants?

❌ Dissonant

Plot: the hero cherishes life and keeps saying “I don’t want to hurt anyone.”
Gameplay: the whole game runs on killing enemies, and the more you kill the more you’re rewarded.
What the player remembers is the gameplay, not the lines.

✔ Consonant

Plot: the hero is a lonely wanderer who trusts no one.
Gameplay: supplies suffice for one person only, and bringing a teammate stretches resources thinner.
The gameplay itself “acts out” this personality.

Dissonance: says no killing, kills nonstop. The hero has a halo and keeps saying “I don’t want to hurt anyone,” yet every enemy defeated pops up +100. However moving the lines, what the rewards point at is what players will do.
Consonance: a horror game with no weapon. The hero is unarmed, and the player really can only run and hide. The fear is built not by lines but by the rule “no weapons.”
Dissonance in daily life: “People first” on the wall. But the performance board honors whoever worked latest. Employees remember the board, not the slogan, the same structure as “says kindness, rewards killing.”
A near-miss: having combat is not dissonance. The plot says “cherish every life,” and in the gameplay sneaking past a guard (+200) pays more than a kill (+50), so the gameplay also encourages not killing, and there is no dissonance here. What counts is where the reward points, not whether there is combat.
Sources: Janet Murray, Hamlet on the Holodeck (1997) on agency; Clint Hocking, “Ludonarrative Dissonance in Bioshock,” Click Nothing blog, October 7, 2007; Henry Jenkins, “Game Design as Narrative Architecture” (2004), on how games tell stories through space and action.
3

What it isBranching narrative: four structures, four costs

Once players are allowed to choose, the story is no longer a line. But there is a practical problem: every extra branch multiplies the content to write and build. So designers gradually found several structures that keep costs in check (names vary a little between sources):

Linear

One road to the end. Easiest to control and the most powerful narratively, but players lack the feeling of “I changed the story.”

String of pearls

The main line is like a thread strung with a few optional side-quest “beads,” all returning to the main line. Costs stay manageable.

Branching tree

Every choice forks, and the endings explode in number. The most freedom and the highest cost; few ever build one in full.

Bottleneck (fork and merge)

Fork first, merge at a “bottleneck,” then fork again. The player feels they are choosing, while the designer writes only a limited amount of content.

Merging is not deceiving. What matters is whether the choice leaves a consequence: even if the plot merges, as long as the earlier choice changed relationships, resources or later lines, the player still feels “this is my story.”
String of pearls: a main line threaded with side quests. The main quest goes straight ahead, with a few optional side quests along the way, and finishing them leads back to the main line. Side quests enrich the world without throwing the main line into disorder.
Bottleneck: two routes, one big scene. Two routes each have a few unique scenes, then merge in the same big battle, and afterward split into different endings according to your earlier choices. The merged scene needs to be written only once.
A bottleneck in daily life: shopping at the supermarket. You may wander any of three shelf aisles, but all of them end up at the same checkout. The route is yours; the destination is the store’s.
Counterexample: every option leads to all-new plot. A novice team wanted every dialogue option to lead to a completely different story, and at the fourth layer found the content had multiplied several times over, so they had to cut a great deal. The final experience was more fragmented than a carefully designed bottleneck.
4

How it was inventedWhat forced branching and the three-act structure into being?

Story A: a page is a line, but the reader wants to decide for the hero

A printed book is linear by nature: after page 1 there is always page 2. But readers always want to interject: “What if he went left?” Literature had this thought early; Borges’s 1941 short story “The Garden of Forking Paths” imagined a world where time keeps forking. Still, it was only a story, and the reader couldn’t act.

The person who really made forking into a book you can flip through was Edward Packard. He worked out a way of writing that let the reader decide how the hero goes, and in 1969–1970 several big publishers rejected the manuscript. In 1976, Vermont Crossroads Press published his Sugarcane Island. In 1979, Bantam launched the “Choose Your Own Adventure” series, with the first volume his The Cave of Time.

Here the problem arrived: every extra choice the author offered meant writing twice as many pages. The pages of a book are fixed, and so is the budget. In the same period, text adventures on computers took another road, where players could type commands to steer the hero, but the ledger behind it was the same: every extra possibility meant writing another passage.

1976 Packard’s Sugarcane Island is published by Vermont Crossroads Press; at a fork the reader has to turn to a specified page number.
Mid to late 1970s Will Crowther writes Colossal Cave Adventure, which Don Woods expands and which spreads widely on mainframes, one of the starting points of text adventures. Then MIT students make Zork, which understands whole sentences, and in 1978 Scott Adams makes Adventureland on a home computer.
1979 Bantam makes this kind of book into a series, with every volume having to fit several routes and several endings into a fixed page count.
1982 Steve Jackson and Ian Livingstone’s The Warlock of Firetop Mountain is published by Puffin, starting the Fighting Fantasy solo gamebook series, with dice and combat added to the books.

So the key insight is plain: with a limited content budget, branching has to be controlled. Let branches merge later, or fork for real at only a few nodes, or use “false choices” that add no content to create a feeling of participation. (This is a summary of how these structures came about, not any book’s official design manual.) You might first do the arithmetic by hand.

Story B: was the three-act structure invented by Aristotle?

Guess first, then open

Answer: no. In the Poetics, Aristotle says only that a complete action has a beginning, a middle and an end; he did not lay down “three acts.” The popularity of the modern three-act structure owes more to Syd Field’s 1979 screenwriting textbook Screenplay.

Imagine you have to write a two-hour script of about 120 pages. By page 50 you start to panic: has the pace collapsed? Where should the turning point go? Nothing tells you “roughly what should be happening now.” A long story needs a locator.

The ancient Greeks were the first to notice that a complete story has a head and a tail (Aristotle’s Poetics), but he spoke only of the whole and said nothing about page counts. In 1863 the German writer Gustav Freytag drew a five-part scheme in Technique of the Drama: exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, catastrophe, the later “Freytag’s pyramid,” but it was drawn for stage plays. In 1949 Joseph Campbell, in The Hero with a Thousand Faces, drew from myths around the world the “hero’s journey”: leaving the ordinary, accepting the call, going through trials, the deepest crisis, and returning with a change.

Finally Field broke the film script into “setup, confrontation, resolution,” roughly 30 / 60 / 30 pages, with a plot point at the end of the first act and at the end of the second. This approach was easy to teach and easy to use, so it became the standard fare of screenwriting classes, and game people borrowed it in turn to pace levels and progression.

4th century BCE Aristotle’s Poetics: a complete plot has a beginning, a middle and an end.
1863 Freytag’s Technique of the Drama: a five-part scheme of exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, catastrophe.
1949 Campbell’s The Hero with a Thousand Faces: the “hero’s journey” drawn from myths, later simplified by screenwriters into five or six steps.
1979 Syd Field’s Screenplay: boils scripts down to three acts and popularizes the idea.

Don’t treat three acts as a formula; it is only a checklist later people drew up: does the story have ups and downs, and are the turns placed where readers are about to tire?

In a game: the three phases of a boss fight. Probing, escalation and a desperate counterattack are three acts in miniature.
In daily life: a football match. The cautious feeling-out at the start, the stalemate and the turn in midfield, a winning goal in the last ten minutes. The audience’s tension rises and falls just like that.
Counterexample: the biggest climax at the very start. The giant boss and the full-screen explosions are used up right away, nothing is left to upgrade afterward, and the player’s tension can only slide downhill.

Why it worksWhy do people naturally read stories “along cause and effect”?

Look at these four sentences first: she left without her keys, so she went back to the café, and the door was already locked, but the clerk held up a key to her through the glass. You understood it with no effort. Now shuffle the order of the sentences and you feel at once that it is awkward. The same information, just because the causes are broken, turns from a story back into scattered news.

Psychologists find that people seem to do this by nature: string events into causal chains, then fill in the characters’ intentions. In 1944, Fritz Heider and Marianne Simmel showed a short animation with only two triangles and a small circle moving in a box, and nearly all the participants told stories of chasing, fighting and hiding. The shapes have no intentions; your brain adds them.

Causal chains, narrative and memory

① Causal understanding: Trabasso and van den Broek’s 1985 study found that the more causal links an event has to those before and after, the easier readers remember it and the more they treat it as the backbone of the story.
② Stories help memory: In 1969 Gordon Bower and Michael Clark had one group weave 10 unrelated words into a story while another group just memorized them. On the later test, those who made up a story remembered far more words (the often-cited figures are medians of 93% versus 13%). Later replications don’t always get a gap this wide, but the direction, “remembered better when linked by cause,” hasn’t changed.
③ Narrative transportation: Green and Brock proposed in 2000 that when a reader is “carried into” a story and temporarily forgets their surroundings, they are more likely to accept the views in the story.

Sources: Heider & Simmel (1944), “An experimental study of apparent behavior,” American Journal of Psychology 57; Trabasso & van den Broek (1985), Journal of Memory and Language 24; Bower & Clark (1969), “Narrative stories as mediators for serial learning,” Psychonomic Science 14: 181–182; Green & Brock (2000), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 79. These conclusions mostly come from text and film; carrying them straight over to games that you both read and operate calls for caution.

A widely repeated saying among writers: two adjacent scenes in a good story should be connectable by “therefore” or “but,” not “and then… and then…” (this is a craft rule of thumb, often credited to the creators of South Park). The same holds in games. What the player did and how the world changed because of it must be visible; quest logs, plot recaps and changes in the scene all maintain this causal chain on the player’s behalf.

In a game: a quest log that states cause and effect. “Because the village was burned, you must find the blacksmith.” You don’t have to guess what to do next.
In daily life: a news report. “Because of the rainstorm, flights were canceled, so travelers were stranded” is easier to remember than three isolated items.
In daily life: a short film with only shapes. There is no one on screen, yet you still tell a story of “bullying” and “running away.” People automatically fill in cause and intention.
Counterexample: a string of “and then.” Take a quest, kill monsters, return to town, take another quest: with no “therefore” or “but” between the events, players can’t remember them and don’t care.

Why it worksWhy does “to be continued” make it so hard to stop?

You have surely had such a night: you were sleepy, the series stopped at the tensest moment, and you still pressed “next episode.” Because there were a few unanswered questions hanging in your head.

In 1994 the behavioral economist George Loewenstein proposed the information-gap theory: curiosity comes from “you know a little, and know that you don’t know.” If you know nothing, you have nothing to be curious about; if you almost understand it all, you are no longer curious either. A gap of the right size is the most tempting.

The human wish for definite answers and for an end to the state of suspense is called the need for cognitive closure. Popular accounts often put it together with the Zeigarnik effect, but the original version of that effect, “unfinished tasks are remembered better,” has not replicated consistently. What stands up better is that unfinished goals keep occupying thoughts, and that making a plan for finishing eases that nagging. So crediting all the magic of suspense to Zeigarnik is an oversimplification.

In a game: the door that won’t open. At the end of a level a locked great door appears, with light seeping through the crack. Carrying the question “what’s inside?”, the player is willing to play another level.
In daily life: the next-episode trailer. The trailer of a mystery series cuts out the most crucial second, giving only the question and no answer, and so at half past midnight you press the next episode again.
In daily life: a friend who says half a sentence. “I’ll tell you something… never mind, another time.” That half sentence can keep you hanging for a whole day.
Counterexample: a “shocking secret” that never pays off. The plot hints again and again at a great secret, hangs it out for a dozen chapters, and at the end gives only “it’s a mystery.” The reader’s curiosity turns into anger at being played.

Why the ending matters more than the middle: the peak–end rule

Remember the peak–end rule from the flow chapter? Applied to narrative, it gives a conclusion designers find rather uncomfortable: when readers recall an experience, they remember almost only the most intense moment and the last moment, and how long it lasted tends to be ignored.

The peak–end rule in narrative

In 1993, Kahneman and colleagues had participants hold a hand in 14 °C cold water for 60 seconds; in another trial they held it for 60 seconds and then 30 seconds more, during which the water was quietly warmed a little (to about 15 °C). Afterward, asked which one they would repeat, most chose the longer one, because it didn’t end so unpleasantly. In 1996, Redelmeier and Kahneman saw a similar pattern in colonoscopy patients: later ratings followed mainly the most painful moment and the last moment.

It is about evaluation in memory, not a claim that the process is unimportant: if the process is too awful, players quit on the spot. The research is also mostly about pain and short films, so applied to games of dozens of hours it can only be an analogy. But it is enough to remind you: put the hook where the reader is about to leave, and take care with the ending.

Sources: Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber & Redelmeier (1993), “When more pain is preferred to less,” Psychological Science 4(6): 401–405; Redelmeier & Kahneman (1996), Pain 66(1): 3–8.

Are false choices useless? Not necessarily. They let players “act out” a character’s personality and can also make them feel respected. The real problem is packaging a false choice as a fate-changing event; once seen through, players feel cheated, which is the agency loop breaking at “consequence visible.”

Common misconceptions

  • “The longer the story, the deeper.” Length is not depth. What matters is clear causes, rising and falling tension, and gameplay matching the theme.
  • “More branches mean more agency.” Agency comes from visible consequences, not the number of forks.
  • “Suspense means hanging without answering.” Suspense that isn’t paid off turns into frustration. The gap has to be filled, ideally better than imagined.
  • “The three-act structure is a rule laid down by Aristotle.” It is a summary by later people, especially twentieth-century screenwriting textbooks, and only a checklist.

Reflection and debate

  • Is a game a narrative at all? Scholars long argued: the narratologists see games as a new medium for storytelling, while the ludologists (who stress rules and play) hold that looking at games through a narrative frame misses their essence. There is still no conclusion, and both sides have a point.
  • “People naturally love stories.” The understanding of cause and intention is backed by a great deal of research, but “story is an adaptation human beings evolved” is only one view.
  • The experimental material is mostly text and film: findings on narrative transportation, causal understanding and the peak–end rule won’t necessarily carry over unchanged to players who both read and operate.
  • The limits of the hook: using suspense to lengthen playtime may be good design, or may turn into a way of “not letting people sleep”; every designer has to draw that line themselves.

Key points

  • Narrative means ranked by involvement: cutscene < embedded < environmental < gameplay narrative. Prefer letting players “do” over “watch.”
  • Agency is “action + visible consequence”; when what the plot says and what the gameplay encourages disagree, that is ludonarrative dissonance.
  • Content cost gave rise to the string of pearls, the bottleneck and false choices in branching narrative; consequences matter more than the number of forks.
  • Three acts are a “locator” for long stories, a tool later people summed up, not a rule from the ancients.
  • People understand stories through causal chains; suspense comes from information gaps and has to be paid off; what readers remember deepest are the strongest moment and the last moment.
Good game narrative: clear cause and effect, a moderate measure of suspense, the player making the story with their own hands, and the ending kept for the most unforgettable moment.
