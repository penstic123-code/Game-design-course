Chapter 7

Feedback & Game Feel

Same rules, so why do some games feel great to hit and others feel like paper?

Learning goals

about 40 min
  • Say what “game feel” and “juice” each refer to, and explain why developers had to coin these two words.
  • After reading, you can explain why feedback has to be fast, and why “about 100 milliseconds” is only a rule of thumb.
  • After reading, you can tell the three kinds of easing apart, scale effect strength in multiples rather than equal steps, and explain why the same effect fades when repeated and how to rescue it.
1

What it isGame feel: an exaggerated response to every action

Take two versions of Breakout with the same ball, paddle and bricks, and not one rule different. The first plays like flipping through slides: the ball touches a brick, the brick vanishes, the number goes up by one, and that is all. In the second, the ball hits a brick with a “pop,” the brick shudders and then breaks into pieces, and the paddle squashes a little when it catches the ball.

It is hard to say what is better, only that the second one has some punch. Developers call that punch game feel. The small effects inside it that change no rules and make an action satisfying purely through instant, exaggerated response have a vivid name: juice.

When the player does something, the game has to answer, and the answer has to be fast, obvious and weighty. Without that answer, a button is just a toggle switch.

Concept card

The sense of how tight and punchy the game is, in the gap between the player’s input and the game’s response. With the rules unchanged, a response that is fast, obvious and weighty gives good game feel.

ExampleBreakout: the ball hits a brick, the brick shudders and breaks into pieces, the paddle squashes a little. That has far more punch than “the brick disappears and the number goes up by one.”

Eating coins in a row: the “ding” rises in pitch each time and the character gives a little hop, so the player knows at once they are scoring in a row. The rules haven’t changed; the response just says one more sentence.
The elevator button’s light: when it lights up after you press, you stand and wait at ease. If it doesn’t light, you can’t help pressing again. That extra press is the symptom of missing feedback.
A flashy explosion, half a second late: you press fire, and the big explosion comes half a second later. However gorgeous the picture, the control still feels “mushy.” No amount of juice rescues a sluggish control.

How it was inventedWhy give “game feel” a name?

Two games with the same rules play completely differently

Imagine a developer on a small team who made a Breakout with a friend, with identical rules. The friend’s version was clearly more fun. He asks: “What did you actually change?” The friend scratches his head: “Just… it feels better?”

For a long time this question had no good answer. “Fun” was treated as a kind of mysticism. Either it was left for the artists and sound designers to paint on at the end, or nobody noticed a problem until players complained that it felt “mushy,” “floaty” or “flat.” Without shared words, there is no way to diagnose it, let alone teach it to newcomers.

The turning point came from a plain idea: game feel can be taken apart, named, stacked on one item at a time and tested. The animation industry had done just this long ago, and human–computer interaction had accumulated rule-of-thumb numbers for “how long counts as immediate.” What game developers needed was to join these two lines.

From the 1930s Disney’s animators worked out “squash and stretch.” A ball flattens when it lands and elongates when it bounces up, and the object at once seems to have weight and spring. In 1981, Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston gathered these methods into 12 principles in The Illusion of Life.
1968 / 1993 Robert B. Miller discusses in 1968 how fast a computer should respond in a conversation with a person; in 1993 Jakob Nielsen’s Usability Engineering sums up this kind of experience in three limits: 0.1 seconds, 1 second and 10 seconds.
2008 Steve Swink published Game Feel: A Game Designer’s Guide to Virtual Sensation (Morgan Kaufmann; some catalogues list it as 2009). The book breaks game feel into six aspects: input, response, context, polish, metaphor and rules. For the first time the games world had a systematic vocabulary.
2012 Martin Jonasson and Petri Purho gave the talk “Juice it or lose it” at Nordic Game in May, and again later that year at GDC Europe. They took a Breakout clone and, live, layered on easing, squash and stretch, screen shake, particles and sound one at a time. The rules never moved, yet the experience was completely different.
2013 Jan Willem Nijman of Vlambeer gave the talk “The Art of Screenshake” at INDIGO Classes, turning details like “screen shake” into a checklist anyone could follow. Every addition made the game punchier.

With these in hand, “game feel” and “juice” went from jargon to tools people could discuss. The word “juice” was already circulating among developers before those talks, and its earliest source is hard to trace now.

Why it worksWhy feedback has to be fast: the sense of causality and the perceptual time window

You press the switch and the light comes on, and you never wonder whether that press was yours. But if the light takes half a second, you will probably press again without thinking, or even suspect it is broken.

In a game, “button → reaction” is the same thing. Once feedback drags on too long, the player stops feeling “I made that happen,” and the control goes “mushy.”

The brain binds “what I did” to “what happened,” but only within a very narrow time window

In the mid-20th century the Belgian psychologist Albert Michotte ran a famous series of experiments. On a screen, one square moves into another, and the second starts moving at once. People see it as “the first one knocked it away.” If the second one starts a little later, the impression of a collision disappears, and the two become separate things each moving on their own. The sense of cause needs the two events to sit very close together.

In 2002, Patrick Haggard and colleagues found a related phenomenon. When a key press is made voluntarily, people feel the press and the tone that follows are closer together in time than they really are. This is called “intentional binding.” The feeling of “I did this” itself pulls the action and its result together. (How to explain and measure it is still debated.)

The best-known numbers in human–computer interaction come from Miller (1968) and Nielsen (1993): within about 0.1 seconds a response feels “instant”; within about 1 second a train of thought is not interrupted; by about 10 seconds attention is near its limit. The broadcast industry’s lip-sync recommendation, ITU-R BT.1359, gives numbers of a similar scale. Audience members start to notice when sound leads the picture by about 45 milliseconds or lags it by about 125 (these thresholds come from clips of a newsreader speaking).

Large-scale measurements show that adults pressing a key at a sudden light take a little over 230 milliseconds on average. There is no way to “react” within 100 milliseconds, yet people can tell that action and result are 100 milliseconds apart. So how fast something feels depends on whether the brain has linked “what I did” with “what happened,” and has nothing to do with reaction speed.

100 milliseconds is a common rule of thumb, not a law of physics. Rhythm games and fighting games are very sensitive to differences of a few tens of milliseconds, while clicking a menu is far more forgiving. The delays of screens, sound and input devices also add up layer by layer.

Sources:

Haggard, Clark and Kalogeras · 2002 · measured perceived timing of voluntary key presses and tones · intentional binding (Nature Neuroscience 5: 382–385).

Why it worksPredictive processing and easing: why a bit of exaggeration actually feels right

You pick up a cup you thought was full of water, and it is empty, and your hand “floats” a little, which feels odd. The hand had prepared its strength for a cup of water in advance, and when reality and the preparation don’t match, the gap gives a start.

Conversely, if the thing’s reaction matches your intention and has a touch of “overdoing it,” you feel you are in control of it. A thing that shoots out with one nudge and then stops steadily just feels good in the hand.

The brain is always predicting, and an easing function is a tool for drawing “expectation” as a curve

Predictive processing (also called predictive coding) holds that the brain keeps predicting what it will feel next and corrects itself with the “prediction error.” Receiving sensory signals is only half of the job. It is in the same family of ideas as the prediction error discussed under the core loop.

Borrowed to explain game feel: when the response matches the intent of an action, it feels like “it listens to me.” Add a little exaggeration, so the result is slightly more “punchy” than expected, and the action feels weightier still.

To keep this exaggeration under control, use an easing function, which decides how the speed of an action changes over time. Linear is constant speed, like a machine. Ease-out is fast from the start and slows as it nears the target. Overshoot with rebound (overshoot) shoots past the target and springs back. What animators call squash and stretch is the same line of thought.

Predictive processing is influential in cognitive science, and also disputed. “Exaggeration equals a sense of control” is an analogy borrowed by game designers and has not been rigorously proven.

Easing equations are widely used in animation and interaction, and are often traced to a set of formulas collected by Robert Penner.
Thomas and Johnston · 1981 · The Illusion of Life · summed up animation principles such as squash and stretch.

Why it worksThe nonlinearity of perception: why effect strength should be designed in proportion

Light a candle in a pitch-dark room and you notice at once. Light one more under the midday sun and nothing changes. Both times it is “the brightness of one more candle,” and the feelings are worlds apart.

This matters a lot to people who make effects. Change the screen-shake amplitude from 2 pixels to 4 and the player sees at a glance it got stronger. Change it from 20 to 22, also 2 pixels more, and almost nobody can tell.

The reason is that people’s sensitivity to change depends on the proportion of the change to the original intensity.

The Weber–Fechner law (short version)

In the 19th century the German physiologist Ernst Weber measured the smallest change a person can notice (the just-noticeable difference) and found that it is roughly proportional to the strength of the original stimulus. This is Weber’s law.

Gustav Fechner went a step further in 1860, proposing that perceived intensity is roughly proportional to the logarithm of stimulus intensity.

Advanced: as a formula

Weber’s law: ΔI / I ≈ a constant. I is the original stimulus intensity, and ΔI is the smallest change that can just be noticed. Suppose the ratio is 50% (the number is only for illustration): if screen shake starts at 2 pixels, adding 1 pixel is noticeable; if it starts at 20 pixels, you must add 10 pixels before anyone notices.

The lesson for design is that the “levels” of an effect, a volume or a vibration should rise by multiples, not by a fixed amount per level. Going from 2 to 4 is a doubling, visible at a glance. Going from 20 to 22 is only 10% more, and goes unseen.

It is an approximate law, and it breaks down at very weak or very strong stimuli. S. S. Stevens proposed in 1957 describing sensation with a power function, which many consider a better fit. Both are explanations, not formulas to copy.

A phone brightness slider: going from 5% to 10% is a huge difference, from 85% to 90% almost unfelt. Volume settings being divided in decibels rather than linearly is the same reason.
Damage numbers two sizes bigger per tier: size 20, 22, 24, and nobody can tell them apart. The ultimate looks no different from a basic attack.

Why it worksHedonic adaptation: why the same effect loses its charm by the hundredth pickup

Remember the “ding” the first time you picked up a coin? Now think of the hundredth time. The sound is identical, the picture identical, and yet you feel nothing at all.

This is a trouble for game-feel design. You labor to build a lovely set of feedback, and after half an hour the player is “immune” to it. Adding more only leads to an ever more expensive arms race.

This has nothing to do with how well the feedback is made. The human nervous system is simply built this way.

Habituation and hedonic adaptation: repeated stimuli fade, and the baseline moves

At the lowest level is habituation: for a stimulus that keeps repeating without consequence, the neural response grows weaker and weaker. In 1966 Richard Thompson and William Spencer summarized this family of phenomena as a set of regularities. They include “the more a stimulus repeats, the weaker the response” and “present a new stimulus and the response comes back.”

One level up is psychological hedonic adaptation. In 1971 Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell offered the metaphor of the “hedonic treadmill”: however fast you run, your baseline of happiness moves forward with you. In 1978 Brickman and colleagues surveyed Illinois lottery winners and accident victims who had been left disabled. A few months on, both groups’ happiness had moved toward that of a comparison group, which became the most-cited evidence.

That conclusion was later revised. Using large German and British panel data, Richard Lucas (2007) showed that for major events such as unemployment or disability, adaptation is incomplete. People cannot get used to everything. Small repeated stimuli do fade.

For the designer, the answer is not to pile on more. Give feedback variation (a slightly different pitch, a slightly different spark each time), and make big events much bigger than small ones (the multiple-based levels of the previous section). Let the player rest now and then, and the thrill returns.

Common misconceptions

  • “The more effects, the better the feel.” Too many effects hide information and cause discomfort. What matters is that feedback is instant, weighty and proportional to the importance of the event.
  • “Game feel is only the artists’ and sound designers’ job.” Input latency, frame rate, how forgiving the hit detection is, and the easing curve of an action are just as critical.
  • “100 milliseconds is an iron law.” It is a common rule-of-thumb threshold that varies with the task, the device and the player.
  • “Easing only makes animation look nice.” It directly changes the sense of weight an action gives and how responsive it feels.
  • “If the effects aren’t enough, add more.” Repeated stimuli fade by nature, and adding more only costs more; leaving room for variation and spreading out the tiers works better.
  • “Finish the content first, add juice last.” Game feel is the foundation of what it is like to play. Adding it late means changing a lot of code and numbers, so many teams get it right at the prototype stage.

Reflection and debate

“Feedback must be fast” has limits too. To convey weight, hit stop deliberately slows the picture down a little, and some games use delay on purpose to create tension or a sense of charging up. A more accurate rule: the player’s input should be confirmed instantly; the pacing of what follows can be designed.

The predictive-processing account is an analogy borrowed by designers. The Weber–Fechner law holds only approximately around medium intensities, and the research on hedonic adaptation is still being revised. These are explanations that help you think, not formulas to copy.

Intense shaking and flashing may make some players uncomfortable, so provide options such as “reduce motion.” In games you’ve played, was there ever a moment when “the effects were gorgeous and yet not satisfying at all”? What was missing?

Key points

  • Game feel comes from immediate, visible, exaggerated feedback: sound, animation, shake, particles, numbers.
  • Words like “game feel” and “juice” appeared so that a difference that was hard to put into words could be named, broken apart and tested item by item.
  • Feedback has to be fast: about 100 milliseconds is a common rule-of-thumb threshold, and the longer the gap between action and result, the weaker the sense of causality.
  • Easing functions determine the “weight” of an action: linear is like a machine, ease-out is crisper, and overshoot is springier.
  • Feedback should be proportional to the importance of the event: small actions get small feedback, big events get big feedback, with tiers spread by multiples.
  • The same stimulus fades with repetition, and the rescue is leaving variation and widening the tiers, since you can’t raise the stakes forever. Don’t overdo it, and also provide “reduce motion.”
Game feel = causality that is fast enough, plus responses “exaggerated” by easing, plus intensity tiers that are spread in proportion and still leave room for variation.
