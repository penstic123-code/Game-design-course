Chapter 7

Feedback & Game Feel

Same rules, so why do some games feel great to hit and others feel like paper?

Learning goals

about 40 min
  • Say what “game feel” and “juice” each mean, and explain why developers had to invent these two words.
  • Explain why feedback has to be fast, and why “about 100 milliseconds” is only a rule of thumb, not an iron law.
  • Tell three kinds of easing apart, set effect intensity tiers by multiples rather than equal steps, and explain why the same effect fades after many repeats and how to rescue it.
1

What it isGame feel: an exaggerated response to every action

Picture two versions of Breakout. Ball, paddle, bricks, with not one word of difference in the rules. Playing the first is like flipping through slides: the ball touches a brick, the brick vanishes, the number goes up by one, and that’s it. In the second, the ball hits a brick and bounces away with a “pop,” the brick shakes before shattering into pieces, and the paddle is squashed down just a little when it catches the ball.

You can’t say what exactly is better, only that the second one has “oomph.” In the industry that oomph is called game feel. The small effects that don’t change the rules and make actions feel great purely through immediate, exaggerated responses have a vivid name: juice.

In the end it comes down to one sentence: the player does something, the game has to respond, and the response has to be fast enough, visible enough and weighty enough. Without that response, a button is just a toggle.

Collecting coins in a row: the “ding” goes up in pitch a little each time and the character gives a small hop, so you know at once you are scoring in a streak. The rules haven’t changed; the response just says one more thing.
The light on an elevator button: once it’s pressed and the light comes on, you stand and wait at ease; if the light doesn’t come on, you can’t help pressing again. That extra press is the symptom of missing feedback.
A dazzling explosion, half a second late: you press fire and the big blast comes half a second later. However gorgeous the picture, the controls still feel “soft.” Juice can’t save sluggishness.

How it was inventedWhy give “game feel” a name?

Two games with the same rules play completely differently

Imagine you are a developer on a small team, and you and a friend each made a Breakout with exactly the same rules. His version is clearly more fun. You ask him: “What did you actually change?” He scratches his head: “It’s just… more satisfying?”

For a long time this question had no good answer. “Satisfying” was treated as a kind of mysticism: either left for the artists and sound designers to add at the end, or noticed only when players complained about “soft,” “floaty,” “no oomph.” Without shared words you can’t troubleshoot, much less teach newcomers.

The turning point was a plain idea: game feel can be broken apart, named, and layered on one item at a time while testing. The animation industry had done this long before, and human–computer interaction had gathered rule-of-thumb numbers for “how fast counts as immediate”; what game developers had to do was join the two lines.

From the 1930s Disney animators work out “squash and stretch”: a ball is flattened as it lands and stretched as it bounces up, and at once the object seems to have weight and spring. In 1981, Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston sum these methods up in 12 principles in The Illusion of Life.
1968 / 1993 Robert B. Miller discusses in 1968 how fast a computer should respond in a conversation with a person; in 1993 Jakob Nielsen’s Usability Engineering sums up this kind of experience in three limits: 0.1 seconds, 1 second and 10 seconds.
2008 Steve Swink publishes Game Feel: A Game Designer’s Guide to Virtual Sensation (Morgan Kaufmann; some catalogs list it as 2009). The book analyzes game feel in six aspects, input, response, context, polish, metaphor and rules, and for the first time the games field has a systematic vocabulary.
2012 Martin Jonasson and Petri Purho give the talk “Juice it or lose it” at GDC Europe: with a Breakout clone they layer on easing, squash and stretch, screen shake, particles and sound one by one, live, with the rules unmoved yet the experience completely different. Reportedly an earlier version was given at the Nordic Game Jam in May the same year.
Around 2013 Jan Willem Nijman of Vlambeer gives “The Art of Screenshake,” turning details like “screen shake” into a checklist you can follow: every item added makes the game hit harder. (I couldn’t verify the exact occasion of the talk.)

Taken together, these are what turned “game feel” and “juice” from a bit of jargon into tools you can discuss. Incidentally, the word “juice” was already circulating among developers before these talks; I couldn’t find its earliest source, so no conclusion is drawn here.

Why it worksWhy feedback has to be fast: the sense of causality and the perceptual time window

You flip a switch and the light comes on. You never doubt “was that me who just pressed it?” But if the light came on half a second late, you would probably press again without thinking, or even suspect it was broken.

“Button → reaction” in a game is the same thing. Once feedback drags on too long, the player no longer feels “I made that happen,” and the controls turn “soft.” So how fast is fast?

The brain binds “what I did” to “what happened,” but only within a very narrow time window

In the mid-twentieth century the Belgian psychologist Albert Michotte ran a classic set of experiments: on a screen one square hits another and the second one immediately moves, and people see it as “the first one knocked it away”; if the second one moves a little later, the impression of a “hit” disappears and it becomes two things each moving on its own. A sense of causality needs two events to be very close together.

In 2002, Patrick Haggard and colleagues found a related phenomenon: when a key press is something you actively do, you feel the press and the sound that follows to be closer together than they really are, called “intentional binding.” In other words, the feeling of “I did it” itself pulls the action and its result together. (How to explain and measure it is still debated.)

The most widely circulated numbers in human–computer interaction come from Miller (1968) and Nielsen (1993): within about 0.1 seconds people feel it is “immediate”; within about 1 second their train of thought isn’t interrupted; and around 10 seconds attention is near its limit. The broadcast industry’s ITU-R BT.1359 recommendation for lip sync gives numbers of a similar order: audience members start to notice when the sound leads the picture by about 45 milliseconds or lags it by about 125 (the figures come from secondhand accounts of that recommendation, not checked against the original, and were measured on news-broadcast footage).

Here is something interesting. Large-scale measurements show that adults take a little over 230 milliseconds on average to press a key in response to a suddenly appearing dot of light. You simply can’t “react” within 100 milliseconds, yet you can notice a 100-millisecond gap between your action and its result. So how fast it feels is not a matter of reaction speed, but of whether the brain has joined “what I did” to “what happened.”

100 milliseconds is a common rule of thumb, not a law of physics. Rhythm games and fighting games are sensitive to differences of tens of milliseconds, while clicking a menu is much more forgiving; and latency from the screen, the audio and the input device stacks up layer by layer.

Sources: Albert Michotte, La perception de la causalité (1946; English translation The Perception of Causality, 1963); Haggard, Clark & Kalogeras, “Voluntary action and conscious awareness,” Nature Neuroscience 5: 382–385 (2002); Miller, AFIPS Fall Joint Computer Conference (1968); Nielsen, Usability Engineering (1993; these numbers have also been criticized as not coming from rigorous experiments); Woods et al., “Factors influencing the latency of simple reaction time,” Frontiers in Human Neuroscience (2015), 1,469 adults aged 18–65, with visual simple reaction time averaging about 231 ms (about 213 ms after subtracting device latency).

Why it worksPredictive processing and easing: why a bit of exaggeration actually feels right

You pick up a cup you thought was full, and it’s empty, and your hand “floats” and feels odd. Your hand had prepared the strength for a cup of water in advance, and the mismatch between reality and preparation gives you a small jolt.

Conversely, if something’s reaction matches your intention and has a touch of “overdoing it,” you feel you are in control of it. Something that shoots off at a push and then stops steadily just feels good.

There is a family of theories in cognitive science that can be borrowed to explain this.

The brain is always predicting, and an easing function is a tool for drawing “expectation” as a curve

Predictive processing (predictive coding) holds that the brain does not passively receive sensory signals but constantly predicts what it will feel next and corrects itself with “prediction error.” It is the same family of ideas as the prediction error in the core-loop chapter. Borrowed to explain game feel: when the response matches your intention you feel “it listens to me”; add a bit more exaggeration, so the result is slightly more “forceful” than expected, and it often feels more weighty.

To keep this exaggeration under control, use an easing function: it decides how the speed of an action changes over time. Linear is constant speed, like a machine; ease-out starts fast and slows as it gets there; overshoot-and-rebound (overshoot) shoots past the target and bounces back. What animators call squash and stretch is really the same idea.

Predictive processing is an influential but still contested framework in cognitive science; “exaggeration equals a sense of control” is an analogy and rule of thumb borrowed by game designers, not a rigorously proven theorem.

Sources: studies of predictive coding often cite Rao & Ballard (1999), and Karl Friston and Andy Clark (2013) wrote reviews; easing equations are widely used in animation and interaction, often traced to a set of formulas compiled by Robert Penner; for squash and stretch see Thomas & Johnston (1981).

Why it worksThe nonlinearity of perception: why effect strength should be designed in proportion

Light a candle in a pitch-dark room and you notice at once; light another in the noon sun and nothing changes. Both are “the brightness of one more candle,” yet the feeling is worlds apart.

This matters a lot to people who make effects. Raise the screen shake from 2 pixels to 4 and the player sees at a glance that it got stronger; raise it from 20 to 22, the same 2 pixels more, and hardly anyone can tell.

The reason is that people’s sensitivity to change depends on the proportion of the change to the original intensity.

The Weber–Fechner law (short version)

In the nineteenth century the German physiologist Ernst Weber measured the smallest change people can detect (the just-noticeable difference) and found it roughly proportional to the original stimulus intensity. This is Weber’s law: ΔI / I ≈ constant. In 1860 Gustav Fechner went further and proposed that the perceived intensity is roughly proportional to the logarithm of the stimulus intensity.

The lesson for design is that the “tiers” of effects, volume and vibration should grow by multiples, not by adding a fixed amount each tier. From 2 to 4 is a doubling, visible at a glance; from 20 to 22 is only 10% more, often invisible.

It is only an approximation: it deviates at very weak or very strong stimuli; S. S. Stevens in 1957 also proposed describing sensation with a power function, which many think fits better. Both are “explanations,” not formulas for you to copy.

Sources: Ernst Weber (nineteenth-century sensory research); Gustav Fechner, Elemente der Psychophysik (1860); S. S. Stevens, “On the psychophysical law,” Psychological Review 64: 153–181 (1957).
Three tiers of screen shake: a basic attack shakes 2 pixels, a heavy hit 8, a critical 16. Players don’t need a hint; one shake tells them which kind of event happened.
A phone brightness slider: going from 5% to 10% is a huge difference, while from 85% to 90% is almost unnoticeable. Volume settings being scaled in decibels rather than linearly is the same principle.
Damage numbers two sizes bigger per tier: size 20, 22, 24, and nobody can tell them apart. The ultimate looks no different from a basic attack.

Why it worksHedonic adaptation: why the same effect loses its charm by the hundredth pickup

Do you remember the “ding” of the first coin you picked up? Now think of the hundredth. The sound is exactly the same and the picture exactly the same, but you feel nothing at all.

This is a problem for game feel design. You worked hard on a lovely set of feedback, and after half an hour of play players have become “immune” to it. If you pile on more, you just fall into an increasingly expensive arms race.

This isn’t because your feedback was poor; it is simply how the human nervous system works.

Habituation and hedonic adaptation: repeated stimuli fade, and the baseline moves

At the lowest level is habituation: for stimuli that keep recurring with no consequence, the neural response gets weaker and weaker. In 1966, Richard Thompson and William Spencer summarized this family of phenomena as a set of regularities, including “the more a stimulus is repeated, the weaker the response” and “switch to a new stimulus and the response comes back.”

One level up is psychological hedonic adaptation. In 1971, Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell offered the “hedonic treadmill” metaphor: however fast we run, the baseline of happiness moves forward with us. In 1978 Brickman and colleagues surveyed Illinois lottery winners and people disabled in accidents and found that after a few months the happiness of both groups had moved toward that of a control group, which became the most often cited evidence.

But this conclusion was later revised. Richard Lucas in 2007, using large longitudinal data from Germany and Britain, pointed out that for major events such as unemployment and disability, adaptation is often incomplete. So don’t treat it as an iron law of “people get used to anything”; the safer statement is: small repeated stimuli certainly fade.

The lesson for designers: don’t brute-force with more. Leave room for variation in the feedback (the pitch and the look of the sparks differ a little each time), make big events far bigger than small ones (the multiple tiers from the previous section), and let players rest now and then, and the satisfaction will return.

Sources: Thompson & Spencer, “Habituation: A model phenomenon for the study of neuronal substrates of behavior,” Psychological Review 73(1): 16–43 (1966); Brickman & Campbell, “Hedonic relativism and planning the good society,” in M. H. Common misconceptions

  • “The more effects, the better the feel.” No: too much covers up information and causes discomfort; the point is to be immediate, weighty and in proportion to how important the event is.
  • “Game feel is only the artists’ and sound designers’ job.” Input latency, frame rate, how forgiving the hit detection is, and the easing curve of an action are just as critical.
  • “100 milliseconds is an iron law.” It is a common rule-of-thumb threshold that varies with the task, the device and the player.
  • “Easing only makes animation look nice.” It directly changes the sense of weight an action gives and how responsive it feels.
  • “If the effects aren’t enough, add more.” Repeated stimuli fade by nature, and adding more only costs more; leaving room for variation and spreading out the tiers works better.
  • “Finish the content first and add juice last.” Game feel is the foundation of “playing,” and adding it late often means changing a lot of code and numbers; many teams get it right at the prototype stage.

Reflection and debate

“Feedback should be fast” has its limits too: to show weight, hit stop is precisely making the picture a little slower on purpose, and some games deliberately use delay to create tension or a sense of charging up. So a more accurate statement is: the player’s action must be acknowledged immediately, and after that the pacing of the presentation can be designed.

The predictive-processing account is an analogy borrowed by designers; the Weber–Fechner law holds only approximately around medium intensities; and research on hedonic adaptation is being revised too. These are explanations to help you think, not formulas to copy.

Also, intense shaking and flashing can make some players uncomfortable, so options such as “reduce motion” are needed. In a game you have played, was there a moment when “the effects were gorgeous, but it wasn’t satisfying at all”? What was missing?

Key points

  • Game feel comes from immediate, visible, exaggerated feedback: sound, animation, shake, particles, numbers.
  • Words like “game feel” and “juice” appeared so that a difference that was hard to put into words could be named, broken apart and tested item by item.
  • Feedback has to be fast: about 100 milliseconds is a common rule-of-thumb threshold, and the longer the gap between action and result, the weaker the sense of causality.
  • Easing functions determine the “weight” of an action: linear is like a machine, ease-out is crisper, and overshoot is springier.
  • Feedback should be proportional to the importance of the event: small actions get small feedback, big events get big feedback, with tiers spread by multiples.
  • The same stimulus fades with repetition: rescue it with variation and spread-out tiers, not endless piling on; don’t overdo it, and provide a “reduce motion” option.
Game feel = causality that is fast enough, plus responses “exaggerated” by easing, plus intensity tiers that are spread in proportion and still leave room for variation.
