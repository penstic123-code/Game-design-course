Chapter 8

Meaningful Choices

A game is a series of decisions. What kind of decision is worth making the player stop and think?

Learning goals

about 45 min
  • Check with “information, trade-offs, consequences” whether a choice is meaningful, and root out “dominated options” and “always-best options.”
  • Explain why a coin-toss game of 1713 ran the “just count the expected value” approach into a wall, and how later people went step by step to utility and prospect theory.
  • Explain why repeated options lose value and why losing points hurts more than gaining them, and use that to build a set of options with pros and cons and a moderate number.
1

What it isWhat makes a choice “meaningful”?

Chapter 2 said the core loop turns because the player makes a decision every lap. But decisions come real and fake. Walk into the game’s apothecary, and on the shelf stand a bottle of red potion and a bottle of blue. Red heals 50, blue heals 50, same price, and only the bottle color differs. You grab the red one.

Strictly speaking, no decision happens here at all: whichever bottle is taken, the result is the same, and nothing makes the player think for even a second. In game design an option like this is a false choice.

A fork in the road is different. The short way has a guard, the long way is safe but misses a treasure chest, and the player has to think about what they need more right now.

A fork has three ingredients. The player has to know where each road leads, otherwise they are only guessing. Each road has to give something and cost something, otherwise there is nothing to think about. And after choosing, the situation has to be truly different, in a way the player can see, otherwise the choice was wasted.

👀

Information

The player knows what the options are and can judge.

⚖️

Trade-off

Each option gains and loses something; none is “always best.”

🌱

Consequence

The choice affects the situation afterward, and the player notices.

A line often quoted in design circles is “A game is a series of interesting decisions.” It is usually attributed to Sid Meier, designer of the Civilization series, though the exact source is unclear. He looked back on and expanded the idea in his GDC 2012 talk “Interesting Decisions.”

Short path or long path: the short path has guards, and the long path is safe but skips a chest. The player can see what each path costs and knows what choosing will do, so they have to think hard: do I lack health or money more right now?
Two cards in a card game: one cheap but weak, one expensive but strong. Which goes into the deck depends on how the whole deck wants to play, not on which is “inherently better.”
Renting a flat: the one near work is dear, and the one farther off is cheaper but costs forty more minutes of subway each day. None wins on every count, and that is why people hesitate over a calculator.
Red potion and blue potion: the same healing, the same price, just a different color. Whichever you take is the same, so there is no decision here, only an act of “picking.”
A near-miss misjudgment: large, medium and small health potions: the small one gives 30 for 10 coins, the medium 60 for 15, the large 100 for 20, the bigger the dearer. They are not dominated: cost and benefit change together, still a normal trade-off. To judge domination, look at benefit and cost together, not at one number.
2

What it isHow to “see” a dominated option

The iron-sword and steel-sword pair makes the flaw easy to see, but with many options the flaw hides. One way is to plot each option on a “cost–benefit” plane: the horizontal axis is costs like price and cooldown, and the vertical axis benefits like damage and healing.

Once drawn, the rule is intuitive: the whole area to the lower right of an option (higher cost, lower benefit) is the ground it dominates. An option that falls in has no reason to exist. Meaningful options each hold a different place, such as lower left (cheap but weak) and upper right (dear but strong), joined into a frontier of trade-offs.

The chart also explains “always best”: if one option presses all the others into its lower right, the others are only decoration, and the player’s “choosing” collapses back into the red-and-blue potion state.

3

How it was inventedThe St. Petersburg game: why “calculate the expected value” isn’t enough

A bet “worth infinite money in theory” that nobody wants to buy

Faced with a trade-off, people’s first reaction is to count: multiply each outcome’s money by its probability and add up to get the expected value, and pick the larger. This is how seventeenth-century mathematicians handled gambling. It worked well, and never ran into trouble.

On September 9, 1713, Nicolaus Bernoulli, in a letter to Pierre Rémond de Montmort, tossed out a game that made this method look foolish. The rules are simple: toss a fair coin until the first heads. Heads on the 1st toss pays 2 yuan; heads first on the 2nd, 4 yuan; on the 3rd, 8 yuan… heads first on the nth toss pays 2ⁿ yuan.

How much would you pay to take part? Don’t read on yet; try it yourself.

Mathematically, “probability × prize” for the nth term is exactly 1 every time: (½)ⁿ × 2ⁿ = 1. Infinitely many 1s add up to infinity, so by expected value one ought to be willing to pay any price. Yet almost nobody will pay a hundred dollars. Either people are all irrational, or “look only at expected value” is itself the wrong method.

The way out was found by two mathematicians, one after the other. In 1728 Gabriel Cramer wrote in a letter to Nicolaus that the value of money to a person grows like a square root, not in proportion to the amount.

In 1738 Daniel Bernoulli published a paper in the journal of the Imperial Academy of Sciences in St. Petersburg and proposed a fuller idea: the same dollar is worth more to a poor person than to a rich one. What should be averaged is the satisfaction the money brings, that is, the expected utility. He used a logarithmic curve, along which utility rises more and more slowly. Because his paper appeared in St. Petersburg, the gamble got the name “the St. Petersburg paradox.”

Switch to utility and the infinity disappears. Drag the slider below and see what price a person with 1000 yuan would put on this game.

St. Petersburg paradox

St. Petersburg paradox

Nicolaus Bernoulli · posed it in a letter in 1713; Daniel Bernoulli · explained it with utility in 1738

A gamble whose value is infinite by expected value, yet nobody will pay much to play it. It shows that value is not the same as the amount of money.

ExampleFlip a coin; if the first heads comes on flip n, you win 2ⁿ dollars. The expected value is infinite, yet almost nobody will pay a hundred dollars to enter.

Step 3: the more money, the less each dollar is worth

Reprice this game with “logarithmic utility” (how later writers often sum up Bernoulli’s idea). Then see what happens to the expected value if the casino’s ability to pay is limited.

In 1944, von Neumann and Morgenstern axiomatized “expected utility” in Theory of Games and Economic Behavior, and it became the standard tool of economics.

But experiments in the mid-20th century kept finding that real people often violate it, for example Allais’s counterexample from the 1950s, and effects like “losing points hurts more than gaining them.”

In 1979 Kahneman and Tversky changed the question. They stopped asking how people “should” choose and recorded how people “actually” choose. The theory built on that approach is prospect theory.

For the game designer, value is not the same as the number itself. The same 50 points of damage weigh differently on a player with a lot of health and one with little. Losing the same 18 points also differs for someone who has saved 100 points and someone who has saved 20.

4

Why it worksWhy a “second identical sword” has no meaning

You already have a health potion in your bag, and picking up another is a pleasure. By the ninth, you hardly feel anything. The same “+1” is worth less and less, which is exactly what Daniel Bernoulli’s curve looks like inside a game.

The same choice has another half. If you spend 100 gold on an iron sword, you cannot buy the shield, and the best alternative you gave up is the price of this choice. Put the two together and they answer “should I take one more?” If the added item is worth more than “spending the resources elsewhere,” take it; if not, stop.

Diminishing marginal utility + opportunity cost

Marginal utility diminishes: the more of the same thing you hold, the less each extra one adds. Opportunity cost: the real cost of a choice is the best other option given up.

Sources:
Jevons · 1871, Menger · 1871, Walras · 1874 · each proposed marginal utility independently · often called the “marginal revolution.” Earlier still, Daniel Bernoulli in 1738 had explained the St. Petersburg paradox with the idea that utility from wealth grows more and more slowly.
Friedrich von Wieser · 1914 · Social Economics · the term “opportunity cost” is often traced to him.

Diminishing marginal utility

Diminishing marginal utility

The more of the same thing you have, the less each additional one adds in value.

ExampleThe first health potion in the bag can save a life; the ninth just gathers dust.

This curve governs item design directly. For repeated, interchangeable options, the second one’s added value quickly falls below the opportunity cost, and from then on “take one more of the same” is a dominated option. For the second to stay worth having, it must differ from the first in some other way, which brings us back to the trade-offs described earlier.

The 9th health potion: the first saves your life, and the ninth gathers dust in the bag. So good design either caps the potions or gives later potions different uses.
100 gold: a sword or a shield: buy the sword and you lose the survival the shield would bring. The shield’s value is the opportunity cost of this choice, unseen but real.
Four burgers in a row: when you’re hungry the first is wonderfully good, and the fourth is almost a burden. Choosing a film on Saturday afternoon, the opportunity cost is the game you didn’t go to.
Counterexample: collectible card sets: the more you collect, the more the whole set is worth, and the fifth card is worth more than the first, so marginal utility here is increasing. Diminishing is a common pattern, not an iron law.
5

Why it worksWhy losing points hurts so much: prospect theory and loss aversion

Chapter 6 said players fear boredom most, and also anxiety. And “possibly losing what you already have” is the cheapest way to create anxiety. Try it with a small game: take turns rolling a die and adding the points to a “turn score”; roll a 1 and the whole turn score is lost; at any time you may “bank,” moving the turn score into your total.

By the time the score reaches 18, have you started to hesitate? Rolling a 1 now hurts far more than “18 more points” would please. This is a very stable tendency in human decision-making, and it has nothing to do with being stingy.

The psychologists Kahneman and Tversky wrote it into prospect theory.

Concept card

Prospect theory

Prospect theory

Daniel Kahneman, Amos Tversky · their 1979 paper “Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk”

People judge gains and losses by whether they have more or less than the status quo. A loss of a given size hurts more than a gain of the same size (loss aversion).

ExampleYou have built up 18 points rolling dice, and one more roll could wipe them all out; most people hesitate over whether to bank them.

Prospect theory has three main points.

People judge “more or less than the status quo,” and the status quo is the reference point, not the final absolute number.

Loss aversion: a loss of a given size weighs roughly twice as much as a gain of the same size.

People also distort probabilities and overestimate small ones, which is why they buy both lottery tickets and insurance.

A classic finding: play it safe when winning, gamble when losing

“A sure 100, or a half chance of 200”: the expected value is the same, and most people take the sure thing. Flip it into losses, “a sure loss of 100, or a half chance of losing 200,” and many people are suddenly willing to gamble. With the same mathematical structure, switching from the “gain” side to the “loss” side reverses the choice. This is the reflection effect in prospect theory.

Sources:
Kahneman and Tversky · 1979 · recorded how people actually choose under risk in experiments · proposed prospect theory (“Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk,” Econometrica 47(2)).
Tversky and Kahneman · 1992 · estimated the value function · exponent about 0.88, loss-aversion coefficient λ about 2.25, which are medians across participants, with large individual differences (“Advances in Prospect Theory,” Journal of Risk and Uncertainty 5).

Advanced: how this is calculated

Subjective net gain of “roll again” = average gain 3.33 − λ × T / 6 (T is the points this turn). A roll of 2 to 6 adds points, about 3.33 on average per roll; a roll of 1 has probability one in six and loses T points, and that pain is multiplied by λ.

Try it: first drag λ to 3.5 and play a game to 50 points; then drag λ to 1 and compare how far apart the two “stopping lines” are.

Objectively, the break-even point between “keep rolling” and “bank” is T = 20 turn points. This is the well-known “stop at 20” rule in dice games like Pig, which maximizes expected score in a single turn. In a race to 50, the best strategy also shifts with the score.

If the pain is λ times the gain, the subjective stopping line is 20/λ. Designers can use this: let the player own something first and then face losing it, and the tension is far higher than with a mere “might gain.”

Roguelike death resetting everything: on dying you lose all the gear from this run. The more gear piled up, the tenser each step, because what you are guarding already belongs to you.
Login-streak rewards: miss one day and it resets to zero. Players open the game to avoid losing the streak, and the reward comes second. It works so well that it deserves a second thought: is it giving players enjoyment, or trading on their loss aversion to buy their time?
Struck-through price tags: “was 500, now 400” makes people want to buy more than “we always sell it at 400.” The reference point changed, and the same 400 is a bargain in one case and merely a price in the other.
Counterexample: every small slip resets: loss aversion magnifies punishment more than twofold, so excessive “point deductions” bring not tension but frustration. The player feels the game is targeting them.
6

MoreAre more options always better? Choice overload

Open a menu with three hundred dishes and you feel more anxious, and finally order “the usual.” This phenomenon is choice overload. The best-known evidence is the “jam study” of 2000: at a supermarket, a stall with 24 jams drew 60% of passers-by to stop, while a stall with 6 drew 40%. But of those who stopped, only 3% bought at the 24-jam stall, against nearly 30% at the 6-jam stall.

For twenty years the result was cited to show that “more options make choosing harder.” Its later fate has been less kind: studies that repeated it in other settings often failed to find the same thing.

The jam study, and its controversy

In 2010, Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd pooled a large number of related experiments and found that the average effect is close to zero, with big differences between studies. Later work proposed that whether the effect appears depends on conditions: whether the options are hard to compare, whether people already have a clear preference, whether they can filter by category, and whether there is time pressure. Many options do add to the burden of deciding; whether that turns into a bad outcome depends on the situation.

Sources:
Iyengar and Lepper · 2000 · supermarket jam-stall experiment · purchase rates differed greatly between the 24-jam and 6-jam stalls (“When Choice is Demotivating,” Journal of Personality and Social Psychology).
Scheibehenne, Greifeneder, Todd · 2010 · meta-analysis · average effect close to zero.
Chernev, Böckenholt, Goodman · 2015 · review of moderating factors.

“3 to 5 distinct options is usually enough” is a rule of thumb. Players of skill-tree and building games love huge numbers of options; the key is to use categories, staged unlocks and recommendations to keep the burden of each added option low.

Forty skills shown at once: a new player just closes the window. Switch to staged unlocks with a pick-one-of-three each time, and the same content makes choosing easy.
The “recommended for you” on a video site: among tens of thousands of films you don’t know what to watch, and the platform picks 6, and you click one at once. With fewer options, the choosing actually happens faster.
Counterexample: ten almost identical pieces of gear in the shop: though there are many, none is distinct. The player compares a pile of noise, not a set of trade-offs, which is worse than having fewer options.
7

MoreHow to measure “suspense”: information

An outcome that can be guessed with certainty teaches nothing when it happens. An outcome that is never quite predictable makes people go “oh” when it arrives. Dominated options and always-best options are meaningless partly because their results are too easy to guess.

How easy something is to guess can be measured. Claude Shannon gave the measure in his foundational 1948 paper on information theory, and its name is information entropy.

Information entropy: one number for “how uncertain”

For a two-way outcome, when the two results are equally likely (a coin flip, say), suspense is greatest and the entropy is 1 bit. If one result has a 99% chance, the entropy is only about 0.08 bit, and there is almost no suspense.

Advanced: as a formula

Let the probability of one outcome be p. The information entropy is H(p) = −p·log₂p − (1−p)·log₂(1−p), in bits. At p = 0.5, H = 1; at p = 0.99, H ≈ 0.08.

Source: Claude Shannon · 1948 · proposed information entropy to measure the uncertainty of outcomes (“A Mathematical Theory of Communication,” Bell System Technical Journal). Using it as an analogy for whether “a choice carries information” is this course’s own borrowing.
Concept card

Information entropy

Information entropy

Claude Shannon · his 1948 paper “A Mathematical Theory of Communication”

A number measuring how hard an outcome is to guess, in bits. The harder to guess, the higher the entropy.

ExampleA coin flip is the hardest to guess, with entropy of 1 bit; if 97% of players buy the same weapon, the entropy of that choice is close to 0.

Think of “how will players choose” as a probability distribution: if 99% of players pick the same one, the information in that choice is close to 0, the same as a false choice. If the shares picked are fairly balanced, the trade-off is real. When reading data, a developer can check each option’s pick rate to see whether any is dominated.

97% of players buy the same weapon first: that weapon is “always best,” and in the players’ eyes the shop offers no choice.
Guessing the murderer in the first ten minutes: the remaining ninety minutes of a mystery carry almost no information. The audience doesn’t dislike the film; it just gives nothing new.
A counterexample: a pure lottery draw: entropy is at its highest, but the player’s choice changes nothing; it is just rolling dice. Information entropy measures only suspense, not consequence, so a meaningful choice still needs trade-offs and consequences added.

If everyone picks it, the information is close to 0.

Common misconceptions

  • “More options mean more freedom.” Number is only one dimension, and distinctness and presentation matter more. A lot of similar options create noise.
  • “Risk means meaning.” If you can’t tell how big the risk is, or choosing has no consequence, it is just a roll of the dice.
  • “Delete every dominated option.” A newcomer’s starter gear or an upgradeable precursor is sometimes a deliberate growth ladder. The question is whether it will mislead the player.
  • “Pick whatever has the higher expected value.” The St. Petersburg game is the counterexample. Value depends on the situation and the reference point, not just the numbers.

Reflection and debate

  • Prospect theory describes how people choose, not how they should. The strength of loss aversion is disputed too: Gal and Rucker (2018, Journal of Consumer Psychology) hold that its generality and size are overstated, which is one view in the field. Others reply that loss aversion has moderating factors, but has not disappeared.
  • Experiments versus games. Gains and losses in experiments are often money; in games they often come with emotion, social ties and a sense of achievement, so behavior may differ from questionnaires.
  • “Logarithmic utility” is only one way out. The St. Petersburg paradox has other resolutions (such as a finite bankroll), and some have built variants that logarithmic utility still can’t resolve.
  • Information entropy is only an analogy. A fully random outcome has the highest entropy, but random does not mean meaningful.

Key points

  • A meaningful choice = information + trade-off + consequence; avoid “dominated options” and “always-best” options.
  • Looking only at expected value hits a wall (the St. Petersburg game): value depends on the situation, hence utility and diminishing marginal utility. People also don’t choose entirely by expected utility, hence prospect theory.
  • Opportunity cost makes repeated options lose value; loss aversion makes losing what you have hurt more than never getting it.
  • More options are not always better; 3 to 5 distinct ones are usually enough. This is a rule of thumb, and the research on choice overload is not consistent. The easier an outcome is to guess, the less information the choice carries.
A meaningful choice is one where the player can see each option clearly, every option gives something and costs something, and after choosing, the situation really is different.
