Chapter 8

Meaningful Choices

At heart, a game is a string of decisions. But which decisions are worth stopping to think about?

Learning goals

about 45 min
  • Use “information, trade-off, consequence” to check whether a choice is meaningful, and catch “dominated options” and “always-best options.”
  • Explain why a coin-flipping bet in 1713 made “just calculate the expected value” hit a wall, and how later thinkers got step by step to utility and prospect theory.
  • Explain why repeated options lose value, why losing points hurts more than gaining them, and use that to assemble a set of options with moderate numbers and different strengths and weaknesses.
1

What it isWhat makes a choice “meaningful”?

Chapter 2 said the core loop turns because the player makes decisions on every lap. But decisions can be real or fake. You walk into a game’s potion shop, and on the shelf are a red potion and a blue potion. The red one heals 50, the blue one heals 50, the price is the same, and only the bottle color differs. You grab the red one without thinking.

Strictly speaking, no decision has happened here. Whichever you take, the result is the same, and nothing makes you think for even a second. Game design calls this a false choice.

So what does a real choice look like? Think of a fork in the road in a game: the short path has guards, the long path is safe but misses a treasure chest. You have to think about what you are short of right now, and that thinking is the evidence that the choice is meaningful.

Take the fork apart and there are three things in it. The player has to know where each path leads, or it is just a guess; each path has to give and take, or there is no need to think; and after choosing, the situation has to really be different in a way the player can see, or the choice was pointless.

👀

Information

The player knows what the options are and can judge.

⚖️

Trade-off

Each option gains and loses something; none is “always best.”

🌱

Consequence

The choice affects the situation afterward, and the player notices.

A line often quoted in design circles is: “A game is a series of interesting decisions.” It is mostly credited to Sid Meier (designer of the Civilization series), though the exact source is unclear; he looked back on and developed the idea himself in his GDC 2012 talk “Interesting Decisions.”

Short path or long path: the short path has guards, and the long path is safe but skips a treasure chest. You can see what each path costs and know what happens when you choose, so you have to think seriously about whether you are short of health or of money right now.
Two cards in a card game: one is cheap but weak, one costly but strong. Which one goes in the deck depends on how you want your whole deck to play, not on which is “better to begin with.”
Renting a home: the one close to work is expensive, and the one farther away is cheap but costs an extra forty minutes on the subway each day. Neither wins in every respect, which is why you hesitate over a calculator.
Red potion and blue potion: the same healing, the same price, just a different color. Whichever you take is the same, so there is no decision here, only an act of “picking.”
A near-miss: big, medium and small flasks: the small one heals 30 for 10 coins, the medium 60 for 15, the large 100 for 20, the bigger the costlier. They are not dominated: cost and benefit change together, so it is still a normal trade-off. To judge dominance you must look at benefit and cost together, not stare at one number.
2

What it isHow to “see” a dominated option

The flaw in the iron sword and steel sword pair is easy to spot, but with many options the flaw hides. One way is to plot each option on a “cost–gain” plane, with the horizontal axis for costs such as price and cooldown, and the vertical axis for gains such as damage and healing.

Once drawn, the rule is intuitive: the whole region to the lower right of an option (higher cost, lower gain) is the territory it dominates. An option that falls inside has no reason to exist. Truly meaningful options each hold a different position, such as lower left (cheap but weak) and upper right (costly but strong), and joined up they form a frontier of trade-offs.

This diagram also explains “always best”: if one option pushes every other option into its lower right, the others are mere decoration, and the player’s “choosing” collapses back into the state of the red and blue potions.

How it was inventedThe St. Petersburg game: why “calculate the expected value” isn’t enough

A bet “worth infinite money in theory” that nobody wants to buy

Faced with a trade-off, people’s first reaction is to do the sums: multiply each outcome’s money by its probability and add them up to get the expected value, and take whichever is bigger. Seventeenth-century mathematicians handled gambling this way, and it worked well and never caused trouble.

On September 9, 1713, Nicolaus Bernoulli, in a letter to Pierre Rémond de Montmort, tossed out a game that made this method look foolish. The rules are simple: flip a fair coin until heads first appears. Heads on the 1st flip pays 2 dollars; heads first on the 2nd flip pays 4; on the 3rd, 8… first heads on flip n pays 2ⁿ dollars.

Why the early method fails. Mathematically, the nth term, “probability × prize,” is exactly 1: (½)ⁿ × 2ⁿ = 1. Infinitely many 1s add up to infinity, so by expected value you should be willing to pay any price. Yet almost nobody will pay a hundred dollars. Either people are all irrational, or the method of “only look at the expected value” itself has a problem.

The key step. Two mathematicians found the way out almost at once. In 1728, Gabriel Cramer wrote to Nicolaus that the value of money to a person grows roughly like a square root rather than in proportion to the amount. In 1738, Daniel Bernoulli published a paper in the journal of the St. Petersburg Imperial Academy of Sciences with a fuller idea: the same dollar is worth more to a poor person than to a rich one. What should be calculated is not the expectation of money but the expectation of the satisfaction money brings, that is, utility. He used a logarithmic curve, with utility growing more and more slowly. The game got the name “St. Petersburg paradox” because his paper was published in St. Petersburg.

Once you switch to utility, the infinity disappears. Drag the slider below and see what price a person with 1,000 dollars would offer for this game.

Step 3: the more money, the less each dollar is worth

Price the game again using “logarithmic utility” (later writers often sum up Bernoulli’s idea this way); then see what happens to the expected value if the casino’s ability to pay out is limited.

Then a new problem appeared. In 1944, von Neumann and Morgenstern axiomatized “expected utility” in Theory of Games and Economic Behavior, and it became the standard tool of economics. But experiments from the mid-twentieth century on kept finding that real people’s choices often violate it, such as the counterexample Allais gave in the 1950s, and phenomena like “losing points hurts more than gaining them.” In 1979, Kahneman and Tversky changed the question altogether: don’t ask how people “should” choose, only record how they “actually” choose. This is prospect theory.

What does this line of thought mean for game designers? Value is not the number itself: the same 50 points of damage weighs differently for a player with lots of health and one with little; losing 18 points also differs for someone who has saved 100 points and someone who has saved 20. The next two sections are the two legs of this relay that get used most.

4

Why it worksWhy a “second identical sword” has no meaning

You already have a health potion in your bag, and picking up another makes you happy. By the ninth, you barely feel anything. The same “+1” is worth less and less, which is just what Daniel Bernoulli’s curve looks like in a game.

There is another half. If you spend 100 gold on the iron sword, you can’t buy the shield. The best alternative you gave up is the true cost of this choice. Put the two together and you can answer “should I take one more?”: take it as long as the extra one is still worth more than “spending the resource elsewhere”; once it isn’t, stop.

Diminishing marginal utility + opportunity cost

Diminishing marginal utility: the more you hold of the same kind of thing, the smaller the added value each extra one brings. Opportunity cost: the real cost of making a choice is the best other option you gave up.

Sources: the idea of marginal utility was proposed independently around the 1870s by Jevons (1871), Menger (1871) and Walras (1874), often called the “marginal revolution”; earlier, in 1738, Daniel Bernoulli used “utility of wealth grows slower and slower” to explain the St. Petersburg paradox. The term “opportunity cost” is often traced to Friedrich von Wieser of the Austrian school (Social Economics, 1914).

This curve directly governs item design: for repeated, interchangeable options, the added value of the second one quickly drops below the opportunity cost, and at that point “take another of the same kind” is a dominated option. To keep the second one valuable there is only one way, which is to make it different in some other respect, that is, to go back to the trade-off described earlier.

The 9th health potion: the first saves your life, and the ninth gathers dust in the bag. So good design either caps the potions or gives later potions different uses.
100 gold for a sword or a shield: buy the sword and you lose the survival the shield would have brought. The shield’s value is the opportunity cost of this choice, invisible but real.
Four burgers in a row: when hungry, the first is wonderful and the fourth is almost a burden. If you picked a movie on Saturday afternoon, the opportunity cost is the game you didn’t go to.
A counterexample: sets in collectible card games: the more cards you collect, the more valuable the set, and the fifth card is worth more than the first. Marginal utility is increasing here. Diminishing is a common pattern, not an iron law.
5

Why it worksWhy losing points hurts so much: prospect theory and loss aversion

Chapter 6 said players fear boredom most, and also anxiety. And “possibly losing what you already have” is the cheapest way to create anxiety. Try it with a small game: take turns rolling a die and adding the points to a “turn score”; roll a 1 and the whole turn score is lost; at any time you may “bank,” moving the turn score into your total.

When you have 18 points saved up, haven’t you already started to hesitate? Rolling a 1 at that moment feels far worse, usually, than the pleasure of “getting 18 more.” That isn’t you being stingy; it is a fairly stable tendency in human decision-making.

The psychologists Kahneman and Tversky wrote it into prospect theory.

It has roughly three points. First, people judge “more or less than the status quo,” and the status quo is the reference point, not the final absolute number. Second, loss aversion: a loss weighs about twice as much as a gain of the same size. Third, people distort probabilities, often overestimating small ones, which is why they both buy lottery tickets and buy insurance.

A classic finding: play it safe when winning, gamble when losing

“A sure 100, or a half chance of 200”: the expected value is the same, and most people take the sure thing. Flip it into losses, “a sure loss of 100, or a half chance of losing 200”: many people are now willing to gamble. The same mathematical structure, on the “gain” side or the “loss” side, reverses the choice, which is called the reflection effect.

Source: Daniel Kahneman & Amos Tversky, “Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk,” Econometrica 47(2), 1979; Tversky & Kahneman, 1992, “Advances in Prospect Theory,” Journal of Risk and Uncertainty 5: the typical estimates given for the value function are an exponent of about 0.88 and a loss-aversion coefficient λ of about 2.25 (median of the participants; individual differences are large).

Objectively, the break-even point between “keep going” and “bank” is a turn score of T = 20 (maximizing expected score per turn, the “bank at 20” familiar from dice games like Pig; in a race to 50 the best strategy also changes with the score). If your pain is λ times your pleasure, your subjective stopping line is 20/λ. Designers can use this: letting players first own something and then face losing it produces much more tension than merely “might gain.”

Permadeath in roguelikes: if you die you lose all the gear from the run. The more gear you pile up, the tenser every step gets, because what you are guarding is already yours.
Login streak rewards: miss a day and it resets to zero. Players don’t open the game for the reward but to avoid losing that string of numbers. It works very well, which is exactly why you should ask yourself whether you are giving players fun or trading on their loss aversion for playtime.
Struck-through price tags: “was 500, now 400” makes people want to buy more than “we always sell it at 400.” The reference point changed, and the same 400 is a bargain in one case and merely a price in the other.
A counterexample: every small slip resets you: loss aversion magnifies penalties by more than double, so excessive “point deductions” bring not tension but frustration. Players feel the game is out to get them.
6

MoreAre more options always better? Choice overload

Open a three-hundred-dish menu and you may feel more anxious, and in the end order “the usual.” This phenomenon is called choice overload, and the most famous evidence is the 2000 “jam study”: at a supermarket, a display with 24 jams drew 60% of passersby to stop, while a display with 6 drew only 40%; but of those who stopped, only 3% at the 24-jam display actually bought, against nearly 30% at the 6-jam display.

This result has been cited for twenty years to show that “more options aren’t necessarily better.” But its later fate has been rocky: people who redid it in other settings often failed to get the same result.

The jam study, and its controversy

In 2010, Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd pooled many related experiments and found that, on average, the effect was close to zero and varied a great deal between studies. Later work suggested that whether the effect appears depends on conditions: whether options are hard to compare, whether people already have a clear preference, whether they can filter by category, and whether there is time pressure. A safer statement is: more options add to the decision burden, and whether that becomes a bad outcome depends on the situation.

Sources: Sheena Iyengar & Mark Lepper, “When Choice is Demotivating,” Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2000; meta-analysis: Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd, 2010; review of moderating factors: Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman, 2015.

So “3 to 5 distinct options are usually enough” is only a rule of thumb. Players of skill-tree and building games often enjoy huge numbers of options; the key is to use categories, staged unlocking and recommendations to push down the burden of each added option.

Showing 40 skills at once: newcomers often just close the window. Switch to staged unlocking and pick-one-of-three each time, and with the same content the choosing gets lighter.
“Recommended for you” on a video site: out of tens of thousands of films you don’t know what to watch, but the platform picks 6 and you click one right away. With fewer options, choosing actually happens faster.
A counterexample: 10 nearly identical items in a shop: there are many, but none is distinct. The player has to compare a pile of noise, not a set of trade-offs, which is worse than having few options.
7

MoreHow to measure “suspense”: information

An outcome you can guess one hundred percent teaches you nothing when it appears; an outcome you can never quite guess is what makes you say “oh” when it comes. Dominated options and always-best options are meaningless partly because their results are too easy to guess.

This “how easy to guess” can be measured. In his founding paper of information theory in 1948, Claude Shannon gave a quantity called information entropy.

Information entropy: one number for “how uncertain”

For a two-way outcome where one result has probability p, the information entropy is
H(p) = −p·log₂p − (1−p)·log₂(1−p) (in bits). For a coin flip (p = 0.5), H = 1, the most suspense; at p = 0.99, H ≈ 0.08, almost no suspense.

Source: Claude Shannon, “A Mathematical Theory of Communication,” Bell System Technical Journal, 1948. Using it as an analogy for “whether a choice carries information” is borrowed for this course and is not an accepted law of game design.

Treat “how players will choose” as a probability distribution: if 99% of players pick the same one, the choice carries almost no information, which makes it equal to a false choice; if the shares of the options are fairly balanced, real trade-offs exist. When developers read data, they use each option’s pick rate to check for dominated options.

97% of players buy the same weapon first: that weapon is “always best,” and in the players’ eyes the shop offers no choice.
Guessing the killer in the first ten minutes: for the remaining ninety minutes of the mystery, the information is close to zero. It isn’t that you dislike the film; it just hasn’t told you anything new.
A counterexample: a pure lottery draw: entropy is at its highest, but the player’s choice changes nothing; it is just rolling dice. Information entropy measures only suspense, not consequence, so a meaningful choice still needs trade-offs and consequences added.

The “three types of players” are a sketch: they score the options with different weights to see whether they pick the same one; if everybody picks it, the information is close to 0.

Common misconceptions

  • “More options means more freedom.” Quantity is only one dimension; distinctness and presentation matter more, and lots of similar options create noise.
  • “Risk means meaning.” If you can’t tell how big the risk is, or choosing has no consequence, it is just a roll of the dice.
  • “Delete every dominated option.” A newcomer’s starter gear or an upgradeable precursor is sometimes a deliberate growth ladder; the question is whether players will be misled by it.
  • “Higher expected value means you should pick it.” The St. Petersburg game is the counterexample; value depends on circumstances and the reference point, not only on numbers.

Reflection and debate

  • Prospect theory describes how people choose, not how they should. The strength of loss aversion is also disputed: Gal and Rucker (2018, Journal of Consumer Psychology) argue its universality and size have been exaggerated, which is one view in the field; others reply that loss aversion has moderating factors but has not disappeared.
  • Experiments versus games. Gains and losses in experiments are often money; in games they often come with emotion, social ties and a sense of achievement, so behavior may differ from questionnaires.
  • “Logarithmic utility” is only one way out. The St. Petersburg paradox has other resolutions (such as a finite bankroll), and some have built variants that logarithmic utility still can’t resolve.
  • Information entropy is only an analogy. A fully random outcome has the highest entropy, but random does not mean meaningful.

Key points

  • A meaningful choice = information + trade-off + consequence; avoid “dominated options” and “always-best” options.
  • Only calculating expected value hits a wall (the St. Petersburg game): value depends on circumstances, hence utility and diminishing marginal utility; people also don’t quite choose by expected utility, hence prospect theory.
  • Opportunity cost makes repeated options lose value; loss aversion makes losing what you have hurt more than never getting it.
  • More options are not always better, and 3 to 5 distinct ones are usually enough (a rule of thumb; results on choice overload are inconsistent); the easier the result is to guess, the less information the choice carries.
A meaningful choice is one the player can see clearly, is reluctant to make, and yet has to make: each option has its gains and its losses, and after choosing, the situation really is different.
