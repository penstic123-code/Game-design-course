Chapter 23 · Case study

Tetris

The rules fit on one page, and people have been playing it for forty years.

⏱ about 40 min

Learning goals

  • Explain why the pieces have 4 cells and come in 7 kinds, not 5 cells and 12 kinds, and how this trade-off served both “enough variety” and “learned at a glance.”
  • Take a minimal game apart with “one knob for difficulty, one bag for luck,” and say what problem the four-line high score, the shuffle bag and the certain-loss ending each solve.
  • Explain the urge to “play one more” with prediction error and the peak-end rule, and know which parts are evidence and which are only conjecture.
1

What it isHow do rules of three sentences last for decades?

If you had to explain how to play Tetris to someone who had never seen it, in three sentences, you probably could. First: seven shapes, each made of four small squares, fall one at a time from the top of a well. Second: you can only shift one left or right, or turn it. Third: fill a whole row across and that row disappears; stack up to the top and you lose.

Three sentences, done. Yet those same three sentences stole countless lunch breaks on the 1989 Game Boy, and to this day people practice until their fingers shake to set records. How did three sentences hold up for decades?

There is no “growth” step in this loop: the player doesn’t get stronger, the challenge does. That is what makes it quite unlike most games.

To see why it lasts, we have to separate “the rules” from “the position.” The rules are just those few sentences, small enough for a card. The position is another matter: when you place the first piece, the well is empty; by the thirtieth, where each piece may go is limited by your previous twenty-nine decisions, and the shape you face has probably never been met by anyone else in the world.

Not one of these positions was written by the designer. Plays like “leave a whole column for a long bar,” “keep the stack low to survive” and “risk saving up four rows for one big clear” grew out of players running into the rules. In MDA terms, the rules are the mechanics, and the plays that grew are the dynamics. Minimal mechanics with rich dynamics is the core hand this game holds.

But be careful: few rules do not guarantee much variety. The examples below set the positive and the negative side by side.

Tetris: only 7 shapes, all recognized at a glance. But each can go in a dozen columns and turn four ways, so a single step has dozens of placements, and the previous step’s choice changes every later choice, a combination space so large it can hardly be exhausted.
Go: the rules can be told in very few words: place a stone, capture, count territory. Yet its variety runs so deep that after more than two thousand years of play new things are still being found.
LEGO bricks: a counterpart from daily life. With just one rule, “studs snap together,” a child builds houses, spaceships and nameless monsters.
Tic-tac-toe: also minimal rules, but a 3×3 grid is simply too small, and as long as neither side errs, the result must be a draw, which children see through after a few games. The point of this counterexample: variety does not come from few rules, but from a combination space that is large enough.
2

How it was inventedHow a box of puzzle pieces became blocks that fall

Moscow, a puzzle in a shop

Alexey Pajitnov was then doing speech-recognition research at the Computer Center of the Soviet Academy of Sciences in Moscow, and in his spare time liked writing small games. One day he bought a box of pentomino puzzle pieces in a shop: twelve shapes each made of five squares, to be packed snugly into a box. He fiddled with them in the office and thought: could this be moved into a computer?

His first attempt was almost a straight copy: move the pieces with the arrow keys and fit them together one by one. But according to Vadim Gerasimov, the teenager who later helped port the game, this version was rather dull. A puzzle is fun on a table because you can pick it up, study it and think slowly; moved onto a screen, only shifting around is left.

The turning point came from making the pieces move on their own: a never-ending stream of shapes falling from a rectangular well, faster and faster, and you have to place them before they land. But a new problem came at once: how many cells should each piece have? Don’t look at the answer yet; try it yourself.

Pajitnov’s choice agrees with what several reports say: twelve five-cell shapes were too complicated. On a machine with no graphical interface they were hard to draw and hard to fit on a limited screen, and players would first have to spend effort recognizing shapes. So he changed to four cells, 7 shapes in all. The game’s name came from this too: the Greek for “four” (tetra), plus his favorite sport, tennis.

But with only falling and placing, the well would fill up quickly and the game would be over. Hence the next rule: when a row is completely filled, erase it. Jimmy Maher, in reviewing this history, wrote that it gave the player a little time and space. With that rule the game’s character changed completely: you are no longer finishing a box of puzzle pieces, which has an end; you are in a tug-of-war you cannot win, trying to drag out the time.

Around 1984 Moscow. Pajitnov buys a pentomino puzzle and wants to move it onto a computer. (Sources differ on the year: in an interview with The Guardian he said June 1984, and some sources say it was finished in 1985.)
First attempt A straight copy of the puzzle, using arrow keys to fit five-cell pieces into “creatures,” “quite boring,” in Gerasimov’s words. The fun of a puzzle on a table didn’t come along onto the screen.
The key idea Let shapes fall from the top of the well and fall faster and faster, change to four cells and 7 kinds, and erase rows that are filled. The first version ran on an Elektronika 60 with no graphical interface, with blocks made of spaces and square brackets, written in Pascal, and reportedly with no score and no levels.
1985–1986 Gerasimov, sixteen, ported it to Soviet IBM PC compatibles and added color; Dmitry Pavlovsky added a high-score table. Floppy disks spread one copy at a time through Moscow’s computer circles.
1989 Henk Rogers negotiated Nintendo’s handheld and home-console rights from the Soviet side. The Game Boy version went on sale in Japan on June 14, and in the United States on July 31 bundled with the console, and from then on it went around the world.

What it isOne knob: difficulty rests almost only on “speed”

Making the shapes “fall faster and faster” later became the difficulty method shared by every version. Think how convenient it is: not a word of the rules changed, the player has nothing more to learn, only one number is turned, and the same rules go from a leisurely stroll to a hundred-meter sprint.

For the player this knob is also the easiest to understand. You don’t need to read a manual to know that “faster” means “harder,” and you feel that difficulty step by step through your own hands.

The figure below is this page’s setting: level 0 drops one cell every 800 milliseconds, each level multiplies the interval by 0.82, bottoming out at 70 milliseconds. (Level 0 of the Nintendo NES version is 48 frames per cell, which at 60 frames per second is exactly about 800 milliseconds; the curve after that is one this page set itself for demonstration, not the original’s numbers.)

The challenge is raised along a line that keeps climbing; the player’s skill, though, has a ceiling. The curve has no top, so some day the challenge will exceed the skill.

This explains why it has no end. There is no “clearing the game”: you are just caught up with, step by step, by ever faster blocks. Mathematicians have even proved it: Heidi Burgiel, in her 1997 paper “How to Lose at Tetris,” showed that as long as the piece sequence has long enough runs of alternating S and Z shapes, the game must eventually end however you place them; and under pure random generation, such a sequence is bound to appear sooner or later, so every game is a certain loss. Failure being inevitable makes every game a contest of “how long can you hold on.”

A treadmill: only one knob, speed. The same person goes from a stroll to a sprint, and the machine itself hasn’t changed at all.
Snake: the difficulty in the game grows by itself: the more you eat, the longer the body, and the harder it is to go around yourself. Each bite the player takes turns their own knob.
A tennis ball machine: the ball speed is raised notch by notch, and your reactions are pushed to the limit little by little. The thing in life most like a “speed knob.”
A game that only speeds up: the lights flash faster and faster, and the play hasn’t changed at all. Late on, all that remains is a pure reaction test. The point of this counterexample: the speed knob works in Tetris because at every speed the placement choices stay interesting; speed with nothing to think about leaves only hand speed.
4

What it isOne bag: putting a bridle on luck

The order of the pieces is random, which is another reason it stays fresh. But randomness brings a trouble: if every piece is drawn independently from 7 kinds, a long stretch with no long bar I is entirely possible, just when you need it most.

People are not sensitive to randomness. True randomness naturally contains long “droughts,” but the player’s feeling is only: the game is out to get me.

Designers thought of more than one fix. The NES version’s way was plain: if the piece drawn is the same as the last one, redraw once. This stops three identical pieces in a row but cannot stop a long bar from being missing for dozens of pieces. The later official design guideline’s way is the shuffle bag (7-bag): one of each of the 7 kinds goes into the bag, which is shuffled and dealt out, then a fresh bag is used. According to the Tetris Wiki, it was first used in The New Tetris, where the bag held 9 of each kind, 63 in all.

The shuffle bag: keep the randomness, cut off the extremes. Who comes first and who later in each bag is uncertain, but every kind of piece is sure to appear within 14 pieces.
A shuffled deck of cards: each card is in the deck only once, so you can’t draw five aces in a row. The “randomness” at a card table has memory to begin with.
A playlist on “shuffle”: many players shuffle the playlist first and then play it in order, instead of drawing each song independently, so the same song doesn’t play twice in a row (a common approach, and it varies by software).
Flipping a coin repeatedly: a coin has no memory, and after five heads in a row, the next is still half and half; it won’t “make up” for you. Pure randomness does not seek balance for the player, which is why the designer must do it for the player.

Why it worksWhy does the four-line clear feel especially good

You have built up a three-deep gap and waited a dozen pieces, and finally the long bar arrives. It drops straight into the gap, four rows burst at once, and the score jumps a good way. The thrill of that moment is something no other line clear can match.

Look at the ledger first. At level 0, clearing 1 row scores 40, 2 rows 100, 3 rows 300 and 4 rows 1200 (the NES calculation, later multiplied by level plus one). If the score were proportional to rows, four rows would be 160. The actual is 1200, nearly 7.5 times as much.

A reward better than you expected: this “better than expected” has a name in neuroscience: reward prediction error. Remember the gacha mentioned in the core-loop chapter? The same kind of mechanism. The difference is that gacha hands you its surprise by probability, while Tetris’s surprise is something you build up yourself, layer by layer.

Dopamine computes “how far off,” not “how great it feels”

In the 1990s Wolfram Schultz and colleagues recorded dopamine neurons in monkey brains. They found: when an unexpected reward (say a drop of juice) appears, these neurons fire briefly; once a signal can announce the reward, the excitement moves to the signal itself, and when the reward truly arrives there is hardly a ripple; and when a predicted reward doesn’t come, the firing falls below normal. This amounts to a ledger of “how much the prediction missed,” which corresponds exactly to the error term used to update expectations in reinforcement-learning algorithms.

The lesson for designers: a reward’s size is not absolute; it depends on how far it is from expectation. The same 1200 points, if you can clear four rows easily every time, is no longer special. To keep surprise effective, rewards must be tied to things “you aren’t too sure of.” (This is reasoned from animal experiments to players, a plausible inference, with no direct measurement on Tetris players. Also, researchers don’t entirely agree on whether dopamine is “pleasure” or a “learning signal,” and the latter, common view is taken here.)

Source: Wolfram Schultz, Peter Dayan & P. Read Montague, “A Neural Substrate of Prediction and Reward,” Science 275: 1593–1599 (1997).

Why it worksWhy do you want another game after losing

Every game of Tetris ends the same way: you lose. By common sense, a game that always ends in failure should leave people discouraged. The reality is the opposite: in the half second after losing, your hand is already pressing “play again.”

The psychologist Daniel Kahneman and colleagues ran a famous experiment. Participants put one hand in 14°C cold water for 60 seconds, and another time for 90 seconds, except that in the last 30 seconds the water was quietly warmed by one degree, easing the pain slightly. Asked later which one they would repeat, most chose the longer one, though it clearly hurt 30 seconds more.

Their reading: when people recall an experience, they hardly care how long it was and mostly look at two points, the most intense moment (the peak) and the moment it ended (the end). This rule of thumb later came to be called the peak-end rule.

Memory is not adding up the time

Applied to Tetris, this gives one reading: although a game ends in failure, the last few dozen seconds before it are often the most tense and absorbing stretch of the whole game, and replaying costs almost nothing, so the bad ending hasn’t had time to leave an impression before the next game has begun. This is only one reading using the peak-end rule, with no study of this game.

The rule itself is fairly solid: a 2022 meta-analysis pooling over a hundred effect sizes found that peak and end predict later evaluation strongly, but the overall average predicts it as well, with little difference between them; and duration has almost no effect. So a more accurate statement: memory does not add up by length, it looks at a few especially striking moments.

Sources: Daniel Kahneman, Barbara Fredrickson, Charles Schreiber & Donald Redelmeier, “When More Pain Is Preferred to Less: Adding a Better End,” Psychological Science 4(6): 401–405 (1993); the meta-analysis by Alaybek et al., Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes 170 (2022).

One more: less is brainpower

The number of shapes counted in Experiment A is in fact also a cognitive-load question. Cognitive load theory, proposed by John Sweller from 1988, says working memory can hold only very little at once. Seven shapes, three actions and no text tutorial mean a newcomer has almost nothing to memorize, and all the brainpower can go into “where does this piece go.” The difficulty has been moved to spatial reasoning and hand speed, not to understanding the rules.

Source: John Sweller, “Cognitive load during problem solving: Effects on learning,” Cognitive Science 12(2) (1988).

① Find your “main knob”

Difficulty rests on a single parameter, “speed,” easy to tune, easy to understand, easy to balance. Does your game have such a knob?

② Minimal rules, endless combinations

7 shapes × a dozen columns × 4 orientations, dozens of placements per step, and no “right answer” holds forever.

③ Give information, not answers

The next-piece preview and the ghost landing spot tell you only the consequences, and the decision stays with the player, so the choice means something.

④ Put a bridle on randomness

What players can’t stand is extreme luck, not randomness itself. Keep the surprise, cut off the extremes.

Common misconceptions

  • “Few rules means easy to make”: minimalism is the result of repeated polishing. Cutting twelve shapes to seven and putting the randomness into a bag, every step is a trade-off, and often harder than adding rules.
  • “The case shows the one right way”: a loop without “growth” suits it, and not necessarily your game.
  • “Psychology principles are a winning recipe”: prediction error and the peak-end rule help you form hypotheses, and in the end players’ playtests must verify them.
  • “The line-clear rule was Pajitnov’s explicit design intent”: this page cites a secondhand overview, and we have not found his own words.

Think about it: survivorship bias

We keep discussing Tetris because it succeeded. Many games with equally minimal rules and equally rising speed are known to almost nobody, and we don’t use them as cases. So “it did this, so it succeeded” may be only survivorship bias, with timing, licensing and platform (a Game Boy bundle) just as much to thank.

Key points

  • The key trade-off: changing from 12 five-cell shapes to 7 four-cell ones, balancing “enough variety” and “learned at a glance.”
  • Minimal mechanics grow rich dynamics; few rules do not mean much variety, and tic-tac-toe is the counterexample.
  • Difficulty rests almost only on one knob, “speed,” and the challenge keeps rising until it exceeds anyone’s skill, so the game has no end, only being caught.
  • The shuffle bag is a design for randomness: keep the surprise, cut off the extremes, with at most 12 pieces between two long bars.
  • The high score of a four-line clear creates a reward beyond expectation (prediction error); the tension at the end of a game and a cheap restart may make “one more game” feel natural (the peak-end rule, a reading).
Tetris uses a few rules, one speed knob and one shuffle bag, and draws on cognitive load, prediction error and the peak-end rule at once, but it is a surviving case, not a winning recipe.
