Chapter 23 · Case study

Tetris

The rules fit on one page, and people have been playing it for forty years.

⏱ about 40 min

Learning goals

  • Explain why the pieces are 4 cells and 7 kinds, not 5 cells and 12 kinds, and how this choice provides both “enough variety” and “learned at once.”
  • Take a minimal game apart as “one knob for difficulty, one bag for luck,” and explain what the four-line clear’s high score, the shuffle bag and the unwinnable ending each solve.
  • Use prediction error and the peak-end rule to explain the urge to play “one more run,” and also know where these explanations are evidence and where they are only conjecture.
1

What it isRules of three sentences, played for decades

Three sentences are enough to explain how Tetris is played to someone who has never seen it. First: seven shapes, each made of four small squares, fall one by one from the mouth of the well. Second: you can only shift one left or right and turn it. Third: fill a whole row across, and that row disappears; pile up to the top, and you lose.

Three sentences, done, and yet those same three sentences stole countless lunch breaks on the 1989 Game Boy, and to this day people practice until their fingers shake to set records.

There is no “growth” step in this loop: the player doesn’t get stronger, the challenge does. That is what makes it quite unlike most games.

To see why it lasts so long, separate “rules” from “situations.” The rules are just those few sentences, which fit on a small card. The situation is another matter: when you place the first piece the well is empty, and by the thirtieth every piece’s destination is constrained by the twenty-nine decisions before it. Hardly another person in the world has ever met the position you are facing.

Not one of these positions was written by the designer. Plays like “leave a whole column for a long bar,” “keep the stack low to survive” and “risk saving up four rows for one big clear” grew out of players running into the rules. In MDA terms, the rules are the mechanics, and the plays that grew are the dynamics.

Few rules do not guarantee much variety. The examples below set the positive and the negative side by side.

Tetris: only 7 shapes, all recognized at a glance. But each can go in a dozen columns and turn four ways, so a single step has dozens of placements, and the previous step’s choice changes every later choice, a combination space so large it can hardly be exhausted.
Go: the rules can be told in very few words: place a stone, capture, count territory. Yet its variety runs so deep that after more than two thousand years of play new things are still being found.
LEGO bricks: a counterpart from daily life. With just one rule, “studs snap together,” a child builds houses, spaceships and nameless monsters.
Tic-tac-toe: also minimal rules, but a 3×3 grid is simply too small, and as long as neither side errs, the result must be a draw, which children see through after a few games. The point of this counterexample: variety does not come from few rules, but from a combination space that is large enough.

How it was inventedHow a box of puzzle pieces became blocks that fall

Moscow, a puzzle in a shop

Alexey Pajitnov was then doing speech-recognition research at the Computer Center of the Soviet Academy of Sciences in Moscow, and in his spare time liked writing small games. One day he bought a box of pentomino puzzle pieces in a shop: twelve shapes each made of five squares, to be packed snugly into a box. He fiddled with them in the office and thought: could this be moved into a computer?

A shape made of 5 squares joined edge to edge; not counting rotations and flips there are 12 in all. Shapes of 4 squares are tetrominoes, 7 in all.

ExampleThe 7 shapes of Tetris are the 7 tetrominoes; the puzzle Pajitnov first bought was the 12 pentominoes.

At first he tried something nearly a straight copy: move the pieces with the arrow keys and assemble them one by one. But according to the teenager Vadim Gerasimov, who later helped port the game, that version was quite boring. A puzzle on a table is fun because you can pick the pieces up, look them over and think slowly. Moved onto a screen, all that is left is shuffling things around.

The turning point came from making the pieces move by themselves: an unstoppable stream of shapes falling from a rectangular well, faster and faster, and the player has to place them before they land. But a new problem came up at once: how many cells should each piece be made of? Don’t look at the answer yet; try it yourself.

Pajitnov’s choice agrees with what several reports say: twelve five-cell shapes were too complicated. On a machine with no graphical interface they were hard to draw and hard to fit on a limited screen, and players would first have to spend effort recognizing shapes. So he changed to four cells, 7 shapes in all. The game’s name came from this too: the Greek for “four” (tetra), plus his favorite sport, tennis.

But with only falling and placing, the well would fill up very soon and the game would end. So came the next rule: when a row is completely filled, erase that row. Jimmy Maher, reviewing this history, wrote that it gave the player a little time and space. Once this rule is added, the character of the game changes completely: the player is no longer finishing a box of puzzle pieces (which has an end), but is in a tug-of-war that cannot be won, trying to stretch out the time as long as possible.

Around 1984 Moscow. Pajitnov buys a pentomino puzzle and wants to move it onto a computer. (Sources differ on the year: in an interview with The Guardian he said June 1984, and some sources say it was finished in 1985.)
First attempt A straight copy of the puzzle, using arrow keys to fit five-cell pieces into “creatures,” “quite boring,” in Gerasimov’s words. The fun of a puzzle on a table didn’t come along onto the screen.
The key idea Let shapes fall from the top of the well and fall faster and faster, change to four cells and 7 kinds, and erase rows that are filled. The first version ran on an Elektronika 60 with no graphical interface, with blocks made of spaces and square brackets, written in Pascal, and reportedly with no score and no levels.
1985–1986 Gerasimov, sixteen, ported it to Soviet IBM PC compatibles and added color; Dmitry Pavlovsky added a high-score table. Floppy disks spread one copy at a time through Moscow’s computer circles.
1989 Henk Rogers negotiated Nintendo’s handheld and home-console rights from the Soviet side. The Game Boy version went on sale in Japan on June 14, and in the United States on July 31 bundled with the console, and from then on it went around the world.

Sources: Wikipedia’s “Tetris” entry (citing The Guardian’s 2014 interview with Pajitnov); Jimmy Maher, “The Digital Antiquarian,” 2017-06-30 (filfre.net); NPR’s 2025 interview with Pajitnov. The motive for clearing lines comes from a secondhand overview, not his own words.

What it isOne knob: difficulty rests almost only on “speed”

Making the shapes “fall faster and faster” later became the difficulty method shared by all versions. How convenient this is: not a word of the rules changes and the player has nothing more to learn, yet turning a single number makes the same rules go from a leisurely stroll to a hundred-meter sprint.

For the player, this knob is also the easiest to understand. No need to read a manual: you know that “faster” means “harder,” and that this kind of hardness is felt through your own hands, cell by cell.

The figure below is this page’s setting: level 0 drops one cell every 800 milliseconds, each level multiplies the interval by 0.82, bottoming out at 70 milliseconds. (Level 0 of the Nintendo NES version is 48 frames per cell, which at 60 frames per second is exactly about 800 milliseconds; the curve after that is one this page set itself for demonstration, not the original’s numbers.)

The challenge rises along a line that keeps climbing, while the player’s skill has a ceiling. The curve has no top, so some day the challenge will exceed the skill.

This explains why it has no end. There is no “clearing the game” in it; the player is simply caught up, step by step, by ever faster pieces.

Mathematicians have even proved it: in her 1997 paper “How to Lose at Tetris,” Heidi Burgiel showed that if the piece sequence contains a long enough run of alternating S and Z pieces, the game must eventually end however you place them. Under purely random generation such a sequence is bound to appear sooner or later, so every game is a loss.

Failure is inevitable, which makes every game a contest of “how far can you hold out.”

A treadmill: only one knob, speed. The same person goes from a stroll to a sprint, and the machine itself hasn’t changed at all.
Snake: the difficulty in the game grows by itself: the more you eat, the longer the body, and the harder it is to go around yourself. Each bite the player takes turns their own knob.
A tennis ball machine: set the ball speed faster notch by notch, and reaction gets pushed to its limit bit by bit. The thing in daily life most like a “speed knob.”
A game that only speeds up: the lights flash faster and faster, and the play hasn’t changed at all. Late on, all that remains is a pure reaction test. The point of this counterexample: the speed knob works in Tetris because at every speed the placement choices stay interesting; speed with nothing to think about leaves only hand speed.
4

What it isOne bag: putting a bridle on luck

The order of the pieces is random, which is another reason it stays fresh. But randomness brings a problem: if every piece is drawn independently from 7 kinds, then a very long wait without the long piece I is entirely possible, and the player needs it precisely then.

People are poor at judging randomness. True randomness naturally contains long “droughts,” but the player’s feeling is only: the game is out to get me.

Designers thought of more than one fix. The NES version’s way was plain: if the piece drawn is the same as the last one, redraw once. This stops three identical pieces in a row but cannot stop a long bar from being missing for dozens of pieces. The later official design guideline’s way is the shuffle bag (7-bag): one of each of the 7 kinds goes into the bag, which is shuffled and dealt out, then a fresh bag is used. According to the Tetris Wiki, it was first used in The New Tetris, where the bag held 9 of each kind, 63 in all.

Shuffle bag: keeps the randomness and trims the extremes. The order within each bag is uncertain, but every kind of piece is certain to appear within 13 pieces.
A shuffled deck of cards: there is only one of each card in a deck, so you can’t draw five aces in a row. “Random” at the card table has always had memory.
“Shuffle” on a playlist: many players first shuffle the playlist and then play it in order, rather than drawing each song independently, so the same song won’t play twice in a row (a common approach; details vary by software).
Flipping coins in a row: a coin has no memory, and after five heads in a row the next flip is still fifty-fifty; it does not “make up for it.” Pure randomness won’t find balance for the player, which is why the designer has to do it for them.

Why it worksWhy does the four-line clear feel especially good

You have been saving up a three-high gap, waiting a dozen pieces, and the long piece finally arrives. It drops straight into the gap, four rows burst at once, and the score jumps by a big chunk. The thrill of this is something other line clears can’t match.

Look at the ledger first. At level 0, clearing 1 row scores 40, 2 rows 100, 3 rows 300 and 4 rows 1200 (the NES calculation, later multiplied by level plus one). If the score were proportional to rows, four rows would be 160. The actual is 1200, a full 7.5 times as much.

A reward better than expected, this “better than expected,” is in neuroscience reward prediction error. Chapter 2 covered gacha pulls, and this is the same kind of mechanism. The difference is that a gacha’s surprise is handed to the player by chance, while a Tetris surprise is something the player builds up layer by layer.

Dopamine computes “how far off,” not “how great it feels”

In the 1990s Wolfram Schultz and colleagues recorded dopamine neurons in monkey brains. They found: when an unexpected reward (a drop of juice, say) appeared, these neurons fired briefly. Once a signal could announce the reward in advance, the excitement shifted to the signal itself, and when the reward actually arrived it caused hardly a ripple. When an announced reward did not come, firing dropped below usual. This amounts to a ledger of “how wrong the prediction was.” It corresponds exactly to the error term used to update expectations in reinforcement-learning algorithms.

The lesson for designers: the size of a reward is not absolute, it depends on how far it lies from expectation. The same 1200 points, if you can clear four rows easily every time, are no longer special. For surprise to stay effective, the reward must be tied to something “not entirely certain.” (This is an extension from animal experiments to players, a reasonable inference, with no direct measurement on Tetris players. Also, whether dopamine is “pleasure” or a “learning signal” is not agreed by all researchers; the latter, more common view is taken here.)

Source: Wolfram Schultz, Peter Dayan & P. Read Montague, “A Neural Substrate of Prediction and Reward,” Science 275: 1593–1599 (1997).

Why it worksWhy do you want another game after losing

Every game of Tetris ends the same way: you lose. By common sense, a game that ends in failure every time should leave people frustrated. But the opposite is true: half a second after losing, the hand is already pressing “play again.”

The psychologist Daniel Kahneman and colleagues ran a famous experiment. Participants put one hand in 14°C cold water for 60 seconds, and another time for 90 seconds, except that in the last 30 seconds the water was quietly warmed by one degree, easing the pain slightly. Asked later which one they would repeat, most chose the longer one, though it clearly hurt 30 seconds more.

Their reading is: when people recall an experience, they hardly care how long it was and mainly look at two points: the most intense moment (the peak) and the moment it ended (the end). This rule of thumb later became the widely known peak-end rule.

Memory is not adding up the time

Applying this rule to Tetris allows one reading: although a game ends in failure, the last few dozen seconds before it are the tensest and most absorbing stretch of the whole game. And restarting costs almost nothing, so the bad ending has no time to leave an impression before the next game has started. This is only one interpretation using the peak-end rule, with no research on this game.

The rule itself is fairly solid: a 2022 meta-analysis pooling over a hundred effect sizes found that the peak and the end predict later evaluation strongly, but the overall average predicts about as well, with little difference between them, and duration has almost no influence. Memory does not add up by length; it looks at a few especially conspicuous moments.

Sources: Daniel Kahneman, Barbara Fredrickson, Charles Schreiber & Donald Redelmeier, “When More Pain Is Preferred to Less: Adding a Better End,” Psychological Science 4(6): 401–405 (1993); the meta-analysis by Alaybek et al., Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes 170 (2022).

One more: less is brainpower

The number of shapes counted in Experiment A is also a cognitive-load question. Cognitive load theory, proposed by John Sweller from 1988, says working memory can hold only very little at once. Seven shapes, three actions and no text tutorial mean a newcomer has almost nothing to memorize, and all the brainpower can go into “where does this piece go.” The difficulty has been moved to spatial reasoning and hand speed, not to understanding the rules.

Source: John Sweller, “Cognitive load during problem solving: Effects on learning,” Cognitive Science 12(2) (1988).

① Find your “main knob”

Difficulty relies on the single parameter of “speed,” easy to tune, easy to understand, easy to balance. In your own game, is there such a knob?

② Minimal rules, endless combinations

7 shapes × a dozen columns × 4 orientations, dozens of placements per step, and no “right answer” holds forever.

③ Give information, not answers

The next-piece preview and ghost piece only show the consequence, while the decision remains in the player’s hands, so the choice is meaningful.

④ Put a bridle on randomness

What players can’t stand is extreme luck, not randomness itself. Keep the surprise, cut off the extremes.

Common misconceptions

  • “Few rules means easy to make”: minimalism is the result of repeated polishing. Cutting twelve shapes to seven and putting the randomness into a bag, every step is a trade-off, and harder than adding rules.
  • “A case study shows the only correct way”: a loop with no “growth” suits it, but other games must be decided by their own circumstances.
  • “A psychological principle is a recipe for victory”: prediction error and the peak-end rule help form hypotheses, and in the end it still takes player playtests to verify them.
  • “The line-clear rule was Pajitnov’s explicit design intent”: this page cites a secondary overview, and his own words were not found.

Think about it: survivorship bias

Tetris is discussed again and again precisely because it succeeded. Many games with equally minimal rules and equally increasing speed are almost unknown, and nobody takes them as case studies. So “it did this, therefore it succeeded” may well be just survivorship bias, with timing, licensing and platform (the Game Boy pack-in game) equally responsible.

Key points

  • The key trade-off: changing from 12 five-cell shapes to 7 four-cell ones, balancing “enough variety” and “learned at a glance.”
  • Minimal mechanics grow rich dynamics; few rules do not mean much variety, and tic-tac-toe is the counterexample.
  • Difficulty rests almost only on one knob, “speed,” and the challenge keeps rising until it exceeds anyone’s skill, so the game has no end, only being caught.
  • The shuffle bag is a design for randomness: keep the surprise, cut off the extremes, with at most 12 pieces between two long bars.
  • The four-line clear’s high score produces a reward beyond expectation (prediction error). The tension at the end of a game and the cheap restart may make “one more game” feel natural (the peak-end rule, an interpretation).
Tetris uses a few rules, one speed knob and one shuffle bag, and draws on cognitive load, prediction error and the peak-end rule at once, but it is a successful case that has lasted, not a winning recipe.
