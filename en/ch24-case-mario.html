Chapter 24 · Case study

Super Mario 1-1

A tutorial without a single word taught hundreds of millions of players.

⏱ about 45 min

Learning goals

  • Explain why the opening of 1-1 puts just one Goomba, what the designers swapped out at the time, and why.
  • Take apart the teaching order of any level with “introduce → practice → vary,” and explain with the zone of proximal development and scaffolding why it teaches.
  • Say what forgiving feel and cheap failure each protect in the player, and know which statements are the designers’ recollections and which are only our reading afterward.
1

What it isA first level without a single word

Someone playing Super Mario Bros. for the first time sees not a word: no “press A to jump,” no “stomp enemies,” and nobody tells you to go right. Yet within a minute, most people have learned to run, jump, stomp and bump bricks.

This is Nintendo’s 1985 work, generally credited as designed mainly by Shigeru Miyamoto and Takashi Tezuka, with music by Koji Kondo. Its first level, 1-1, became the most-cited model of “teaching without text” in game design. What exactly did it do?

This is a seconds-long micro loop: every jump is one “action–feedback,” repeated dozens of times in a level.

Look at the very first screen after the game starts. You, a stretch of flat ground, a question-mark block overhead, and a Goomba walking toward you in the distance. There is nothing else: no second threat, no second thing to learn.

This screen does just one thing: it sets the concept of “enemy” alone in front of you. You have only two paths: run into it, or jump over it or onto it. Whichever you take, you understand at once: this thing hurts, and a stomp flattens it.

1-1 teaches the player in three ways. The first is what you just saw: introduce alone first, then combine.

Mario: the opening has only one Goomba and no other threat beside it. Only later come combinations like “pipe + enemy” and “pit + platform.”
Learning to drive: practice starting and stopping on an empty lot first, then merge and change lanes on the road. Nobody throws you into rush hour on day one.
Learning the piano: master one hand first, then play with both. Practicing both hands at once is learning two new things at once.
The “tutorial wall”: ten pop-up boxes in the first minute, eight systems explained at once. With so much information, the player remembers nothing, and might as well just play. The point of this counterexample: not teaching too little, but teaching too much at once.

How it was inventedWith no text, how do you teach a first-time player?

1985: plug in the cartridge and play, and few opened the manual

Home consoles then had no such thing as “onboarding.” Players got a cartridge and started playing, and the manual might lie in a drawer for a lifetime. The designers faced a very real problem: at the start of the first level the player knows nothing, so how do you, without writing a word, get them to learn to run, jump, stomp enemies and bump bricks?

More interesting, according to Takashi Tezuka’s recollection, 1-1 was not made first, but fixed in the last stage of development to balance the difficulty of the whole game. That is, this “teaching model” was tuned again and again, not a single flash of inspiration.

    Below are several public accounts of the design process of 1-1. They come from interviews with Miyamoto and Tezuka around 2015, some retold through the media, so take them as the designers’ own recollections, not word-for-word history:

    Graph paper Tezuka said levels were first drawn on graph paper and handed to the programmers, with a playable version coming back only the next day, “a slow turnaround.” Small changes were made by laying tracing paper over the original, drawn and handed back in. Trial and error was costly, so every encounter had to be thought through beforehand.
    The first enemy According to a report of a 2015 Eurogamer interview, Miyamoto said they first wanted a Koopa (a turtle) as the opening enemy, then felt that for a first-time player “jump on it and kick it away” was a bit too hard. A shell is not dealt with by a single stomp, so they made a “bad mushroom” instead, the Goomba.
    The mushroom According to a report of the same interview, Miyamoto wanted the player to feel the reaction of “Mario getting big,” and getting big needed a power-up, hence the Super Mushroom. It looks a bit like the Goomba, and newcomers often dodge it; but the low bricks overhead make it hard not to touch, and one touch makes you big, and only then do you understand “it’s a good thing.”
    Learning as you play Miyamoto said the purpose of the first level is to let players “learn what this game is about”; he wanted players to grasp the rules while doing the things they would naturally do, with no tutorial. He also said one line: once players understand what to do, “it becomes their game.”
    Repeated playtesting Tezuka (2015 Sankei Shimbun interview) said levels almost never succeed the first time, “people rarely play the way you intended,” so he watches from the side and then adjusts; when designers make levels themselves, because they know the answer, “they inevitably make them too hard.”

    Sources: Game Revolution’s quotation of Miyamoto’s Eurogamer interview; GIGAZINE (2015-09-14, retelling the Eurogamer interview); SoraNews24 (2015-09-09); Tezuka’s Sankei Shimbun interview (2015-11-30, English translation by shmuplations). Retold content may differ slightly from the original words, and only the parts on which the sources agree are taken here.

    What it isThe second way: give controls some forgiveness, so the hands have weight

    Teaching alone isn’t enough; the controls themselves must also be trustworthy. When you press the jump key, the character should jump when you want, as high as you want. If it is off by a little, the player doesn’t feel “I need more practice,” only “it doesn’t listen to me.”

    According to GIGAZINE’s retelling of Miyamoto, he felt the earlier Donkey Kong had “low freedom,” so he let Mario move in the air after jumping; the “slide” while running was to give Mario a sense of weight. Jumping with a running start goes farther, and holding the jump key goes higher (variable jump height), and these later became standard in platformers.

    Many such games add a more hidden layer of forgiveness: for a short time just after walking off a platform edge you can still jump, called coyote time; pressing jump an instant before landing and jumping as soon as you land is called input buffering. Which of these the original Mario used exactly, the community accounts differ, so no conclusion is drawn here; the mini level on the page makes them switches so you can feel them yourself.

    Coyote time: for a short time after stepping off the edge you can still jump. You won’t notice it exists, and only feel “I definitely pressed it right, and it did jump” (the mini level below lets you switch it on and off).
    Input buffering: pressing jump an instant before landing, then jumping right on landing. The hand is a little faster than the eye, and the game remembers the intention for you.
    A phone keyboard’s error tolerance: if your finger lands a little off, you still type the letter you wanted. The same idea in life: don’t punish “almost.”
    Frame-perfect, zero tolerance: one frame off counts as failure, and the player feels “it isn’t my fault” rather than “I should practice.” The point of this counterexample: forgiveness isn’t lowering difficulty, it is making the controls match the intention.
    5

    What it isThe third way: make failure cheap

    The third way is the least conspicuous and the most crucial. In 1-1 you will certainly fail several times: bumped by a Goomba, dropped into a pit. But a few seconds later you are standing near the start again, with your hand already on the jump key.

    The cheaper failure is, the more the player dares to try jumps they are unsure of, and those “unsure tries” are exactly the main way they learn the game.

    Mario: fall in a pit or get hit, and you restart within seconds, with no need to rewatch an opening or walk a long road.
    Learning to swim: splash about in the shallow end first, and you don’t go into deep water right away. The shallow end is a protected place for trial and error.
    Writing a draft: you can scribble freely on scratch paper, with no pressure that “one mistake voids the whole book.” So you dare to write sentences you haven’t thought through.
    Half an hour to redo after failing: each death means a long walk before you can try again, and the player chooses “not to take risks.” The point of this counterexample: not failure itself, but the price of failure decides whether people dare to learn.
    6

    Why it worksWhy does climbing one step at a time teach

    Look back at the pipes: first short, then medium, then tall. Why not put the tallest first? Because the player’s question is never “can I jump?” but “can I clear it?” A short pipe he clears at once, and confidence is built; a medium pipe he must hold longer, and he learns something new; the tall pipe needs everything accumulated before. Each step asks a little more than what was just learned, and not so much more that it can’t be jumped.

    Educational psychologists call this “a little more” range the zone of proximal development: what a learner can’t yet do alone but can reach with a helping hand. Too close, and there is nothing to learn; too far, and only frustration is left.

    The “helping hand” in 1-1 is not a teacher but the level itself: the lonely first enemy, the flat runway, the pipes growing taller one notch at a time. They are like scaffolding built beside a wall, taken away bit by bit as you get stronger.

    Who said it: Vygotsky and scaffolding

    The zone of proximal development was proposed by the Soviet psychologist Lev Vygotsky, whose ideas were written mainly in the 1930s and later collected in Mind in Society (1978). The “scaffolding” metaphor comes from another body of work: in 1976 David Wood, Jerome Bruner and Gail Ross observed how tutors guided children through a building task, helping with the parts the child temporarily couldn’t do and gradually withdrawing as the child improved.

    Note that moving this educational psychology onto game levels is an analogy, not strict experimental evidence. The zone of proximal development is itself a rather broad concept, often loosely cited in education, and how to measure it exactly is still debated.

    Sources: L. S. Vygotsky, Mind in Society (1978); David Wood, Jerome S. Bruner & Gail Ross, “The role of tutoring in problem solving,” Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry 17(2): 89–100 (1976).

    Why it worksWhy cheap failure makes people dare to learn

    In reality, learning often costs you for failing: a bad exam leaves a transcript, a slip leaves consequences. So people are naturally conservative in reality and pick the safe road. Games can rewrite this account: fall in a pit and it costs a few seconds at most.

    The education scholar James Paul Gee, in What Video Games Have to Teach Us About Learning and Literacy (2003), listed this as one of the learning principles of good games, using the psychologist Erikson’s term “psychosocial moratorium”: learners can take risks in a space where the consequences are turned down. His point is that bold attempts are themselves learning.

    Gee’s argument and its limits

    Gee’s argument comes from his observation and theoretical thinking about game learning environments, not from controlled experiments. “Lowering the cost of failure helps learning” also has its range of application: it speaks of teaching and the beginner stage; for games that pursue high-risk thrills, high stakes are exactly the source of the fun, and other trade-offs apply. In 1-1 retrying is fast, which fits this principle of his.

    Source: James Paul Gee, What Video Games Have to Teach Us About Learning and Literacy (2003), on “psychosocial moratorium” (the concept comes from Erik Erikson).

    Jump height is roughly estimated as “strength² ÷ (2 × gravity),” in units of one brick; with variable jump height off, a tap and a hold are equally high.

    Try it: ① with “Forgiving feel” on, deliberately jump a little late at a platform edge until the “Forgiveness saves” counter shows a number; ② turn off “Variable jump height” and go stomp the first Goomba: is it harder to fine-tune where you land?

    ① Safety first, then danger

    In a space without pressure, let the player learn the first move through their own trial and error. The lower the cost of failure, the bolder the learning.

    ② Let the environment teach

    Teach with layout, position and enemy behavior, not pop-ups. What players find themselves is remembered more firmly.

    ③ One idea, three ways of saying it

    Introduce → practice → vary. The same “jump” appears first alone, then again and again with different heights and different risks.

    ④ Feel is an ensemble of parameters

    Acceleration, inertia, variable jump height, forgiveness time… each is subtle, and stacked together they are “jumping smoothly.”

    Common misconceptions

    • “1-1 is the one right answer to teaching”: it is just one effective approach; text tutorials and coach-style hints work equally well in other games.
    • “Teaching without text means giving no hints at all”: 1-1’s hints are hidden in the layout, and the design work is not small; and, according to the designers, it was tuned through repeated playtesting.
    • “Forgiving feel means lowering difficulty”: it makes the controls more “responsive,” and the difficulty is still in the level.
    • “Applying the principles equals understanding the design”: the zone of proximal development and psychosocial moratorium are explanations after the fact, and we are not sure the designers thought that way at the time. The real test is to see how players play.

    Think about it: survivorship bias

    1-1 is cited countless times because Super Mario Bros. was a huge success. We rarely see the games that used similar “teaching without text” and remained unknown. So “it taught this way and so it succeeded” may be only survivorship bias: success also has hardware timing, brand, overall quality and many other factors.

    Write a sentence: in your own game, which design that “copies 1-1” might not suit it? Why?

    Key points

    • 1-1 teaches through designed situations, not text: introduce safely, practice repeatedly, then add variation.
    • The key decision: the first enemy should be “one stomp and done” (according to Miyamoto’s recollection, the turtle was given up for this) and should appear alone; the mushroom comes out of an overhead block so newcomers can’t dodge it.
    • Difficulty should climb along the player’s zone of proximal development; the level itself is the scaffolding.
    • Feel is a set of parameters: inertia, gravity, variable jump height, forgiveness time; the point of forgiveness is to make the controls match the intention.
    • Cheap failure makes players dare to try; and difficulty has to be tuned by watching players play, because the designer knows the answer.
    1-1 builds scaffolding with the level itself, gives the controls forgiveness and makes failure cheap, so it teaches without a word, but it is only a much-discussed surviving case.
