Chapter 1

What Is a Game?

First, what are we actually making? And why has such a simple-looking question stumped a whole line of philosophers?

Learning goals

about 40 min
  • Use four pillars (goal, rules, feedback, and voluntary participation) to decide whether an activity counts as a game, and say which pillar is missing.
  • Explain why “game” still has no agreed definition, and how philosophers have argued about it.
  • Explain why rules deliberately make things harder, why children and animals play at all, and sort games by four sources of fun.
1

What it isFour pillars: what holds a game up

Start with a game everyone has played: hide-and-seek. One person closes their eyes and counts to ten while the others scatter and hide. The rules are so simple they need no explaining, yet a group of kids can run around all afternoon. What makes it so good?

Take it apart and four things turn up. The seeker has to find everybody and the hiders have to stay unfound: that is the goal. Nobody leaves the yard and nobody peeks while counting: those are the rules. A shriek when you get tagged, a pounding heart when you slip past, and at every moment you know whether you are winning or losing: that is feedback. And the last one may matter most: anyone who gets tired can say “I’m out,” and that is voluntary participation.

Designers like to call these four things the “four pillars” of a game. It is not a definition handed down by some scholar, just a handy pair of glasses. Pick any activity and count its pillars; the missing one usually decides what the activity turns into.

🚩

Goal

Where you are headed and what you are trying to get done. Without one, it is just a toy.

📏

Rules

What you can do and what you can’t. Rules create difficulty, and difficulty is what makes success feel earned.

💡

Feedback

The game’s answer to every move: did that bring you closer, or farther away?

🤝

Voluntary participation

You came in because you wanted to, and you can walk out any time. Being forced takes the fun out.

📊 The four pillars side by side: who has what

The missing pillar decides what it becomes: without a goal it is a toy; without voluntary participation it is a chore.

Hide-and-seek: the goal is not to be found, the rule is that nobody leaves the yard, being found or not is the feedback, and anyone can say “I’m out.” All four pillars stand in a neat row.
Tidying a room, turned into a game: it is the same tidying, but add “get the floor visible in 10 minutes, and give yourself a star for every area you clear,” and goal, rules and feedback are all there. Plenty of people find they suddenly mind it less. Even small chores can have the four pillars “installed.”
Tetris: the goal is to clear lines and score, the rules are that pieces can only fall and rotate, and the feedback is sound, line clears, and a speed that keeps climbing. All four pillars are present and each one is crystal clear.
Playing with blocks: you play because you want to, and when the tower falls it clatters (feedback), but there is no goal and no rule. Two pillars are missing. The moment someone says “let’s see who builds the tallest, and a fall means you lose,” it becomes a game.
Homework you were told to do: it has a goal, rules, and score feedback, but you had no choice. Just one pillar is missing, and the flavor changes from “game” to “task.”
Watching a movie: you chose it and you enjoy it, but there is nothing to achieve, no rule limits you, and nothing you do can change the plot. It is enjoyment, not a game.

What it isFour kinds of fun: where does the pleasure come from?

The four pillars tell us what a game is made of, but not why it makes people happy. Chess and a merry-go-round are both called “play,” yet the first is fun because you win and the second because you get dizzy, which have almost nothing in common. In Les jeux et les hommes (1958; Man, Play and Games), the French scholar Roger Caillois asked a different question: not what a game is, but where people get their pleasure. He found only four answers.

Competition

Measuring yourself against others, with skill deciding who wins.
Chess, football, races

Chance

Handing the result over to fate.
Dice, drawing lots, gacha pulls

Make-believe

Pretending to be someone else, or stepping into another world.
Playing house, role-playing

Vertigo

Chasing a moment of dizzy, out-of-control thrill.
Merry-go-rounds, roller coasters

Most games, as it turns out, are mixtures. In Teamfight Tactics you have to work out a lineup strategy and also depend on which cards you draw; in a tabletop role-playing game you play a character, and the dice decide whether you succeed. Caillois also drew a horizontal axis: at one end is free, improvised “frolic” (paidia), and at the other is “contest” with strict rules (ludus). The same kind of fun can sit anywhere along this axis.

Teamfight Tactics: building a good lineup is competition, and the luck of the card refresh is chance. One game holds two kinds of fun.
Tabletop role-playing: you play a character (make-believe), and the dice decide whether your actions succeed (chance).
Which kind is a management sim? Caillois built his categories for traditional games, so video games fit a little awkwardly. Is building a city “pretending to be the mayor,” or “wrestling with yourself”? Different people will answer differently, and that is perfectly normal.

Source: Roger Caillois, Les jeux et les hommes (1958). His ideas were inspired by, and also argue with, the Dutch scholar Johan Huizinga’s Homo Ludens (1938).

How it was inventedWhy is “game” so hard to pin down?

Everybody can play, yet nobody can say what it is

It sounds a bit absurd. Nobody ever taught you, but you know hide-and-seek is a game and a final exam is not. Yet if someone asked you to write “game” down in one sentence, you would probably get stuck.

The twentieth-century philosopher Wittgenstein tripped over exactly this problem. Our intuition says a word can point to many things because they share a common essence, the way every triangle has three sides. So what about “game”? There ought to be one thread running through all games.

You probably noticed it: every “must” you add wrongly throws out some perfectly good game, and without it everything gets let in. This is the trap philosophers have been in for decades. Here is how they got through it, step by step:

1938 The Dutch historian Huizinga argued in Homo Ludens that play is not a by-product of civilization but one of its sources. He also noticed something: once people start to play, it is as if a circle has been drawn on reality. Inside it a different set of rules applies, and outside everything returns to normal. He called it the “magic circle.” But his account reads more like a checklist of features, and it never pins down exactly where the circle is drawn.
1953 Wittgenstein ran into the same trouble, but his method was unusual: don’t “think,” look. He laid out board games, card games, ball games, the Olympics, and asked what they have in common. Winning and losing? A child bouncing a ball off a wall and catching it has no winner. An opponent? Solitaire needs none. Always fun? Not necessarily. Searching and searching, he found only a web of similarities: A resembles B, B resembles C, yet A and C may have nothing in common. He called it family resemblance: in a family, one person has Dad’s eyes and another has Mom’s nose, but no one feature is shared by the whole family.
1958 Caillois simply took another road. If no common feature can be found, stop asking “what is it?” and ask “where is the fun?” Sorting games into four families by the source of pleasure gives the four types you saw earlier.
1978 Not everyone was convinced. The philosopher Bernard Suits thought Wittgenstein had “looked too quickly”: games can be defined, and one thread is enough. His version: to play a game is to voluntarily attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles. He put the idea in a quirky, delightful book called The Grasshopper.
Today The argument is not over. Game designers took the practical route: stop chasing the ultimate definition and keep a handy tool, which is the four pillars from the start. They work well on core cases, and when they break down on an edge case, that is often a hint that an interesting design space is hiding there.

🕸️ Family resemblance: no single thread runs through every member

The labels on the lines are the similarities between neighboring games. It is a sketch, not a strict classification.

“Family resemblance” in daily life: what is a “chair”? A three-legged stool, a squashy sofa, a swing, an office swivel chair… it is hard to find one feature that all chairs share, yet you almost never get it wrong. “Game” is a word like that.

Suits’s reply: treat “unnecessary obstacles” as the heart of a game

Suits thought Wittgenstein gave up too early. After looking closely at many games, he found they do share a structure. First there is a goal you could state even without the rules (say, getting a ball into a hole). Then rules block off the easiest route (no walking over and dropping it in; you must use a club). Finally, the players are willing to accept these limits, an attitude he called the lusory attitude. Put together: to play a game is to voluntarily try to overcome unnecessary obstacles.

Compare that with the four pillars and it lines up: his “prelusory goal” is the goal, his “rules” are the rules, and his “lusory attitude” is voluntary participation. Interestingly, “feedback” does not appear in his definition at all.

Not everyone agrees, of course. A professional footballer who plays for a salary: is he “playing”? Suits’s definition has trouble answering that, and the debate has never been settled.

Sources: Ludwig Wittgenstein, Philosophical Investigations (1953), §§66–67; Bernard Suits, The Grasshopper: Games, Life and Utopia (1978); Johan Huizinga, Homo Ludens (1938).

Why it worksWhy do rules make things hard on purpose?

The most intriguing words in Suits’s definition are “unnecessary obstacles.” You could take a cable car up the mountain, and in golf you could simply carry the ball over and drop it in the hole, yet the rules block the easy route on purpose. Isn’t that just meddling?

Think about it: if golf let you walk the ball to the hole and put it in, it would be over in a second and you would feel no sense of achievement at all. It is the inconvenience of “clubs only” that turns “getting it in the hole” into something that takes skill and patience, so that at the moment you succeed you think “I did it.” Obstacles are not the enemy of a game; they are its engine.

But the obstacles have to be accepted willingly

Climbing stairs as a punishment is drudgery, while choosing to climb them for exercise is a little game. The only difference is whether you had a choice. That is the weight of the “voluntary” pillar: it turns the very same obstacle from torment into fun.

Source: Bernard Suits, The Grasshopper (1978). This is Suits’s theory. It explains why games need rules, but it does not say which obstacles are the most fun.
Golf: the rules forbid carrying the ball to the hole; you may only hit it with a club. The easy route is blocked, so getting it in the hole means something.
Climbing in The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild: you could always walk around, but “climbing up by hand,” limited by the stamina bar, becomes a challenge that feels rewarding.
Choosing the stairs: for the exercise, you give up the elevator, the easier option, and climbing becomes a little game of counting floors and watching the clock.
A tedious approval process at work: it is a limit too, yet nobody finds it fun. Nobody asked for your consent, and it was not built to give you an interesting challenge.

Why it worksWhy do people and animals “play”?

A kitten pounces on a ball of yarn, and having caught it does not eat it. Lion cubs wrestle, but their bites are gentle. They are not “working,” yet these moves look almost exactly like adult hunting and fighting.

Why would nature let young animals spend so much of their time on things that seem useless?

Play is a “safe practice ground”

One influential view is that play lets the young rehearse, in an environment where failure costs very little, skills they will need later. The German scholar Karl Groos set out this “practice theory” systematically in Die Spiele der Tiere (1896) and Die Spiele der Menschen (1899). Missing the yarn ball once will not starve a kitten, but real prey gives only one chance.

Later researchers added an interesting detail: when animals play they often handicap themselves, with the stronger one holding back for the weaker one, and even deliberately create small mishaps. So play may be training not just specific moves but a flexible way of coping with the unexpected. (This is a view put forward in a 2001 review.)

That also shows why rules and feedback matter so much in games: they turn processes that are costly in real life into low-risk versions you can try again and again and get an immediate answer from.

Sources: Karl Groos, The Play of Animals (1896) and The Play of Man (1899); Marek Špinka, Ruth Newberry and Marc Bekoff, “Mammalian play: training for the unexpected,” The Quarterly Review of Biology 76(2): 141–168 (2001). The function of play is still debated, and practice theory is only one of the proposals.
A kitten pouncing on yarn: chasing and pouncing rehearse hunting moves, and a miss costs no meal.
Lion cubs wrestling: the bites are gentle, because what is being learned is “use full force but know when to ease off,” and where to stop.
Kids playing house and hide-and-seek: practicing roles, negotiating who does what, and guessing what others are thinking.
Play that doesn’t quite fit: purely recreational activity in adults, and play where it is hard to see what skill is being practiced. That is why practice theory explains only part of play, and why researchers suspect play may have several functions, perhaps even some that are by-products.

Common misconceptions

  • “Anything with rules is a game”: traffic laws and company policies have rules too. What counts is that the rules exist to create an interesting challenge, and that you accept them willingly.
  • “A game must have winning and losing”: sandboxes, interactive stories and management sims can have no clear win or loss, and players can set their own goals. You already tried this in the definition tester.
  • “Play is only for kids”: adults play too, practicing skills and building relationships in a low-risk setting, just in different forms.
  • “The four pillars are the definition of a game”: they are a handy tool, not a definition; real edge cases will make them break down.

Reflection and debate

“Can a game be defined at all?” is itself an unfinished argument: Wittgenstein’s camp says there is only family resemblance; Suits’s camp says a definition is possible; and some philosophers think the idea of family resemblance is itself problematic. Caillois’s four-way split is likewise just one view. The “practice theory” of play is the same: it is intuitive, but it cannot explain why adults play.

Try thinking of an example that breaks the four pillars, such as a management sandbox with no goal, or a virtual pet. Then ask: if you had to design one, how would you go about it?

Key points

  • Games are often broken into four pillars: goal, rules, feedback, voluntary participation. Designing a game means designing how these four work together. Whichever is missing decides what you get: no goal gives a toy, no choice gives a chore.
  • “Game” is hard to define because it is a family-resemblance concept: no single feature runs through all games (Wittgenstein).
  • Suits’s answer: a game is the voluntary overcoming of unnecessary obstacles. Rules create the obstacles on purpose, and voluntary participation turns them from torment into fun.
  • The four pillars are a tool, not a definition: they work on core cases and fail on edge cases.
  • Play can be seen as a low-risk practice ground; the pleasure of games comes roughly from four sources, competition, chance, make-believe and vertigo, and most games are mixtures.
A game is a low-risk rehearsal where you willingly accept rules, head for a goal, and keep getting answers back; the four pillars are useful, but they are a tool, not a definition.
