Chapter 1

What Is a Game?

Everyone uses the word “game,” yet it has stumped a good many philosophers.

Learning goals

about 40 min
  • Use the four defining traits of a game (goal, rules, feedback system, voluntary participation) to judge whether an activity is a game, and say which trait it lacks.
  • Explain why “game” still has no agreed definition, and how Wittgenstein and Suits each thought about it.
  • Explain why rules deliberately make things harder, why children and young animals “play,” and sort games with Caillois’s four kinds of pleasure.
1

What it isThe four defining traits: goal, rules, feedback system, voluntary participation

Five children are playing hide-and-seek in a yard. Xiaoyu faces the locust tree and counts to ten, then turns around. Xiaomi is behind a drying bedsheet with a pair of sneakers sticking out. Ajie is crouching beside the trash can and is found at a glance. He yells “Oh no!”, and the next round he does the counting.

It was an ordinary round, and yet it had everything a game needs. In Reality Is Broken (2011), Jane McGonigal points out that all games share four traits: goal, rules, feedback system, voluntary participation.

Concept card

The four defining traits of a game

Goal, rules, feedback system, voluntary participation

Jane McGonigal · Reality Is Broken, 2011

Every game has a goal, rules, a feedback system and voluntary participation. Take one away and the activity turns into something else.

ExampleHide-and-seek: the seeker must find everyone (goal), no peeking while counting (rules), being found tells you that you lost (feedback), and anyone who gets tired can quit (voluntary).

Take one trait away and hide-and-seek changes. If a grown-up ruled that Xiaoyu “must be the seeker today and can’t go home until everyone is found,” the same rules would become a chore.

🚩

Goal

What the game is trying to get done: the seeker must find everyone, the others must stay hidden to the end. Without a goal, it is just a toy.

📏

Rules

No peeking while counting, no hiding outside the yard. Rules create difficulty, and difficulty brings a sense of achievement.

💡

Feedback

Being found brings a shout of “got you!”, and not being found means watching the seeker walk away. At every moment you know whether you are winning or losing.

🤝

Voluntary

Tired players can say “I’m out” and walk away. Forced to play, it becomes a task.

📊 The four traits compared: who has them, who doesn’t

A missing trait changes what the activity becomes: without a goal it is more a toy, without voluntary participation more a task.

Hide-and-seek: the goal is not to be found, and the rule is no leaving the yard. Whether you have been found is the feedback, and anyone who wants out can shout “I’m out.” All four are there.
Tidying up turned into a game: it is the same room, but add “make the floor visible in 10 minutes, and reward yourself with a star for each cleared area,” and goal, rules and feedback are all there. Many people find they suddenly mind it less.
Tetris: the goal is to clear lines for points, the rule is that blocks can only fall and rotate, and the feedback is sound, cleared lines and an ever faster speed. All four are clear.
Playing with blocks at random: you play by choice, and the tumbling blocks make a clatter (feedback), but there is no goal and no rules, two traits short. Once someone says “let’s see who builds highest, and the one who knocks it over loses,” it becomes a game at once.
Assigned homework: there is a goal, there are rules, there is score feedback, but no choice. With only voluntary participation missing, it goes from “game” to “task.”
Watching a movie: voluntary and enjoyable, but nothing needs to be achieved, no rules limit you, and nothing you do changes the plot. That is enjoyment, not a game.

What it isFour kinds of fun: where does the pleasure come from?

The four traits say what a game is made of, but not why it makes people happy. The joy of chess is in winning and the joy of the merry-go-round is in getting dizzy, which have almost nothing in common, and both count as “play.” In Man, Play and Games (1958), the French scholar Roger Caillois asked the question differently: where do people get their pleasure? He found four answers.

Concept card

Caillois’s four categories of games

Agôn, alea, mimicry, ilinx

Roger Caillois · Man, Play and Games, 1958

Games sorted by the source of their pleasure: competition, chance, simulation and vertigo.

ExampleChess runs on competition, dice on chance, playing house on make-believe, and roller coasters on vertigo.

Competition

Measuring yourself against others, with skill deciding who wins.
Chess, football, races

Chance

Handing the result over to fate.
Dice, drawing lots, gacha pulls

Make-believe

Pretending to be someone else, or stepping into another world.
Playing house, role-playing

Vertigo

Chasing a moment of dizzy, out-of-control thrill.
Merry-go-rounds, roller coasters

Most games are mixed. Teamfight Tactics demands lineup strategy and also depends on which cards you draw. In a tabletop role-playing game, players act out characters and rely on dice to decide success or failure. Caillois also drew a horizontal axis: at one end, free and improvised “frolic” (paidia), at the other, rule-bound “contest” (ludus). The same kind of pleasure can sit anywhere along this axis.

The positions of the kinds of play on the axis are illustrative, not precisely measured.

Teamfight Tactics: matching lineups is competition, and the luck of what your draws refresh is chance. One game holds two kinds of pleasure.
Tabletop role-playing: you play a character (make-believe), and the dice decide whether your actions succeed (chance).
Which kind is a management sim? Caillois built his categories for traditional games, so video games fit a little awkwardly. Is building a city “pretending to be the mayor,” or “wrestling with yourself”? Different people will answer differently, and that is perfectly normal.

Source: Roger Caillois, Les jeux et les hommes (1958). His ideas were inspired by, and also argue with, the Dutch scholar Johan Huizinga’s Homo Ludens (1938).

How it was inventedWhy is “game” so hard to pin down?

Everybody can play, yet nobody can say what it is

Nobody taught us, yet everyone knows hide-and-seek is a game and a final exam is not. But ask people to put “game” into one sentence and they will probably get stuck.

The twentieth-century philosopher Wittgenstein stumbled over just this. By intuition, a word can point to many things because they share a common essence, the way all triangles have three sides. Then “game” should have a line that all games share.

Every “must” added wipes out some perfectly decent game, and with none added, everything gets let in. Philosophers were stuck right here for decades. These are some of their attempts.

1938 In Homo Ludens, the Dutch historian Huizinga proposed that games are one of the sources of civilization, not a by-product of it. He also noticed that once play begins, everyone acts as if a circle has been drawn in reality: inside the circle there is another set of rules, and outside it everything returns to normal. He called this circle the “magic circle.” But his description reads more like a list of features, and where exactly the circle is drawn he did not settle.
1953 Wittgenstein met the same trouble, and his way was unusual: don’t “think,” first “look.” He laid out board games, card games, ball games and the Olympics and asked: what do they have in common? Winning and losing? But a child tossing a ball against a wall and catching it has no winner. An opponent? Solitaire needs none. Always fun? Not always. Searching and searching, he found only a web of resemblances: A resembles B, B resembles C, yet A and C may have nothing in common. It is much like a family: the eyes look like Dad’s and the nose like Mom’s, yet no single feature is shared by everyone. Wittgenstein called this relation family resemblance.
1958 Caillois took another road. Since no common point could be found, he stopped asking “what is it” and asked “where is the fun,” sorting games into four families by the source of pleasure, the four kinds seen earlier.
1978 The philosopher Bernard Suits thought Wittgenstein had given up too early. In his view a game can be defined, and grasping one thing is enough. His version: to play a game is to voluntarily attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles. He put the idea in a strange and delightful book called The Grasshopper.
Today The argument is not over. Game designers mostly sidestep the ultimate definition and use a practical checklist. The four traits McGonigal wrote down in 2011 are the checklist designers commonly use now. It works well on typical games such as hide-and-seek and Tetris, and fits less well on edge cases such as sandboxes and virtual pets.
Concept card

Family resemblance

Family resemblance (German: Familienähnlichkeit)

Ludwig Wittgenstein · Philosophical Investigations, 1953

Some concepts have no single feature shared by all members, only a web of mutual resemblance: A resembles B, B resembles C, and A and C can look nothing alike.

ExampleBoard games have winners, but tossing a ball against a wall has none. Solitaire can be played alone, but football needs two teams. All of them count as “games,” yet no feature is shared by every member.

🕸️ Family resemblance: no single thread runs through every member

The labels on the lines are the similarities between neighboring games. It is a sketch, not a strict classification.

“Family resemblance” in daily life: what is a “chair”? A three-legged stool, a squashy sofa, a swing, an office swivel chair… it is hard to find one feature all chairs share, and yet we almost never get it wrong. “Game” is a word like that.

Suits’s reply: treat “unnecessary obstacles” as the heart of a game

Suits looked closely at many games and found a shared structure. First there is a goal that can be stated without the rules, such as getting the ball into a hole. Then the rules block the easiest route: no walking over and putting it in, only striking it with a club. Players are willing to accept these limits, an attitude Suits called the lusory attitude. His definition is made of these three: to play a game is to voluntarily attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles.

Mapped onto the four traits, his “prelusory goal” is the goal, the “rules” are the rules, and the “lusory attitude” is voluntary participation. His definition has no item for feedback.

Some disagree. Does a professional footballer who plays for a salary count as “playing”? Suits’s definition has trouble answering that.

Sources: Wittgenstein, 1953, Philosophical Investigations, §§66–67, which uses “game” to explain family resemblance. Suits, 1978, The Grasshopper: Games, Life and Utopia, which proposes a definition of games. Huizinga, 1938, Homo Ludens.

Why it worksWhy do rules make things hard on purpose?

You could take a cable car up the mountain, and in golf you could simply carry the ball to the hole and drop it in. Yet the rules block the easy route on purpose, and what is left is what Suits called “unnecessary obstacles.”

Imagine golf allowing you to carry the ball to the hole and drop it in. It would be done in a second, and nobody would feel any achievement. It is the inconvenience of “only with a club” that makes getting the ball in a matter of skill and patience. Only when the ball drops in does the golfer think: I did it.

But the obstacles have to be accepted willingly

Climbing stairs as a punishment is drudgery, while choosing to climb them for exercise is a small game. The difference is whether you have a choice.

Source: Suits, 1978, The Grasshopper. He proposed that a game is the voluntary overcoming of unnecessary obstacles, which explains why games need rules.
Golf: the rules forbid carrying the ball to the hole and allow only striking it with a club. The easy road is blocked, and so getting it in means something.
Climbing in The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild: you could perfectly well walk around. But climbing up bare-handed means watching a stamina bar, picking a route and finding a place to rest, and reaching the top brings a sense of achievement.
Choosing the stairs: for exercise, you voluntarily give up the elevator, the easier means, and climbing becomes a small game: counting floors and watching the clock.
The tangle of approvals in a company: limits too, yet nobody finds them fun. Nobody asked for consent, and it wasn’t made to create an interesting challenge.

Why it worksWhy do people and animals “play”?

A kitten pounces hard on a ball of yarn and doesn’t eat it when it gets it. Young lions wrestle, biting only gently. Their movements are almost the same as hunting and fighting in adulthood, yet there is no real prey and no real injury.

Young animals spend a great deal of time on things that seem useless, and there must be a reason. Animal behaviorists have offered several explanations, and one of them has been very influential.

Play is a “safe practice ground”

The German scholar Karl Groos proposed the “practice theory” in The Play of Animals (1896) and The Play of Man (1899). In his view, play lets the young rehearse the skills they will need later in an environment where failure is cheap. Missing the yarn ball once won’t starve a kitten, while real prey gives only one chance.

Later researchers added a detail. When animals play, they handicap themselves on purpose, the stronger one holding back for the weaker, and they deliberately create small mishaps. A 2001 review took this to mean that play also trains “how to cope with the unexpected.”

The rules and feedback in games do the same job: they turn processes that are costly in reality into low-risk versions that can be tried again and again, with an instant response.

Sources: Karl Groos, The Play of Animals (1896) and The Play of Man (1899), which proposed that play is practice for later skills. Špinka, Newberry and Bekoff, “Mammalian play: training for the unexpected,” Quarterly Review of Biology 76(2): 141–168 (2001), a review of how play relates to coping with the unexpected. The function of play is still debated, and practice theory is only one account.
A kitten pouncing on yarn: chasing and pouncing rehearse the moves of hunting, and missing doesn’t mean going hungry.
Lion cubs wrestling: the bites are gentle, because what is being learned is “use full force but know when to ease off,” and where to stop.
Kids playing house and hide-and-seek: practicing roles, negotiating who does what, and guessing what others are thinking.
Play that doesn’t fit: some pure pastimes of adults, some play shows no skill to practice. So practice theory can explain only a part, and researchers guess that play may have several functions, some of them even by-products.

See whether these things are there: ① self-handicapping (the stronger side easing off); ② role reversal (the chaser becomes the chased); ③ signals that “this is play” (laughter, exaggerated movements); ④ losing isn’t serious. Common misconceptions

  • “Anything with rules is a game”: traffic rules and company policies have rules too. What matters is that rules are there to create an interesting challenge, and that players accept them voluntarily.
  • “A game must have winning and losing”: sandboxes, interactive stories and management sims may have no clear win or lose, and the goal can be set by the player. You already tried this in the definition tester.
  • “Play is only for small children”: adults play too, likewise practicing skills and building relationships in a low-risk setting.
  • “The four traits are the definition of a game”: they are a checklist designers commonly use. Edge cases such as sandboxes and virtual pets are beyond it.

Reflection and debate

“Can a game be defined” is itself an argument that has not ended. Wittgenstein’s camp holds there is only family resemblance, Suits’s camp holds it can be defined, and some philosophers think the very idea of family resemblance is flawed. Caillois’s four-way division is likewise only one view. So is the “practice theory” of play: it is intuitive, yet it can’t explain why adults play too.

Think about it: are a goalless management sandbox or a virtual pet games? The four traits do not fit them. If you had to design one, what would you add?

Key points

  • The four defining traits (McGonigal, 2011): goal, rules, feedback system, voluntary participation. Without a goal it is like a toy, without voluntary participation like a task.
  • “Game” has no agreed definition. Wittgenstein held that it is a family-resemblance concept: no single feature runs through all games.
  • Suits’s answer: a game is the voluntary overcoming of unnecessary obstacles. Rules create the obstacles on purpose, and voluntary participation turns them from torment into fun.
  • The four traits work well on typical games and fit less well on edge cases such as sandboxes and virtual pets.
  • Play is a low-risk practice ground. Caillois sorted the pleasure of games into four kinds: competition, chance, make-believe and vertigo, and most games are mixtures.
A game is a low-risk rehearsal in which players willingly accept rules, work toward a goal and keep getting responses.
