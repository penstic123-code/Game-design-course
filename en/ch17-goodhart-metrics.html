Chapter 17

Goodhart & Metrics

Measure something, and people become what you measure, sometimes something the designer never wanted.

Learning goals

  • Explain in your own words why a metric is only a shadow of the real goal, why the shadow distorts once it is rewarded, and how this idea first ran into the light in monetary policy.
  • Lay out the mechanism by which “a correlation collapses under optimization pressure,” and tell which cases in games, education and AI (specification gaming) are Goodhart phenomena and which are some other problem.
  • Explain why a reward sometimes wears interest away, and design a set of metrics that are harder to game for a game goal.
about 40 min
1

What it isA metric is the shadow of a goal

Suppose you are making an open-world game, and what you most want players to do is really explore this world. But “exploring” happens inside the player’s head, where the back end cannot see it. The back end sees only numbers, such as how many collectibles a player has picked up in total.

So the team made a natural decision: pick up all 100 and get an achievement. Now there are two things in hand. One is what they actually care about, the player’s experience of exploring, the true goal. The other is the number in hand, the metric, which is the shadow the true goal casts on the ground.

A shadow is useful because ordinarily it moves together with its owner: players who love wandering pick up more along the way, so the collectible count and the experience of exploring rise and fall together. The trouble starts the moment that number is rewarded. Someone will notice that to lengthen the shadow you needn’t move the owner at all; it is easier to just paint the shadow.

A metric and a goal are only “normally correlated.” Once someone sets out to push the metric up, a shortcut appears that goes around the goal.

This phenomenon was later summed up as Goodhart’s law: once a metric is used as a means of control or as a reward, its link to the true goal weakens.

Concept card

Goodhart’s Law

Goodhart’s law

Charles Goodhart · 1975, “Problems of Monetary Management: The U.K. Experience”; the usual wording was boiled down by Marilyn Strathern · 1997

Once a metric is used as a means of control or as a reward, its link to the true goal weakens.

Example“10,000 total kills” was meant to encourage deep play, but someone leaves the game idling to farm monsters: the achievement points go up and the fun of playing doesn’t.

The social sciences have a twin of it, Campbell’s law. They stress different things: Goodhart looks at the statistical relationship failing, and Campbell at the monitored behavior itself being distorted.

Concept card

Campbell’s Law

Campbell’s law

Donald T. Campbell · proposed in 1976 and published in 1979 in Evaluation and Program Planning

The more a quantitative social indicator is used for major decisions, the more it is subject to corruption pressures, and the more it distorts the process it was meant to monitor.

ExampleJudge teachers by exam pass rate and they spend class time on the exam points; guessing the questions pays better than making sure students understand.

First, a few scenes where the shadow got smudged.

Achievement grinding (games) “Total of 10,000 kills” was meant to encourage players to go deeper, but some leave the game idling to farm monsters and some use a bug to trigger kills over and over. Achievement points went up, and the enjoyment of play did not.
Teaching to the test (daily life) Judge teachers by exam pass rate and they spend class time entirely on the exam points. The pass rate is the shadow of “has learned,” and once the shadow is rewarded, guessing the questions pays better than making things understood. This is a classic example Campbell and many later education researchers discussed again and again.
Daily active users (games) Make “daily actives” the core KPI and the most convenient move for a designer is “a reward just for logging in.” Many people claim it and quit; daily actives go up, the number of people actually playing doesn’t, and satisfaction surveys even go down.
Counterexample: ice cream and drowningStatistics show that in months when more ice cream is sold, more people drown. This is “correlation is not causation”: both are pushed up by hot weather, and nobody takes either one as a target to optimize, so it is not Goodhart.
Counterexample: body temperatureA doctor uses body temperature to judge whether someone has a fever. Temperature is also a shadow of “health,” but nobody changes their temperature for a reward, so this metric is stable and reliable. Goodhart warns about “after it is rewarded,” not about every metric.

What it isIs this Goodhart? First, learn to tell

Many things can go wrong with “the numbers,” and only some of them are Goodhart. To tell them apart, ask: has someone taken a proxy metric as the target and optimized it until it came loose from the true goal? Judge the four scenes below.

① The team makes next-day retention the KPI, and the designers add “miss a day and your streak breaks and the rewards are wiped.” Retention goes up, but players say they feel “held hostage.”
Yes. Retention is the shadow of “players like this game,” and once it was rewarded, the team found a shortcut for pushing retention up: make people afraid not to log in. The number looks good and the goal is lost.
② The server stutters now and then, and player ratings fall with it.
No. The drop in ratings is a true reflection of a real problem, and nobody is “optimizing the rating.” The metric hasn’t been distorted, and is faithfully sounding the alarm.
③ The top 100 on the leaderboard get a rare avatar, and small groups appear that do nothing but boost each other’s scores.
Yes. Rank is the shadow of “plays well,” the avatar is a reward for the shadow, and trading boosts is the cheapest shortcut.
④ Data shows that on rainy days noticeably more people log in.
No. On rainy days people stay at home, a common cause, the same kind of thing as ice cream and drowning. Nobody is pushing the login count up for a reward.
A summary appears after all four.

How it was inventedWhy does a steady regularity let go the moment it is “grabbed”?

It begins with monetary policy

In the 1970s, British policymakers wanted to hold down inflation. They had a statistical regularity that looked solid: in past data, a certain money aggregate was tightly tied to prices and incomes. The natural thought was that if the two move together so closely, controlling the money supply would stabilize prices.

Guess first. The policymakers really did go and control the money supply. What happens to that once-steady regularity?

The reason is that the regularity was observed in an era when nobody was watching it specifically. Once the government made the money supply its control target, banks, firms and the public changed their behavior to adapt to the controls and get around them. The regularity was a by-product of people’s behavior, and when the behavior changed, the by-product vanished. In a 1975 paper Goodhart put the lesson in one sentence: any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes.

1975 The British economist Charles Goodhart wrote Problems of Monetary Management: The U.K. Experience for a conference of the Reserve Bank of Australia, and set down the sentence above. He was talking about statistical regularities in monetary policy. Games, and “metrics” in the general sense, were not part of his topic.
1976 / 1979 The American psychologist Donald T. Campbell, in “Assessing the Impact of Planned Social Change,” ran into the same thing from the other direction: the more a quantitative social indicator is used for decisions, the more it is subject to corruption pressures and the more it distorts the process it was meant to monitor. Among his examples are under-recording and downgrading in crime statistics.
1997 The anthropologist Marilyn Strathern, in a paper on the evaluation of British universities, wrote the short line that spread furthest afterwards: “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.” This popular version is not Goodhart’s own wording; it is a later summary.
2016 OpenAI’s blog post “Faulty Reward Functions in the Wild” (Jack Clark and Dario Amodei) showed odd behavior by an AI in the boat-racing game CoastRunners: the game scored hitting targets along the course, so the AI circled in a lagoon hitting targets, scoring on average 20% higher than human players and never crossing the finish line.
2020 DeepMind’s blog (Krakovna et al., April 21) gathered a large number of AI cases under the name “specification gaming”: the agent met the goal literally and failed to do what was really wanted. More than forty years after Goodhart’s 1975 paper, it was the same wall.

Monetary policy, social evaluation and artificial intelligence: three fields each ran into the same thing independently. A proxy metric stands for the goal only while nobody is optimizing it specifically. To reward it is to invite the optimizer to find the cheapest shortcut. Game designers hand out rewards every day, so they send out this invitation every day.

Why it worksWhy does a correlation collapse under pressure?

The players didn’t “turn bad.” A metric stands for the goal because, when nobody is watching it specifically, one and the same thing drives both. A player who loves exploring both explores more and picks up more along the way, for the same reason: curiosity. What was measured is the result, not the cause.

Once the collectible count is rewarded, a new cause appears: picking up for the reward. It can push the number up on its own without pushing up the experience of exploring. The tie between the two holds only in the range where nobody is optimizing, and once someone puts effort into the shortcut, the tie breaks.

And optimizers compare costs. To get the same 100 points, really exploring takes an afternoon and the shortcut takes five minutes. Who would choose the former?

If such a shortcut keeps existing, things get worse and worse, because it comes with a loop of its own:

Goodhart failure comes in more than one shape. In 2018 David Manheim and Scott Garrabrant sorted it into at least four mechanisms. Click through to see what each looks like:

Adversarial: the guide route (games) Someone actively looks for ways to make the metric look good, and finds the cheapest. Run once along a guide route and you can fill the collectibles, with no need to look at the scenery on the way.
Regressional: the leaderboard (games) The metric contains noise. Pick the “strongest players” from the top 10% of a leaderboard as testers, and the board has let in many who got up by luck, who aren’t really that far ahead in real skill.
Extremal: pushed to the end (games) A relation that holds in the ordinary range fails when pushed to the extreme. “A few more collectibles is more fun” does not mean “ten thousand collectibles is more fun.”
Causal: a thermometer in ice water (daily life)The intervention touches only the metric, not the cause of the goal. Put the thermometer in ice water and the reading drops, yet the weather has not gotten colder.
Counterexample: a thermometer reading two degrees high Every reading is 2 degrees too high, a measurement error. It makes the metric inaccurate, but nobody is applying optimization pressure to the readings, the cause is different, and it isn’t Goodhart.

Source of the theory and the classification

Goodhart (1975): a statistical regularity collapses once it is used as a means of control. Campbell (1976/1979): the more an indicator is used for decisions, the more corruption pressure it comes under. Manheim & Garrabrant (2018), in the paper “Categorizing Variants of Goodhart’s Law,” separate “optimization pulls the metric away from the goal” into at least four kinds. The four descriptions above are popular paraphrases of them, not the original definitions.

This “law” is a rule of thumb. When the reward is big, the shortcut is cheap and nobody is supervising, a metric gets gamed to pieces; some metrics stay useful for a long time.

Preprint: arXiv:1803.04585. The field has other ways of dividing it; see the original for the details.

Four variants of Goodhart’s law: regressional, extremal, causal, adversarial

Optimization can pull a metric away from its goal in at least four ways: noise (regressional), pushing to the extreme (extremal), acting on the metric without touching its cause (causal), and someone actively looking for shortcuts (adversarial).

Advanced: how the model is computed

Each player puts a fraction e (intensity × personal tendency) of their effort into the shortcut. True score = (1−e) × natural level; metric = (1−e) × natural level + e × shortcut payoff + noise; the shortcut payoff is unrelated to the natural level.

Try it: drag the strength all the way to 100%, until “the true mean of the top 10% by metric” is lower than the “average true value.” It means that the “strongest” players picked off the leaderboard are worse at the thing you actually care about than an ordinary person drawn at random.

How to read it: at the start, r is close to 1, and picking people by metric is almost the same as picking by the real goal. As the strength rises, the average metric goes up while the average true value goes down: the numbers look better and the thing gets worse. The last step says the most: the “strongest” player picked from the leaderboard may be worse than an ordinary one at the thing that really matters.

r lies between −1 and 1; the closer it is to 0, the harder it is to infer the true goal from the metric.

Advanced: how r is computed

Pearson correlation coefficient r = Σ(x−x̄)(y−ȳ) ÷ √[Σ(x−x̄)² · Σ(y−ȳ)²]. x is the metric score, y is the true score, and x̄, ȳ are their means.

Why it worksRewards also wear down interest: another kind of distortion beyond the numbers

All the worry so far was about a metric getting distorted, but a reward has another problem: it can change a person’s reason for doing the thing. Chapter 3 covered it: when something a person did out of interest gets an outside reward added, the interest can be swapped for “doing it for the reward.” In psychology this is the overjustification effect.

The two distortions together make a mess: rewarding a number both invites shortcuts and quietly swaps “I want to play” for “I want to score.”

Guess first. In 1973, in a Stanford nursery school, researchers found children who already liked drawing with colored markers. Some were told beforehand that “drawing gets you a certificate,” and some were not. A week or two later everyone was back to free play, with no certificates. Which group of children picked up the markers on their own for less time?

The overjustification effect

This is the classic 1973 experiment by Lepper, Greene and Nisbett. The explanation at the time: seeing their drawing earn a certificate, children easily read “why am I drawing” as “for the certificate,” and the original interest is covered by this new reason. The study also had a control group: children who didn’t know beforehand and received a certificate unexpectedly afterward showed no clear loss of interest. The harm comes from the expectation “I am doing it for the reward.”

This conclusion is also disputed. In 1999 Deci, Koestner and Ryan pooled 128 experiments and concluded that outside rewards (especially tangible, announced-in-advance ones) weaken intrinsic motivation overall. Cameron, Pierce and others analyzed with a different method and reached a much milder conclusion, and the two sides still disagree on method. One reasonable reading is that rewards are not poison, but for something people already do out of interest, they should be given with particular care.

This and Goodhart are two complementary views: Goodhart says “numbers get gamed,” and overjustification says “people change.”

Sources: Lepper, Greene & Nisbett, “Undermining children’s intrinsic interest with extrinsic reward: A test of the ‘overjustification’ hypothesis”, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 28(1): 129–137 (1973); Deci, Koestner & Ryan, Psychological Bulletin 125(6): 627–668 (1999).

How to use itDesigning metrics and rewards that are hard to game

First, admit one thing: metrics can’t be eliminated, and shouldn’t be. Teams need numbers to communicate, and without metrics all that is left is guesswork. So the job is to make a metric hard to push up on its own, and to catch it early when it distorts, instead of looking for a “perfect metric.”

There are four common moves.

1. Balance with several metrics

Watch several metrics that hold each other in check and differ in nature, such as retention + a player satisfaction survey + interviews about why people leave, so that gaming any one of them inevitably hurts another.

2. Keep qualitative observation

Regularly watch real people play, recordings, and community comments in players’ own words. Numbers tell you what happened; only people can tell you why, and whether it is what you wanted.

3. Tie rewards to the behavior itself

Reward “having done something meaningful” (discovering the secret of a hidden area), not “the number is big enough.” A design that lets people repeat something just for the number is the easiest to game.

4. Rotate and audit regularly

Once a metric has been in use for a while, people work out the tricks. Spot-check anomalies regularly (a metric jumps while there is no matching player feedback), rotate metrics, and review how they are defined.

Also beware the stacking effect from earlier: attaching points and rewards to an activity done out of interest easily steps into both the Goodhart and the overjustification pits at once.

Common misconceptions

  • “Just swap in a different metric.” As long as there is a reward, the new metric will sooner or later be optimized too. A better way is several metrics, qualitative checks and regular rotation, not hunting for a “perfect metric.”
  • “Players gaming the score is the players’ fault.” Players are simply responding reasonably to the incentives the designer set. On finding gaming, first ask “what is the reward encouraging?”
  • “So don’t use data, make games by feel.” Goodhart warns against “taking a single number as the only goal,” not against “measuring.” Going by gut feeling without data goes astray too.
  • “That sentence is Goodhart’s own words.” The popular version is a later summary (Strathern 1997 is often cited), and Goodhart’s 1975 original meaning concerned statistical regularities and monetary control.

Reflection and debate

Is it really a “law”? Strictly speaking, it is a rule of thumb. Some metrics stay useful over a very long time, depending on the cost of gaming them, the strength of supervision and people’s motives.

Who proposed it first? Goodhart (1975) and Campbell (1976/1979) came almost together, independently, with different emphases. Some (Jeff Rodamar) think some of Campbell’s formulations can be traced back to 1969. Calling them all “Goodhart’s law” is a popular shorthand, and extending it to game design is an analogy, not a rigorous conclusion accepted in the field.

Another view: the play that players game their way into can also be a good game. Some hold that min-max, speedrunning and finding loopholes are themselves part of game culture, and designers shouldn’t always treat them as “fun that was optimized away.” Whether it counts as a problem depends on what the real goal is.

A habit worth keeping: when you see “daily active users,” “completion rate” or “positive review rate,” first ask what it stands for, whether anyone is specifically pushing it, and what the cheapest way to push it would be.

Key points

  • A metric is a proxy for the real goal, and the two are roughly correlated only when “nobody is specifically optimizing the metric.”
  • Goodhart (1975, monetary policy) and Campbell (1976/1979, social indicators): once a metric is used as a target or reward, optimizers look for the cheapest shortcut and the correlation collapses; Strathern’s (1997) short line is a later summary.
  • Mechanism: normally the two are pushed by one cause, and after the reward there is a new cause that pushes only the metric; the process comes with its own escalation loop.
  • Rewards also swap out a person’s reason for acting (the overjustification effect), and distorted numbers and distorted motivation often stack together.
  • Responses: balance with several metrics (varied ones), keep qualitative observation, tie rewards to the behavior itself, audit and rotate regularly, instead of looking for a “perfect metric.”
A number is only the shadow of a goal. Once the shadow is rewarded, people will paint the shadow instead of walking toward its owner.
