Chapter 17

Goodhart & Metrics

Measure something, and people become it—sometimes into something you didn’t want.

Learning goals

  • Explain in your own words why a metric is only the shadow of the real goal, why a shadow that gets rewarded stops being faithful, and that this idea first ran into the wall in monetary policy.
  • Lay out the mechanism by which “a correlation collapses under optimization pressure,” and tell which cases in games, education and AI (specification gaming) are Goodhart phenomena and which are just other problems.
  • Explain why rewards sometimes wear down interest, and design a set of metrics that are harder to game for one game goal.
about 40 min
1

What it isA metric is the shadow of a goal

Suppose you have made an open-world game, and what you most want players to do is really explore the world. But “exploring” happens inside the player’s head, and your back end can’t see it. The back end sees only numbers, such as how many collectibles a player has picked up in all.

So you make a very natural decision: collect all 100 and get an achievement. There are two things here. What you really care about is the player’s exploration experience, which is called the real goal. The number in your hand is the metric, the shadow the real goal casts on the ground.

A shadow is useful because ordinarily it moves along with the body: players who love wandering pick up more along the way, so collectible counts and exploration experience rise and fall together. The trouble starts the moment you begin rewarding that number. Someone will notice that to make the shadow longer, there is no need to move the body, and it is easier to paint the shadow directly.

A metric and a goal are only “normally correlated.” Once someone sets out to push the metric up, a shortcut appears that goes around the goal.

This phenomenon later got a name, Goodhart’s law: once a metric is used as a means of control or as a reward, the link between it and the real goal weakens. In the social sciences there is a twin statement called Campbell’s law. The two stress different things: Goodhart looks at the statistical relationship breaking down, and Campbell looks at the monitored behavior itself being distorted. First, a few scenes where shadows got painted over.

Achievement grinding (games) “Total of 10,000 kills” was meant to encourage players to go deeper, but some leave the game idling to farm monsters and some use a bug to trigger kills over and over. Achievement points went up, and the enjoyment of play did not.
Teaching to the test (daily life) Judge teachers by exam pass rate and they spend class time entirely on the exam points. The pass rate is the shadow of “has learned,” and once the shadow is rewarded, guessing the questions pays better than making things understood. This is a classic example Campbell and many later education researchers discussed again and again.
Daily active users (games) Make “daily actives” the core KPI and the most convenient move for a designer is “a reward just for logging in.” Many people claim it and quit; daily actives go up, the number of people actually playing doesn’t, and satisfaction surveys even go down.
Counterexample: ice cream and drowning Statistics show that in months when more ice cream is sold, more people also drown. This is “correlation is not causation”: both are pushed up by hot weather, and nobody is treating either as a target to optimize, so it is not Goodhart.
Counterexample: body temperature A doctor uses temperature to judge whether you have a fever. Temperature too is a shadow of “health,” but nobody changes their temperature to earn a reward, so the metric is stable and reliable. Goodhart warns about “after it is rewarded,” not about every metric.
2

What it isIs this Goodhart? First, learn to tell

Not every “numbers gone wrong” is called Goodhart. One question is enough to decide: has someone taken a proxy metric as the target to optimize, so that it has come loose from the real goal? You judge the four scenes below.

① The team makes next-day retention the KPI, and the designers add “miss a day and your streak breaks and the rewards are wiped.” Retention goes up, but players say they feel “held hostage.”
Yes. Retention is the shadow of “players like this game,” and once it was rewarded, the team found a shortcut for pushing retention up: make people afraid not to log in. The number looks good and the goal is lost.
② The server stutters now and then, and player ratings fall with it.
No. The drop in ratings is a true reflection of a real problem, and nobody is “optimizing the rating.” The metric hasn’t been distorted, and is faithfully sounding the alarm.
③ The top 100 on the leaderboard get a rare avatar, and small groups appear that do nothing but boost each other’s scores.
Yes. Rank is the shadow of “plays well,” the avatar is a reward for the shadow, and trading boosts is the cheapest shortcut.
④ Data shows that on rainy days noticeably more people log in.
No. On rainy days people stay at home, a common cause, the same kind of thing as ice cream and drowning. Nobody is pushing the login count up for a reward.
A summary appears after all four.

How it was inventedWhy does a steady regularity let go the moment it is “grabbed”?

It begins with monetary policy

The problem. In the 1970s, British policymakers wanted to bring inflation down. They had a statistical regularity that looked very steady: in past data, a certain money aggregate was closely tied to prices and incomes. The natural thought was: if the two move together this tightly, then if I control the money supply, prices will be stable.

Guess first. The policymakers did go and control the money supply. What do you think happened to that regularity that had been so steady?

Why it failed. That regularity had been observed in an age when nobody was specifically watching it. Once the government made the money supply a control target, banks, firms and the public changed their behavior to adapt to and get around the controls. The regularity was a by-product of people’s behavior, and when the behavior changed, the by-product was gone. In a 1975 paper Goodhart wrote the lesson in one sentence: any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes.

1975 The British economist Charles Goodhart wrote “Problems of Monetary Management: The U.K. Experience” for a conference of the Reserve Bank of Australia, and wrote the sentence above. He was talking about statistical regularities in monetary policy, not about games, and not about “metrics” in the general sense.
1976 / 1979 In “Assessing the Impact of Planned Social Change,” the American psychologist Donald T. Campbell ran into the same thing from another direction: the more a quantitative social indicator is used for decisions, the more subject it is to corruption pressures, and the more it distorts the process it was meant to monitor. His examples included under-recording and downgrading in crime statistics.
1997 In a paper on evaluating British universities, the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern wrote the short line that later spread the furthest: “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.” So this popular version is not Goodhart’s own wording, but a later summary.
2016 OpenAI’s blog post “Faulty Reward Functions in the Wild” (Jack Clark and Dario Amodei) showed odd behavior by an AI in the boat-racing game CoastRunners: the game scored hitting targets along the course, so the AI circled in a lagoon hitting targets, scoring on average 20% higher than human players and never crossing the finish line.
2020 DeepMind’s blog (Krakovna et al., April 21) gathered a large number of AI cases under the name “specification gaming”: an agent meets the goal you wrote literally and does not do what you really wanted. This was more than forty years after Goodhart’s 1975 paper, and it was the same wall again.

The key insight. Monetary policy, social evaluation and artificial intelligence, three fields unaware of each other, independently ran into the same thing: a proxy metric stands for the goal only while nobody is specifically optimizing it; rewarding it is an invitation to optimizers to look for the cheapest shortcut. Game designers hand out rewards every day, and so issue such invitations every day.

Sources: Goodhart, “Problems of Monetary Management: The U.K. Experience”, Papers in Monetary Economics Vol.1, Reserve Bank of Australia (1975), reprinted in 1981 in Courakis (ed.); Campbell, “Assessing the Impact of Planned Social Change”, Evaluation and Program Planning 2(1): 67–90 (1979; Dartmouth College Occasional Paper #8, 1976); Strathern, “‘Improving ratings’: audit in the British University system”, European Review 5(3): 305–321 (1997); Clark & Amodei, “Faulty Reward Functions in the Wild”, OpenAI (2016), the source of the “20%”; Krakovna et al., “Specification gaming: the flip side of AI ingenuity”, DeepMind (2020). Strathern credits the line to Keith Hoskin; this rests on the retelling in the Wikipedia entry “Goodhart’s law,” and we did not consult the original directly.

Why it worksWhy does a correlation collapse under pressure?

You might think the players have “turned bad.” They haven’t; think it through and the key becomes clear: a metric can stand for the goal because, when nobody is specifically watching it, the two are driven by one and the same thing. A player who loves exploring both explores more and picks up more along the way, with one cause behind both: curiosity. What you measure is a result, not a cause.

When you reward the collectible count, a new cause appears: collecting for the reward. It can push the number up alone, without pushing the exploration experience. The link between the two holds only in the range where nobody is optimizing, and once effort goes into the shortcut, the link snaps.

And optimizers compare costs. To get the same 100 points, really exploring takes an afternoon and the shortcut takes five minutes. Who would choose the former?

If such a shortcut keeps existing, things get worse and worse, because it comes with a loop of its own:

Goodhart failure comes in more than one form. In 2018, David Manheim and Scott Garrabrant sorted it into at least four mechanisms. Click through and see what each looks like:

Adversarial: the guide route (games) Someone actively looks for ways to make the metric look good, and finds the cheapest. Run once along a guide route and you can fill the collectibles, with no need to look at the scenery on the way.
Regressional: the leaderboard (games) The metric contains noise. Pick the “strongest players” from the top 10% of a leaderboard as testers, and the board has let in many who got up by luck, who aren’t really that far ahead in real skill.
Extremal: pushed to the end (games) A relation that holds in the ordinary range fails when pushed to the extreme. “A few more collectibles is more fun” does not mean “ten thousand collectibles is more fun.”
Causal: the thermometer in ice water (daily life) What you intervene on is only the metric, not the cause of the goal. Put a thermometer in ice water and the reading drops, while the weather hasn’t gotten colder.
Counterexample: a thermometer reading two degrees high Every reading is 2 degrees too high, a measurement error. It makes the metric inaccurate, but nobody is applying optimization pressure to the readings, the cause is different, and it isn’t Goodhart.

Source of the theory and the classification

Goodhart (1975): a statistical regularity tends to collapse once it is used as a means of control. Campbell (1976/1979): the more a metric is used for decisions, the more it is subject to corruption pressure. Manheim & Garrabrant (2018) in “Categorizing Variants of Goodhart’s Law” separate “optimization decoupling the metric from the goal” into at least four kinds. The four descriptions above are our popular summaries of them, not the original definitions.

This “law” is more a rule of thumb than a law of physics: it doesn’t guarantee that every metric will be gamed, only warns that when the reward is big, the shortcut is cheap and nobody is watching, the chance of being gamed is large.

Preprint: arXiv:1803.04585. The field has other ways of dividing it; see the original for the details.

The model: each person puts a fraction e (strength × personal tendency) of their effort into the shortcut, real score = (1−e) × natural level, metric = (1−e) × natural level + e × shortcut payoff + noise; the shortcut payoff has nothing to do with the natural level. r is the Pearson correlation coefficient computed live from the 70 points in the chart.

Try it: push the strength all the way to 100% until the “real mean of the top 10% by metric” is lower than the “mean real.” It means: the “strongest” players picked by the leaderboard are worse at what you really care about than an ordinary person drawn at random.

How to read it: at the start r is close to 1, and picking people by the metric is almost like picking them by the real goal. As the strength rises, the mean metric goes up while the mean real goes down: the numbers look better and the thing gets worse. The last box says the most: the “strongest” players you pick from the leaderboard may be worse than ordinary players at what you care about.

Why it worksRewards also wear down interest: another kind of distortion beyond the numbers

So far, what we have worried about is the metric being distorted. Rewards bring another trouble: they can change a person’s reason for doing the thing. Remember the idea from the discussion of player motivation: something a person does out of interest, once an external reward is added, may have its interest swapped for “for the reward.” Psychology calls this the overjustification effect.

The two distortions together make a mess: rewarding a number both invites shortcuts and quietly swaps “I want to play” for “I want to score.”

Guess first. In 1973, at a Stanford nursery school, researchers found children who already loved drawing with colored markers; some were told in advance that “drawing earns a certificate,” and some were not. A week or two later everyone was back in free play, with no certificates. Which group do you think spent less time picking up the markers on their own?

The overjustification effect

This is the classic 1973 experiment by Lepper, Greene and Nisbett. The explanation at the time: seeing their drawing earn a certificate, children easily read “why am I drawing” as “for the certificate,” and the original interest is covered by this new reason. The study also had a control group: children who didn’t know beforehand and received a certificate unexpectedly afterward showed no clear loss of interest. The harm comes from the expectation “I am doing it for the reward.”

There is dispute. In 1999 Deci, Koestner and Ryan pooled 128 experiments and held that external rewards (especially tangible, announced-in-advance ones) generally weaken intrinsic motivation; Cameron and Pierce and others analyzed with another method and reached much milder conclusions, and the two sides still disagree on method. One way to take it: rewards are not poison, but for an activity done out of interest, hand them out with special care.

This and Goodhart are two complementary views: Goodhart says “numbers get gamed,” and overjustification says “people change.”

Sources: Lepper, Greene & Nisbett, “Undermining children’s intrinsic interest with extrinsic reward: A test of the ‘overjustification’ hypothesis”, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 28(1): 129–137 (1973); Deci, Koestner & Ryan, Psychological Bulletin 125(6): 627–668 (1999).

How to use itDesigning metrics and rewards that are hard to game

First admit one thing: you can’t get rid of metrics, and shouldn’t. A team needs numbers to communicate, and without metrics all that is left is gut feeling. So the goal is not to find a “perfect metric,” but to make a metric hard to push up by itself, and to catch it early when it is distorted.

There are four common moves.

1. Balance with several metrics

Watch several metrics that hold each other in check and differ in nature, such as retention + a player satisfaction survey + interviews about why people leave, so that gaming any one of them inevitably hurts another.

2. Keep qualitative observation

Regularly watch real people play, recordings and the community’s own words. Numbers tell you what happened, and only people can tell you why, and whether it is what you wanted.

3. Tie rewards to the behavior itself

Reward “doing something meaningful” (finding the secret of a hidden area), not “the number is big enough.” A design that lets people repeat something just for the number is the easiest to game.

4. Rotate and audit regularly

Once a metric has been used a while, people learn its tricks. Spot-check for anomalies (a metric soaring without matching player feedback), rotate, and recheck the metric definitions.

Also beware the stacking effect from earlier: attaching points and rewards to an activity done out of interest easily steps into both the Goodhart and the overjustification pits at once.

Common misconceptions

  • “Just swap in a different metric.” As long as there is a reward, the new metric will sooner or later be optimized too. A better way is several metrics, qualitative checks and regular rotation, not hunting for a “perfect metric.”
  • “Players gaming the score is the players’ fault.” Players are only responding reasonably to the incentive you set. When you find gaming, first ask “what is my reward encouraging?”
  • “So don’t use data, make games by feel.” Goodhart warns against “taking a single number as the only goal,” not against “measuring.” Going by gut feeling without data goes astray too.
  • “That sentence is Goodhart’s own words.” The popular version is a later summary (Strathern 1997 is often cited), and Goodhart’s 1975 original meaning concerned statistical regularities and monetary control.

Reflection and debate

Is it a “law”? Strictly speaking, it is more a rule of thumb. “A metric will always get gamed” does not always hold: some metrics stay useful for a very long time, depending on the cost of gaming, the strength of oversight and people’s motivation.

Who proposed it first? Goodhart (1975) and Campbell (1976/1979) came almost at the same time, each independently, with different emphases; some (Jeff Rodamar) think certain statements of Campbell’s can be traced to 1969. Calling them all “Goodhart’s law” is a popular habit, and extending it to game design is an analogy more than a conclusion accepted in the field.

Another view: the play that players game their way into can be a good game too. Some argue that min-max play, speedrunning and finding exploits are themselves part of game culture, and designers should not simply treat them as “fun that was optimized away.” Whether it is a problem depends on what your real goal is.

How it might change how you see things: next time you see “daily actives,” “clear rate” or “positive review rate,” ask first: what is it standing for? Is anyone specifically pushing it? What is the cheapest way to push it?

Key points

  • A metric is a proxy for the real goal, and the two are roughly correlated only when “nobody is specifically optimizing the metric.”
  • Goodhart (1975, monetary policy) and Campbell (1976/1979, social indicators): once a metric is used as a target or reward, optimizers look for the cheapest shortcut and the correlation collapses; Strathern’s (1997) short line is a later summary.
  • Mechanism: normally the two are pushed by one cause, and after the reward there is a new cause that pushes only the metric; the process comes with its own escalation loop.
  • Rewards also swap out a person’s reason for acting (the overjustification effect), and distorted numbers and distorted motivation often stack together.
  • Responses: balance with several metrics (varied ones), keep qualitative observation, tie rewards to the behavior itself, audit and rotate regularly, instead of looking for a “perfect metric.”
Numbers are only the shadow of a goal; once the shadow is rewarded, someone will paint the shadow instead of walking toward the body.
