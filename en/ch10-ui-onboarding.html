Chapter 10

UI & Onboarding

A good interface is “invisible”: it is there when the player needs it and out of the way when they don’t.

Learning goals

about 50 min
  • Explain why the HUD was invented and why it can’t be stuffed full, using “working memory is tiny” and “extraneous load,” and group interface elements with the Gestalt principles of proximity, similarity, closure and continuity.
  • Lay out a HUD by hand: decide for each element in each scene whether it is “always on, dimmed or hidden,” and read the visual-load, center-occlusion and grouping diagnostics.
  • Judge whether a default is a “nudge” or “manipulation,” and pick sensible defaults for onboarding.
1

What it isFour places a UI can “live”: where does the health bar actually live?

You have surely met this: the fight is at its hottest, the screen goes black with a “you were knocked down,” and the first question in your head is: how much health did I have left?

The health bar was on screen the whole time, and you just didn’t look at it. It’s not that you weren’t paying attention; there were a dozen things on the screen fighting for your eyes. The job of the interface (UI) is to find a balance between “letting the player know” and “staying out of the player’s way.”

Before doing that, ask a seemingly dull question: where does this piece of information “live”? Designers often sort it with two questions: does it exist in the story world? Does it exist in the game’s 3D space? This gives four kinds.

Diegetic

The characters themselves can see it too. Upside: the strongest immersion. Downside: not always clear or easy to read, and it takes the most work to make.

Non-diegetic (the most common)

A HUD that serves purely the player, pasted onto the screen. Upside: clear and easy to change. Downside: it easily breaks immersion, and tends to pile up.

The HUD is the layer of information always overlaid on the picture. There is no right or wrong choice among them; it depends on whether you need clarity or immersion more.

Diegetic: the glowing bar on the back. In Dead Space the glowing spine bar on the protagonist’s back is the health: both the character and the player can see it, and no numbers are needed on screen. Horror games often use this trick to keep immersion.
Non-diegetic: numbers in a screen corner. The health and ammo of a competitive shooter are pasted in the screen corners. They don’t belong to the story world, but they read at a glance, and when winning and losing are at stake, clarity matters more than immersion.
In daily life: a dashboard and a battery bar. A car’s dashboard really is installed in the car and is “diegetic” for the driver; the battery bar at the top of a phone is not an object in the phone’s world but a layer the system puts on top, so it is “non-diegetic.”
A near-miss: diegetic does not mean usable. For immersion, health is made a tiny indicator light on the character’s clothes. In a fight the camera shakes and the player simply can’t see it. Either make it a more eye-catching glow, or add a layer of meta UI like a reddening screen when things get critical.

This four-way split is often traced to Fagerholt and Lorentzon’s 2009 master’s thesis at Chalmers University of Technology, Beyond the HUD. The word “diegetic” is borrowed from film, and some think it doesn’t always fit games (Jørgensen and others have made a similar criticism about game sound).

What it isInformation hierarchy: not all information is equally important

Knowing where information lives is not enough. Screen space and the player’s attention are both scarce, and you have to decide how eye-catching each piece of information is. This “eye-catchingness” is called visual weight. A simple three-tier method is:

1

Always prominent

Information the current decision needs right now, such as health in combat.

2

Dimmed

Glanced at now and then, like the minimap. Smaller and paler.

3

Shown only when needed

If it isn’t needed, don’t show it. Pop-up menus, displayed only when an event triggers.

The difficulty is that the same element belongs in different tiers at different moments. The figure below is the “seating chart” of the same HUD at three moments.

In games: the gold bar changes its face with the scene. In combat the gold bar folds away and only grows in the shop; when exploring, the minimap gets bigger, and as soon as combat begins it shrinks into a pale thumbnail in the corner.
In daily life: the low-fuel light. A car’s “low fuel” light is normally off and comes on when the fuel is nearly gone, claiming your attention only at the moment you have a decision to make.
In daily life: a phone’s status bar. The time is always shown, while the “battery below 20%” alert pops up only when needed.
Counterexample: everything always on. In combat, the chat box, achievement popups, friends online and weather and time are all up. Each looks “useful” alone, but piled together the player misses the truly deadly one, the health.
3

What it isOnboarding: let the player “learn,” not be “taught”

The interface has another problem: new players know nothing. FTUE (first-time user experience) means those few minutes from first opening the game to “I get it, and I want to continue.” Many people decide in those minutes whether to give up, so they are worth designing with care. There are three core methods:

🖐️

Do first, tell after

Let the player act first, then explain it in a sentence. Use few walls of text.

🪜

Progressive disclosure

Teach one new thing at a time, and release the next once it is learned.

📍

Contextual hints

Hint only when the player really needs it, such as showing “Press E to open” only when they reach the door.

Do first, tell after: an opening without words. Level 1-1 of Super Mario Bros. opens with no text; the first enemy walks slowly toward you, and if you don’t jump you bump into it. It makes you “do” first, and then understand.
Progressive disclosure: one new thing per room. Designers often praise this approach: the first few rooms introduce only one new mechanic at a time, and the next is layered on only once it is learned (Portal is often cited as an example).
A contextual hint in daily life: an app’s little bubble. When a phone app is first opened, a little bubble pops up only the first time you touch a certain button, instead of handing you a manual right away.
Counterexample: a twenty-page forced tutorial. A 20-page illustrated tutorial pops up at the start and demands “finish it all before you can begin.” The player taps skip, and when a real problem comes up later, can’t find the way back in. Good practice is to make it skippable and reviewable.
The best teaching often happens when the player doesn’t notice they are being taught: use level design to make a little room that can only be passed once the thing is learned, which beats a pop-up. Remember to let hints be skipped and revisited; veterans and newcomers both deserve respect.
4

How it was inventedWhy was the HUD invented? And why does it have to be “small”?

Story one: the pilot’s “head-down” problem

Imagine you are in a fast-flying fighter, having to watch the enemy aircraft ahead and read speed and altitude at the same time. The instruments are below the cockpit, and each time you look down your eyes leave the front. Even if it is only a second or two, the situation can change completely.

The early remedy was to arrange the instruments more neatly and make the numerals bigger. But as long as the instruments are “down there,” you have to look down, refocus, and look up again. The problem was not whether the instruments looked good but their position.

So someone changed the approach: don’t make the eyes go looking for information, let the information come to the eyes. Project the key figures onto transparent glass directly in front of the pilot, so that with the head up they can see the outside world and the readout together. That is where the name “Head-Up Display” comes from: you see it with your head up. It evolved step by step from the reflector gunsight in fighters, which projects an aiming ring onto glass.

But a new problem soon arose: more and more information could be projected onto the glass, while the pilot’s attention and memory did not grow. Designers had to decide: which information deserves this precious “straight ahead”? Games borrowed the same name, and the same problem.

Around WWII Fighters carry reflector gunsights that project an aiming ring onto glass in front.
1942 British radar researchers build an experimental device that combines a radar image and the projection of a gyro gunsight onto one windscreen. According to Wikipedia, the device wasn’t installed on an aircraft then, but it already had the core features of the modern HUD.
1956 The psychologist George Miller publishes “The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two”: people can hold only a limited amount of information at once, but they can use chunking to squeeze several small items into one, in effect enlarging what “fits.”
1988 John Sweller proposes cognitive load theory: poor presentation needlessly uses up limited working memory.
2001 Nelson Cowan reviews a large body of experiments and argues that, once rehearsal and chunking are excluded, capacity looks more like about 4 chunks.
Guess first, then try: how many digits can you remember at a time?

The digits will flash for 3 seconds. Guess a number by feel first, then test, and see how far you are from the psychologists’ conclusions.

Try it: with the same N (say 9), test once with “Run together” and once with “Groups of 3.” With the same digits, grouping leaves fewer “units” to remember, and you will most likely feel firsthand what a chunk is. A single test fluctuates a lot, so try several rounds; this is not a rigorous experiment.

Story two: why do we “see the whole”?

Early psychology wanted to take perception apart into a pile of “basic sensations” (dots, lines, patches of color) and add them up like building blocks. But there was a phenomenon that couldn’t be taken apart: light two still bulbs in turn, and what you see is plainly one light running back and forth, though nothing in the picture is “moving.” See for yourself first:

The two red dots below are only lighting up in turn, and neither dot ever moves. What do you see? Press “Slow” and “Fast” and take a look at each.

When it is slow, you see two separate lights; when fast, most people see “a single dot jumping left and right.”

In the version often told, the psychologist Max Wertheimer was struck by flickering lights on a train around 1910, went back to experiment with a stroboscopic device, and in 1912 published a long paper on “apparent motion” (the phi phenomenon). (“Inspiration on the train” is a circulating anecdote whose details vary; what can be confirmed is that 1912 paper.) His conclusion: perception is not the sum of parts, the brain grasps the overall structure first. This is the starting point of the Gestalt (German for “whole”) school.

For interface designers this means: players don’t read your 12 icons one by one; what they see first is “a few blocks.” You can use placement to divide the blocks for them.

1912 Wertheimer publishes “Experimental Studies on the Perception of Motion,” describing the phi phenomenon.
1923 Wertheimer publishes a paper on perceptual organization, summing up grouping tendencies such as proximity and similarity.
Today “Put like things close together, same color and same shape” in interface design applies these tendencies. They are empirical descriptions, not exact laws.

Why it worksWhy can’t a HUD be stuffed too full? Cognitive load and working memory

If 12 control instructions pop up at the start, you probably won’t remember a single one; split a phone number into 3-4-4 and it is much easier to remember. Behind both is the same thing: the human brain can handle very little information at once.

Cognitive load theory and the limit of working memory

Working memory is where we temporarily hold and process information, and its capacity is very limited. John Sweller’s 1988 paper started from the “cognitive burden during problem solving” and later developed into cognitive load theory. Later literature often splits load into three kinds:

  • Intrinsic load: the complexity of the task itself (how many variables must be handled at once).
  • Extraneous load: the extra burden brought by poor presentation (a cluttered HUD, walls of text), which a designer most needs to cut.
  • Germane load: the useful effort that goes into integrating new knowledge into a “routine” (a schema).

As for how big the capacity actually is: the point of Miller’s 1956 paper was that people can hold about 7±2 chunks, not 7 “digits,” and that recoding can enlarge what “fits.” After reviewing many experiments, Cowan proposed in 2001 that with chunking and rehearsal excluded, the real capacity is more conservative, about 4 chunks. The argument between the two numbers has still not been entirely settled, but everyone agrees: it is very small, so spend it sparingly.

Sources: Sweller (1988), Cognitive load during problem solving: Effects on learning, Cognitive Science 12(2): 257–285; Miller (1956), The magical number seven, plus or minus two, Psychological Review 63(2): 81–97; Cowan (2001), The magical number 4 in short-term memory, Behavioral and Brain Sciences 24(1): 87–114 (PubMed). The three-way split of load comes from later reviews by Sweller and others (such as Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas, 1998).
In games: extraneous load. A combat interface with 12 elements lit at once, and the player actually misses the health. It isn’t that the player is dumb; the cluttered presentation used up the capacity they could have used to think.
In games: a tutorial told all at once. Movement, attack, dodge, inventory and crafting are all explained in one go at the start, and almost nobody remembers them. Split into one per room over the first five rooms, and players can all learn it.
In daily life: phone numbers. 13912345678 is easy to remember as 139-1234-5678. The same 11 digits, but the “units” to remember drop from 11 to 3.
A near-miss: “the less information the better.” For veterans, the dozen or so skills they use often have been “packed” by muscle memory into two or three chunks, so the same HUD is far less load for them than for newcomers. So “less” is relative to the player’s skill, and a newcomer’s interface needs even more restraint.
6

Why it worksWhy does a player “read” the layout at a glance? The Gestalt principles

Since brain capacity is this small, what a designer can do is make a dozen elements become two or three blocks in the player’s eyes. Fortunately the brain itself loves grouping: see a few dots in a row and you won’t take them as “6 separate dots” but directly see “a row.”

Gestalt principles of perception

Psychologists such as Wertheimer, Köhler and Koffka (the Gestalt school) summed up some tendencies in how we perceive wholes, and the often-cited ones are:

  • Proximity: things close together are seen as a group.
  • Similarity: things of similar color or shape are seen as a group.
  • Closure: the brain automatically completes an outline with a gap and sees it as a whole shape.
  • Continuity: things arranged along a smooth route are seen as a single line.
Source: Wertheimer, M. (1923). Untersuchungen zur Lehre von der Gestalt II. Psychologische Forschung, 4, 301–350 (an abridged English translation, “Laws of organization in perceptual forms,” is in Ellis (ed.), A Source Book of Gestalt Psychology, 1938; full text online). These “principles” are more like descriptive empirical regularities than exact quantitative laws, and later scholars have rewritten and extended them.
In games (proximity): resources in one group, skills in another. Put the health, mana and stamina bars right next to each other, put the skill icons in a separate row, and leave a gap between the two groups. Even without reading a word, the player knows “these are resources and those are skills.”
In games (closure): the cooldown ring. A skill’s cooldown is shown as a “ring with a small stretch still missing”; the brain fills it in, and you see at a glance “almost ready.” Quest waypoints lined up along one line, and the eye naturally follows them: that is “continuity.”
In daily life: an elevator button panel. Floors, open/close door and alarm are split into three blocks by spacing rather than by text labels. The “folders” on a phone’s home screen likewise ring similar apps into one whole.
Counterexample: 12 evenly spaced icons. The spacing is perfectly even, the colors and shapes all differ, and there is no hint of grouping. Players can only read them one by one, each becomes a separate chunk, and that uses up the capacity exactly.

So when making a HUD, put like things close together and pull unlike things apart (proximity); give like things the same color and shape (similarity); show cooldowns with an unclosed ring (closure); and line up waypoints along one line (continuity). That way the player reads the layout without reading words, and the extraneous load drops. In the “HUD placement bench” later, the grouping diagnostics on the right check exactly this.

Why it worksWhy do “defaults” matter so much? The default effect and choice architecture

When you bought a new phone, did you ever change the factory ringtone? Most people didn’t, even though changing it takes two taps. People often accept the default option, and this matters a great deal to onboarding: whether a switch in the settings is on or off by default decides the final experience of most players.

The default effect and choice architecture

The default effect: a preset option clearly affects the final choice. In 2003, Johnson and Goldstein compared organ-donation registration across European countries in Science: countries with “presumed consent, opt out” had much higher consent rates than countries with “presumed no consent, opt in,” and the example often cited is nearly 100% in Austria against about 12% in Germany. (This is a cross-country comparison with many confounders, and “registered consent” is not the same as “actual donation.”) In Nudge, Thaler and Sunstein put forward choice architecture: there is no “neutral” design, because there always has to be a default and there always has to be an order.

Sources: Johnson, E. J. & Goldstein, D. (2003). Do defaults save lives? Science, 302(5649), 1338–1339; Thaler, R. & Sunstein, C. (2008). Nudge.
Micro-experiment: how many people can a default move? (a sketch model)

Suppose some players sincerely want to turn a feature on. Suppose also that changing the default takes a little effort, and some of “those who want to change it” can’t be bothered. Drag the sliders and see the final share who end up with it on under the two defaults.

The formula: default on = q + (1−q)·inertia; default off = q·(1−inertia). The numbers are all sketches; real proportions have to come from A/B tests.

Try it: drag “can’t be bothered” to 0 and the two bars become equally long, showing that all the power of the default comes from inertia. Then drag it to 90% and see how many players one designer’s choice can “decide for.”
In games: the new-player tips switch. “Show beginner tips automatically” is off by default, and as a result almost no newcomers see the tips. If tips help newcomers, they should be on by default, with turning them off made easy.
In daily life: an installation wizard. A “bundled toolbar” is ticked by default, and many people click “Next” without unticking it.
Counterexample (manipulation): a hidden cancel button. “Auto-renew” is ticked by default, and the way to cancel is hidden three menus deep. The default favors the designer and harms the player, and exiting is deliberately made hard, which slides from a “nudge” into a dark pattern.

How to tell whether a default is a nudge or manipulation? Ask two questions: does it serve the player’s own interest? Is it transparent and easy to change? If both, it is a nudge; if either is not, be wary.

How it is computed (a sketch model): visual load = after merging elements that sit close together into a “chunk,” each group counts (the most prominent member’s weight + 0.25 × the weights of the rest), with always-on weight 1 and dimmed weight 0.35; central blocking = the rough share of the dashed middle area covered by elements (dimmed counted as 0.4); the grouping diagnostics score “like things grouped, unlike things apart.” These rules are teaching simplifications, not measured standards. The reference layout is only one common line of thought, and there is no single answer: whatever this second’s decision uses should stand out, and the rest give way.

Try itLegibility: contrast, and “not color alone”

Even the best layout is wasted if the text can’t be read. Contrast is the ratio of text luminance to background luminance. The AA level of the web accessibility standard WCAG 2.x requires at least 4.5 : 1 for normal text and at least 3 : 1 for large text and interface graphics. Games often overlay text on complex backgrounds, so take extra care. Pick two colors to try, then press “Grayscale view” to see what it looks like when colors can’t be told apart:

A few common cases: an enemy’s health bar doesn’t use only red but adds a “skull icon overhead”; important audio cues (such as footsteps) get a visual cue; a subway map, besides colors, gives each line a number. Conversely, white text over a bright sky background looks “okay” in a screenshot but vanishes on a phone screen in sunlight. Red–green color vision differences are fairly common (the often-cited figure is about 1 in 12 men, depending on the population). Grayscale is only an approximate simulation, but enough to test “is the information still there once color is removed?”

Common misconceptions

  • “The more complete the information, the happier the player.” Extra information adds extraneous load and often drowns the important information.
  • “Memory capacity is 7±2 digits.” Miller was talking about chunks; later estimates (such as Cowan’s) are more conservative, about 4 chunks.
  • “Defaults are neutral.” There is no neutral: the designer must pick one, and that choice affects the outcome.
  • “The less HUD, the classier.” The best density differs for newcomers and veterans; a minimalist HUD can also leave newcomers unable to find information.

Reflection and debate

  • Cognitive load theory is itself contested. The three kinds of load are hard to separate cleanly in experiments, the status of “germane load” has been substantially adjusted in the field, and the theory is still developing.
  • The Gestalt principles are empirical. They describe general tendencies, and players of different cultures, ages and eyesight won’t necessarily match exactly.
  • The effect of nudges is disputed. A study pooled by Mertens et al. in PNAS in 2022 found nudges have some effect, but Maier et al. then pointed out in the same journal that after correcting for publication bias there is almost no overall effect, though individual nudges may still work. So in your game you should verify with A/B tests yourself, not directly believe some average effect.
  • There is no clear line between nudging and manipulation. Some think gentle nudges are acceptable, while others criticize them as “paternalistic” intervention. This involves value judgments, so weigh it against your own project’s player base.

Key points

  • UI is divided by “is it in the story, is it in the space” into diegetic, spatial, meta and non-diegetic kinds; there is no right or wrong, only a trade-off between clarity and immersion.
  • Why the HUD was invented: pilots shouldn’t look down for information, so it was projected in front of their eyes; games inherited the method and also its problem: more and more can be put up, and brain capacity hasn’t changed.
  • Give information a priority: what the current decision uses stands out, the rest gives way or hides; the same element sits in different tiers in different scenes.
  • Cognitive load: working memory is tiny (a conservative estimate is about 4 chunks), so cut extraneous load; Gestalt: proximity, similarity, closure and continuity let a layout group itself, turning many elements into a few chunks.
  • Onboarding: do first and tell after, progressive disclosure, contextual hints; the default effect: defaults change most people’s choices, so use them in the player’s interest; text contrast should normally be at least 4.5:1, and don’t rely on color alone.
A good interface saves the player brain power: put in less information, let the layout group itself, and set defaults in the player’s interest.
