Chapter 3

Player Motivation

Why are people willing to spend their time on a game?

Learning goals

about 55 min
  • Use the three needs of autonomy, competence and relatedness to analyze a game feature. Dragging the “motivation triangle” shows which need a game values most.
  • Explain what Deci’s block experiment found: rewards can weaken interest. Explain the overjustification effect, and know how to give rewards more safely.
  • Explain how Bartle’s player types were distilled from an argument, know their limits, and explain why leaderboards, gifts and guilds attract people.
1

What it isThree basic psychological needs

Two people stay up late: the gamer’s eyes are shining, while the one doing homework yawns. The tiredness is the same, so what is different?

The psychologists Edward Deci and Richard Ryan studied this question. They found that when people do something, they have three psychological needs. First, to be in charge of themselves. Second, to feel capable. Third, to feel connected with others. When these three needs are met, people want to keep going on their own. This drive from inside is called intrinsic motivation. Deci and Ryan called this view self-determination theory.

Almost every popular game satisfies one or two of these needs.

Concept card

Self-Determination Theory

Self-Determination Theory, SDT for short

Edward Deci and Richard Ryan · their 1985 book established the theory

People have three basic psychological needs: autonomy, competence and relatedness. When all three are met, people want to keep doing something on their own.

ExampleA player gaming late into the night has bright eyes: the game lets them choose, lets them keep getting stronger, and lets them be with friends.

🧭

Autonomy

Being able to choose: an open world, designing your own character’s face, several ways to finish.

💪

Competence

Feeling “I’m getting stronger”: leveling up, rank rising, unlocking new skills.

🫂

Relatedness

Feeling “someone needs me”: teaming up, joining a guild, giving gifts, chatting.

Autonomy: Breath of the Wild: the player sees a mountain far away and can walk straight over to climb it; the game does not demand doing some particular thing first.
Competence: the action game Hades: each time the player dies, they remember more of the enemies’ moves and can get further next time.
Relatedness: Animal Crossing: the neighbors on the island remember the player, and friends can visit the island.
Daily life: learning guitar: choosing the songs you want to play (autonomy), finishing a first complete piece (competence), and jamming with friends (relatedness). With all three needs present, it is easy to keep going.
Counterexample: a tutorial you can neither skip nor choose in: the game leads the player step by step, and the player can neither skip nor choose. With no autonomy at all, however interesting the content, it easily gets annoying.
Counterexample: an event that unlocks only if you bring friends into the game: social contact that was voluntary becomes a task: to unlock the reward you have to go and recruit friends. The tie between friends turns into a burden.

Researchers have also used this theory to study video games. In 2006 Ryan, Rigby and Przybylski ran several studies. The result: the more players felt their autonomy, competence and relatedness were met, the more they enjoyed the game and the more willing they were to keep playing.

2

How it was inventedHow did a block experiment discover that “rewards can weaken interest”?

The problem: isn’t giving a reward supposed to make people love doing something more?

In the middle of the twentieth century the mainstream view in psychology was simple: do something, get a reward, and you will do it more afterwards; the bigger the reward, the more you do. But some people had doubts. Solving puzzles, drawing, playing games: often nobody gives a reward and people do them anyway, just because these things are interesting in themselves. So what happens if you add a reward to something already interesting?

While still a graduate student, Edward Deci designed an experiment. He had university students play an interesting Soma cube puzzle (seven irregular pieces made of small cubes, to be assembled into a given three-dimensional shape), three sessions in all. In the second session, one group was paid for every puzzle solved and the other group was not. In the third session, nobody was paid any more.

The key was the few minutes of rest in the middle of each session. The experimenter found an excuse to leave, and the participants could do whatever they liked, such as read a magazine or keep stacking the blocks. Deci recorded from behind one-way glass how long each person voluntarily stacked the blocks. During this time nobody asked and there was no reward, so the longer they stacked, the more interested they were.

Before looking at the result, guess first: in the third rest, which group is more willing to keep stacking the blocks, the group that was paid or the group that wasn’t?

Take the voluntary stacking time in the first rest as 100 (the dashed line). Drag the two sliders to enter your guesses for the third rest, then click “Reveal.” The bars are only guesses, not measured values.

1971 Deci publishes the paper “Effects of Externally Mediated Rewards on Intrinsic Motivation” (Journal of Personality and Social Psychology). The group promised “money for each puzzle solved” spent clearly less time voluntarily stacking blocks in the rest afterward. The unpaid group did not. Verbal praise (not money) actually showed signs of raising interest.
1973 Lepper, Greene and Nisbett had preschool children draw with colored markers and got a similar result. Children told beforehand that “drawing earns a certificate” drew less in later free time. Children who got a certificate only as a surprise after drawing, and children with no certificate at all, did not draw less. This is called the overjustification effect: the reward gives one more reason to do the thing, and the original reason is squeezed out.
1985–2000 Deci and Ryan organized this kind of finding into self-determination theory: people have three basic needs, autonomy, competence and relatedness. In 2000 they (Ryan & Deci) published a review paper, and the theory became a very influential theory of motivation.
1994–2001 The dispute: Cameron and Pierce pooled the data of many related studies and reanalyzed them (this is called a meta-analysis), concluding that the negative effect of rewards is limited. Deci and colleagues also ran a meta-analysis in 1999 (Psychological Bulletin), concluding that tangible rewards really do clearly weaken interest. The two sides still read the data differently.
Concept card

Overjustification Effect

Overjustification effect

Mark Lepper, David Greene, Richard Nisbett · 1973

Something done because you liked it, once a reward is added, feels like “done for the reward.” When the reward is withdrawn, interest is even lower than before.

ExampleDeci’s block experiment: the group that was paid spent less time voluntarily stacking blocks afterward.

3

Why it worksFeeling “controlled” crowds out “I want to”

A child loves drawing. An adult says to him, “Draw one, get a candy.” After a while the child thinks, “I draw for the candy.” When the candy stops, he doesn’t want to draw anymore.

The chapter on the core loop said that if a fixed coin is given too often, players get used to it and stop getting excited. A reward doesn’t only turn into a habit; it can also turn into a reason for doing the thing. Once “for the candy” is in the child’s mind, “because I like it” is pushed aside.

The “meaning” a reward carries matters more than its amount

The self-determination explanation is: if a reward makes a person feel “I must do this to get the reward,” it is control. Then the sense of autonomy falls, and intrinsic motivation falls with it. If a reward tells a person “you did well,” it gives information, and the sense of competence actually rises.

Four factors are often discussed. First, whether the reward is announced beforehand; announced ones are more easily taken as control. Second, whether the reward is tied to performance. Third, whether, when the reward is given, it is also said what was done well. Fourth, whether the thing was interesting to begin with: for something boring from the start, rewards usually don’t weaken interest.

Connection to player motivation: what gets squeezed out is “autonomy,” and what gets strengthened is “competence.” Only people who play from intrinsic motivation play for long. So rewards should not overshadow the fun of the game itself.

Sources:
Deci · 1971 · had university students play a block puzzle, with one group paid for correct solutions · the paid group afterward played voluntarily less (Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 18(1): 105–115).
Lepper, Greene, Nisbett · 1973 · had preschool children draw, with certificates announced beforehand · those children drew less afterward (same journal 28(1): 129–137).
Deci, Koestner, Ryan · 1999 · pooled analysis of a large number of experiments · concluded that announced tangible rewards weaken intrinsic motivation (Psychological Bulletin 125: 627–668).
Ryan & Deci · 2000 · review of self-determination theory (American Psychologist 55(1): 68–78). Some researchers (such as Cameron and Pierce) disagree.
In the game: a daily task list: a game you wanted to play, turned into a daily check-in where “if you don’t do it today you lose out.” Some players feel they have gone from “playing a game” to “going to work.”
Daily life: a reading reward for a child: every time the child reads, the parents give a little toy. Some children turn into “reading for the toy.”
The safe way: feedback that says “well done”: for example, after clearing a level, tell the player: “You used a way of playing few people use.” This speaks of the player’s ability and more easily strengthens competence.
Counterexample: a task that was boring anyway: if a thing was boring to begin with, adding a reward usually doesn’t weaken interest, because there was little interest to be squeezed out. So this effect has conditions and doesn’t appear every time.

Advanced: how the model computes

Drop in interest = (announced beforehand: 1, surprise: 0.1) × (no feedback: 1, feedback: 0.2) × 0.06 × reward strength × original interest. With feedback, interest also rises by 0.02 × reward strength × (100 − original interest). Engagement during rewards = original interest + 4 × reward strength, at most 100.

What it isFour classic player types

The three needs belong to everyone. But in the same game, one person only wants to fill the whole collection, and another burrows to the edge of the map looking for easter eggs. Everyone satisfies their needs in a different way.

The game designer Richard Bartle proposed that players broadly pursue four different kinds of fun, which can be sorted into four types. This way of dividing them is called Bartle player types. One person may also have several at once.

Concept card

Bartle Player Types

Bartle’s player types (Bartle taxonomy of player types)

Richard Bartle · 1996 paper “Hearts, Clubs, Diamonds, Spades: Players Who Suit MUDs”

Players are divided into achievers, explorers, socializers and competitors, by what each most loves in a game.

ExampleA player who spends their time visiting friends’ islands in Animal Crossing is a socializer; a player who tries every combination everywhere in Breath of the Wild is an explorer.

Horizontal axis: whether the fun comes mainly from the game world or from other players. Vertical axis: whether the player acts on things (collecting, defeating, say) or interacts back and forth with them (chatting, studying, say). Bartle’s original calls “competitors” “killers,” because in early multiplayer text games called MUDs they liked defeating other players.

🏆 Achievers

Love collecting, reaching max level, getting every achievement.
Example: completing the whole Pokédex in Pokémon, farming the best gear again and again in Diablo.

🗺️ Explorers

Love finding secrets, burrowing to the map’s edge, working out how the game operates.
Example: trying every combination of objects in Breath of the Wild, studying the game’s loopholes.

🎉 Socializers

The game is a place to be with friends.
Example: visiting friends’ islands in Animal Crossing, chatting in the guild of a multiplayer online game.

⚔️ Killers

Love measuring themselves against others, happiest when they beat an opponent.
Example: climbing the ranks in League of Legends, playing others in fighting games.

Daily life: the same tour group: some photograph everything and tick off the sights (achievers), some insist on ducking into alleys (explorers), some only want to chat with companions (socializers), and some want to compete over who gets the better spot (competitors).
Counterexample: one person is different types in different situations: an explorer when first starting, an achiever after reaching max level, a socializer when teaming up with friends. The classification describes a player’s “tendency at this moment,” not a fixed label.
5

How it was inventedHow did Bartle distill four player types from an argument?

The problem: veteran players started arguing: “What does everyone actually want from this world?”

Around 1978, Roy Trubshaw at the University of Essex in Britain wrote the earliest MUD, which Richard Bartle later took over and kept expanding. A MUD is a multiplayer adventure described only in text, where a group of people who didn’t know each other could for the first time be in the same virtual world at once. The operators soon hit a snag: change the same rule, and some people cheered while others turned and left. Whom should they listen to?

By Bartle’s own recollection, his 1996 paper “grew out of a long and heated discussion.” The discussion ran from November 1989 to May 1990 on the bulletin board (an online message area) of a British commercial MUD. The most experienced players posted several hundred messages arguing about “what people want from a MUD,” with about fifteen main participants. They did no questionnaire, just a great deal of observation and argument.

Bartle was then the chief administrator of that MUD. He gathered everyone’s views together and found that players’ behavior could be separated along two lines.

Why it worksIs the classification of players reliable? Bartle’s basis and limits

An observation from text MUDs, not a strict measurement

Bartle’s classification comes from his observation and summary of player discussion in early text MUDs. It is good for reminding designers not to design only by their own tastes. Bartle also proposed that different types of players affect one another: for example, as killers grow in number, socializers shrink in number.

This classification has three limits. First, it comes from a small group of players in one early game. Second, it is a rough summary, not measured with a questionnaire. Third, the same person changes type in different situations.

In 2006 Nick Yee surveyed a large number of players of massively multiplayer online role-playing games (MMORPGs) with an online questionnaire and arrived at three broad groups of motivation. Achievement: leveling up, mastering mechanics, competing with others. Social: making friends, keeping up relationships, teaming up. Immersion: exploring, role-playing, customizing appearance, using the game to escape from reality. It overlaps with Bartle’s classification but divides things differently. So “how many kinds of players there are” depends on the method used to measure.

Connection to player motivation: the “Bartle test” commonly found online is only a fun little quiz; it measures tendencies and can’t be taken as a judgment on a person.

Sources:
Bartle · 1996 · summarized a long discussion among players of one MUD · proposed four kinds of player (the paper Hearts, Clubs, Diamonds, Spades: Players Who Suit MUDs, Journal of MUD Research 1(1)).
Yee · 2006 · surveyed a large number of MMORPG players with a questionnaire · arrived at three broad groups of motivation, achievement, social and immersion (the paper Motivations for Play in Online Games, CyberPsychology & Behavior 9(6): 772–775).
Application in design: if a game has only competitive rankings, explorers and socializers have nothing to do. Add exploration play and cooperative play, and different players all have a place.
Daily life: a board game at a party: some only want to win (competitors), some only want everyone laughing together (socializers). If the rules are too complicated, socializers lose interest; if too simple, competitors get bored.
Counterexample: labeling players: someone looked like an achiever last week, so the game keeps handing them only achievement tasks, without noticing that this week they would rather chat with friends.
7

Why it worksWhy do leaderboards, gifts and guilds work? Social comparison, reciprocity and reputation

Running is the same, yet running quietly alone feels very different from watching how far others in your circle ran today. A friend sends a small gift, and you think: “Next time I’ll send one back.”

Three forces from human relationships are at work here. None of them was invented by games; games only put them where players meet them every day.

Three forces of human relationships

  • Social comparison: to judge whether they are doing well, people compare themselves with others, and prefer to compare with people similar to themselves. So for most people, “the person one place above me” motivates more than “first place on the whole server.”
  • Reciprocity: receiving a benefit, you want to repay it. Giving gifts, trading stamina, helping in battle all use this. The side effect is that not returning a gift creates pressure.
  • Reputation: being seen and recognized in a group is a very strong reward in itself, such as titles, a wall of achievements, a guild contribution board.

Connection to player motivation: these three forces mainly satisfy “relatedness” (being seen, being needed) and “competence” (stronger than before, stronger than companions). But rankings can also make people feel they are being graded, weakening “autonomy.” This is the same principle as the overjustification effect discussed earlier: once an outside goal gets strong, the feeling of wanting to do it yourself gets weak.

Sources:
Festinger · 1954 · proposed social comparison theory · people evaluate themselves by comparing with similar others (Human Relations 7(2): 117–140).
Gouldner · 1960 · discussed the norm of reciprocity · repaying others’ favors is a norm shared by many societies (American Sociological Review 25(2): 161–178).
The “motivates/discourages” in the figure below is a design inference based on these theories, not measured data.
Server-wide board: gives top players a stage to show off, but mid-ranked players and newcomers see ranks out of reach. Side effects: some use cheats to climb the board, the board is taken over by a few, and most people stop caring.
Friends board: compares you with people you know at a similar level, motivating the most players, and also satisfying “relatedness.” Side effect: people with no game friends have no board to look at.
Tiered board: players are grouped by level, so mid-ranked players and newcomers can see someone one place above them. Side effects: players grow anxious about dropping a tier; experts deliberately use low-tier alt accounts to beat newcomers.
Gift exchange: uses reciprocity, giving social and casual players one more reason to come back every day. But if “you must return the gift” it becomes a burden, so not returning should be allowed and not punished.
Counterexample: a casual game with only a server-wide board: most players’ ranks are forever hundreds of thousands deep, and one glance says “nothing to do with me.” The board makes people want to leave instead.

Common misconceptions

  • “The more rewards, the better they keep people”: if a reward makes people feel controlled, it squeezes out the original interest. Carrying “well done” information in the reward is safer.
  • “A server-wide leaderboard motivates people most”: it motivates only the very top few, and for most people may be discouraging. Tiered and friends boards are usually more suitable.
  • “A person belongs to only one player type”: one person’s type changes with situation, mood and time, and is often a mixture.
  • “The three needs must be equal”: not necessarily. A game can concentrate its effort on one need and take it to the extreme. But the designer must be clear about what is being neglected.

Reflection and debate

Self-determination theory is very influential, but researchers are still discussing whether “the three needs are the same in all cultures.” How big the overjustification effect is depends on the specifics, such as the kind of reward, whether the task was interesting itself, and whether the reward was announced beforehand. Not all studies reach the same conclusion.

Bartle’s classification is only one view, from early text games. Whether it applies to today’s mobile players, game communities and esports players is open to question. Yee’s statistical analysis gives another way of dividing. These classifications are good as thinking tools, not as the standard answer for profiling players.

Think: among the games you have played, which reward made you feel “bought off”? Which made you feel “I’m really good”? Where is the difference?

Key points

  • The three basic needs (self-determination theory): autonomy, competence, relatedness. Before designing a feature, ask: which one does it satisfy? Which does it neglect?
  • Deci’s block experiment found that an external reward announced beforehand can be taken as “control” and weaken intrinsic motivation. This is called the overjustification effect. A reward that carries “well done” information is safer.
  • Bartle’s four types of players come from an argument among veteran MUD players: achievers, explorers, socializers, competitors. It is a rough tool, types change with situation, and Yee and others arrived at a different division.
  • What the designer likes is not necessarily what players like. Learn to think from the position of players of other types.
  • Leaderboards, gifts and guilds work through social comparison, reciprocity and reputation. The form of a board should match the player types.
Make players feel “I want to, I can, we’re in this together,” and don’t let rewards take the credit for what is fun.
