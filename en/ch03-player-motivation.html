Chapter 3

Player Motivation

Why would anyone spend their time in your game?

Learning goals

about 55 min
  • Analyze a game feature through the three needs of autonomy, competence and relatedness, and drag the “motivation triangle” to see which need a game is betting on.
  • Explain why Deci’s block experiment found that “rewards weaken interest,” describe the overjustification effect, and say what makes a reward design safer.
  • Describe how Bartle’s player types were drawn from an argument, know their limits, and explain why leaderboards, gifts and guilds work.
1

What it isThree basic psychological needs

Two people stay up late: the gamer’s eyes are shining, while the one doing homework yawns. The tiredness is the same, so what is different?

The psychologists Edward Deci and Richard Ryan gave an answer called self-determination theory. Their view is that when all three needs are met while doing something, people want to keep going from within. This drive from the inside out is called intrinsic motivation.

Almost every popular game feeds one or two of them with precision.

🧭

Autonomy

Open worlds, character creators, many ways to finish.

💪

Competence

Difficulty curves, ranks and skill trees make you feel “I’m getting good.”

🫂

Relatedness

Teaming up, guilds, gifts and chat make you feel needed by others.

Autonomy: Breath of the Wild: see a faraway mountain and you can just go climb it, with nothing forcing you to do any one thing first.
Competence: a Hades-style action game: every death teaches you the enemies’ moves, and next time you get farther.
Relatedness: Animal Crossing: the neighbors remember you, and friends can visit your island.
Learning guitar: you pick the songs you want to play (autonomy), manage your first complete piece (competence), and play along with friends (relatedness). All three needs are there, so it is easy to stick with.
A tutorial you can neither skip nor choose in: the player is “led by the hand” step by step, and autonomy is taken away. Even if the content is interesting, it easily gets irritating.
An event that can only be unlocked by recruiting friends: “relatedness” is turned into a chore, and socializing becomes a burden.

Researchers have analyzed video games with this theory: several studies in 2006 by Ryan, Rigby and Przybylski found that players who felt their autonomy, competence and relatedness were met enjoyed the game more and were more willing to keep playing.

2

How it was inventedHow did a block experiment discover that “rewards can weaken interest”?

The problem: isn’t giving a reward supposed to make people love doing something more?

In the mid-twentieth century the mainstream view in behavioral psychology was simple: behavior is strengthened by reward, so the more reward, the more behavior. But some people had a nagging doubt: we do many things, solving puzzles, drawing, playing games, with nobody paying us. Is it just because they are “interesting in themselves”? Then what happens if you add a reward to something that is already interesting?

When Edward Deci was still a graduate student, he designed a clean test: college students played with an interesting Soma cube puzzle, over three sessions. In the second session, one group was paid for each puzzle solved and the other was not; in the third session nobody was paid.

The key was the few minutes of break in the middle of each session. The experimenter would leave on some pretext, and participants could do whatever they liked: read a magazine, or keep playing with the blocks. Deci watched from behind a one-way mirror and counted how much voluntary time they spent on the blocks. Nobody asked for it and nothing rewarded it, so this time became a “measuring stick” for interest.

Before looking at the result, make a prediction: in the free time of the third session, who will be more willing to keep playing, the group that was paid or the group that wasn’t?

1971 Deci publishes “Effects of externally mediated rewards on intrinsic motivation” (Journal of Personality and Social Psychology): the group promised money for each puzzle solved spent clearly less time playing with the blocks in the free time afterward; the unpaid group showed no such drop. Verbal praise (rather than money) actually showed signs of raising intrinsic interest.
1973 Lepper, Greene and Nisbett reproduce a similar phenomenon with preschool children and felt-tip pens: kids told in advance that drawing would earn them a certificate drew less in the free time afterward, while kids who got a surprise reward, or none, did not. They called it the overjustification effect.
1985–2000 Deci and Ryan develop these findings into self-determination theory: people have three basic needs, autonomy, competence and relatedness. A 2000 review (Ryan & Deci) makes it a very influential theory of motivation.
1994–2001 The debate: a meta-analysis by Cameron and Pierce concluded that the negative effects of rewards are limited; a 1999 meta-analysis by Deci and colleagues (Psychological Bulletin) concluded that tangible rewards do clearly undermine interest. The two sides still read the data differently.
3

Why it worksFeeling “controlled” crowds out “I want to”

A child who loves drawing, promised “a candy for every picture,” may come to feel “I draw for the candy.” When the candy stops, the child no longer wants to draw.

Remember the coins that expectation “ate up” in Chapter 2? A reward doesn’t just become an expectation; it becomes a reason. Once “for the candy” is in the head, “because I like it” gets pushed into a corner.

The “signal” of a reward matters more than its “amount”

Self-determination theory holds that when an external reward is perceived as control (“you must do this to get the reward”), it weakens the sense of autonomy and so weakens intrinsic motivation; if instead the reward carries information (“you did very well”), it strengthens the sense of competence. Factors often discussed include whether the reward was promised in advance (more likely to be read as control), whether it is tied to performance, whether it also gives feedback on ability, and whether the task was interesting to begin with (for tasks that were dull anyway, rewards usually do not weaken interest).

Link to player motivation: of the three needs, “autonomy” and “competence” are exactly what gets squeezed or strengthened. The design lesson of “only intrinsic motivation lasts” is: don’t let rewards take the credit for what is fun.

Sources: Edward Deci, “Effects of externally mediated rewards on intrinsic motivation,” Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 18(1): 105–115 (1971); Mark Lepper, David Greene and Richard Nisbett, same journal, 28(1): 129–137 (1973); Deci, Koestner and Ryan, Psychological Bulletin 125: 627–668 (1999); Richard Ryan and Edward Deci, American Psychologist 55(1): 68–78 (2000). Meta-analyses support the claim that “tangible rewards promised in advance weaken intrinsic motivation,” but other researchers disagree, and the field is still debating it.
Daily quest lists in games: a game you wanted to play is turned into a check-in where “skip today and you lose out,” and some players feel it has “gone from play to a job.”
Reading rewards for kids: a little toy for every reading session, and some kids end up “reading for the toy.”
The safe approach, “you did well” feedback: for instance, after you clear a level, being told “you used a very rare strategy” carries information about your ability and more easily strengthens competence.
A task that was dull to begin with: for an activity that was dull anyway, adding a reward often does not weaken interest, since there was little “intrinsic motivation” to crowd out. The effect has conditions and is not an iron law.

4

What it isThe four classic player types

The three needs describe the foundation all people share. But in the same game, one person only wants to fill up the collection log and another is bent on probing the edges of the map for easter eggs, so needs are clearly fed in different ways.

The designer Richard Bartle proposed that players broadly enjoy four different kinds of fun, the Bartle player types. One person can also mix several.

Horizontal axis: whether the fun comes mainly from the game world or from other players; vertical axis: whether people like to act on things or interact with them. In his original paper Bartle called the “competitors” killers, since in a MUD they enjoyed “defeating” other players.

🏆 Achievers

Love to collect, max out, and complete every achievement.
Examples: filling the whole Pokédex in Pokémon, farming best-in-slot gear in Diablo.

🗺️ Explorers

Love to find secrets, poke at the edges of the map, and study mechanics.
Examples: trying physics combinations everywhere in Breath of the Wild, studying the game’s bugs.

🎉 Socializers

The game is a place to be with friends.
Examples: visiting friends’ islands in Animal Crossing, guild chatter in an MMO.

⚔️ Killers

Love to measure themselves against others, and beating an opponent feels best.
Examples: climbing ranks in League of Legends, fighting-game matches.

A tour group: one person photographs every landmark (achiever), another insists on ducking into side alleys (explorer), another only wants to chat with companions (socializer), and another competes to see who got the better seat (killer).
The same person plays different types in different situations: someone who is an explorer when newly hooked becomes an achiever at max level and a socializer when playing with friends. The categories describe “a tendency right now,” not a fixed label.
5

How it was inventedHow did Bartle distill four kinds of players from a quarrel?

The problem: veteran players fell out over “what do people really want from this world?”

Around 1978, Roy Trubshaw at the University of Essex in Britain wrote the first multiplayer text adventure, the MUD, and Richard Bartle later took it over and expanded it. Games like this put a crowd of strangers into the same virtual world for the first time, and the operators soon ran into a problem: change a single rule, and some players cheer while others turn around and leave. Whose opinion should they follow?

By Bartle’s own account, his 1996 paper “grew out of a long and heated discussion.” From November 1989 to May 1990, the most experienced players of a British commercial MUD posted hundreds of messages on a bulletin board arguing about “what people want from a MUD,” with about fifteen main participants. They had no questionnaires, only lots of observation and argument.

Bartle, then chief administrator of that MUD, gathered up the views people had put forward and found that player behavior could be placed on two axes.

2006 Nick Yee studies online-game player motivation with a large questionnaire and factor analysis, arriving at three groups, achievement, social and immersion (10 subcomponents in all), giving another view on whether the categories can be supported by data.
6

Why it worksAre player types reliable? Bartle’s sources and limits

An observation from text MUDs, not a rigorous measurement

Bartle’s classification comes from his observation and summing up of discussions among early text MUD players. It works well as a reminder not to design only for yourself, and Bartle himself proposed that the types affect one another (for example, he thought more killers would mean fewer socializers). But it has several limits: it is based on a small group of players in a particular early game; the types are a rough generalization, not something measured by questionnaire; and the same person switches between types depending on context.

In 2006, Nick Yee surveyed large numbers of MMORPG players online and found three broad kinds of motivation: achievement (advancement, mechanics, competition), social (making friends, relationships, teamwork) and immersion (exploration, role-playing, customization, escape). It overlaps with Bartle’s classification but divides things differently, showing that “how many kinds of players there are” itself depends on how you measure.

Link to player motivation: the “Bartle tests” on the internet are only game-like tests of tendency; don’t treat one as a diagnosis of a person.

Sources: Richard Bartle, “Hearts, Clubs, Diamonds, Spades: Players Who Suit MUDs,” Journal of MUD Research 1(1) (1996); Nick Yee, “Motivations for Play in Online Games,” CyberPsychology & Behavior 9(6): 772–775 (2006).
Use in design: a game with only ranked matches leaves no place for explorers and socializers; adding exploration and cooperative play makes room for different people.
Party games: some only want to win (killers), others only want to laugh (socializers); if the rules are too heavy, socializers leave, and if they are too light, killers get bored.
Labeling players: because someone was an achiever last week, you give them nothing but achievement grinding forever, and may miss that this week they want to chat with friends.
7

Why it worksWhy do leaderboards, gifts and guilds work? Social comparison, reciprocity and reputation

Running alone feels different from running while seeing how far your friends ran today; when a friend gives you a small gift, you think “I’ll send one back next time.”

Three social forces are at work here. None was invented by games; games just plugged them into places players meet every day.

Three social forces

  • Social comparison: people judge how they are doing by comparing with others, and tend to compare with people similar to themselves. So “the next rank just above you” motivates more than “first on the whole server.”
  • Reciprocity: when you receive something, you want to repay it. Gifts, swapping stamina, and helping each other in battle all use this, though it can also create “pressure to return the favor.”
  • Reputation: being seen and recognized in a group is a powerful social reward, such as titles, record walls and guild contribution boards.

Link to player motivation: these mainly satisfy “relatedness” (being seen, being needed) and “competence” (I’m better than before, better than my peers). But beware: “controlling” external goals such as rankings can make people feel graded and weaken “autonomy,” the same principle as the overjustification effect earlier.

Sources: social comparison theory, Leon Festinger, “A Theory of Social Comparison Processes,” Human Relations 7(2): 117–140 (1954); the norm of reciprocity, Alvin Gouldner, “The Norm of Reciprocity: A Preliminary Statement,” American Sociological Review 25(2): 161–178 (1960). The “motivates / discourages” figures in the chart below are a design inference based on these theories, not measured data.
Global leaderboard: gives top players a stage, but mid-tier players and newcomers see ranks that are “out of reach.” Side effects: cheating to climb the board, a few people monopolizing it, and most staying silent.
Friends leaderboard: the comparison is with people you know at a similar level, so it motivates the widest range of players, and it also meets “relatedness.” Side effect: people with no friends get no board.
Tiered leaderboard: groups players by level, so both mid-tier players and newcomers can see “the next rank.” Side effects: anxiety about dropping a tier, and strong players deliberately dropping to “raise alt accounts” to beat newcomers.
Gift exchange: uses reciprocity, giving socializers and casual players more reasons to come back; but “must return the gift” becomes a burden, so it should be allowed to go unreturned without penalty.
A casual game with only a global leaderboard: most players are ranked in the hundreds of thousands, glance at it and feel “this has nothing to do with me,” and the board becomes a reason to leave.

Common misconceptions

  • “The more rewards, the more players stay”: if a reward is perceived as control, it may crowd out intrinsic interest; letting a reward carry “you did well” is safer.
  • “A global leaderboard motivates most”: it motivates only the very top few and may discourage most people; tiered and friends leaderboards are often friendlier.
  • “A person belongs to just one player type”: types change with context, mood and time, and are often mixed.
  • “The three needs must be balanced”: not necessarily; a game can bet heavily on one need and push it to the extreme, but should know what it is neglecting.

Reflection and debate

Self-determination theory is a very influential theory of motivation, but whether the “three needs” hold universally is still discussed; the size of the overjustification effect also depends on the situation (type of reward, whether the task is interesting, whether it is expected), and not all studies reach the same conclusion. The interactive models above are only teaching sketches.

Bartle’s classification is “one view,” drawn from a particular early text-game environment; whether it fits today’s mobile games, communities and esports players is open to question. Yee’s factor analysis gives another division. Treat them as thinking tools, not as the standard answer to what players look like.

Think about it: in a game you have played, which reward made you feel “bought off”? Which made you feel “I’m really good”? What was the difference?

Key points

  • The three needs: autonomy, competence, relatedness. Before designing a feature, ask which it meets and which it leaves out.
  • Deci’s block experiment found that an external reward promised in advance can be read as “control” and weaken intrinsic motivation (the overjustification effect, which depends on context); a reward that carries “you did well” is safer.
  • Bartle’s four player types came out of an argument among veteran MUD players: achievers, explorers, socializers, killers; it is a rough tool, types shift with context, and Yee and others arrived at a different division.
  • “What you like” is not necessarily “what players like”; learn to think from the standpoint of other player types.
  • Leaderboards, gifts and guilds work through social comparison, reciprocity and reputation; the form of the leaderboard should match the player type.
Make players feel “I want to, I can, we’re in this together,” and don’t let rewards take the credit for what is fun.
