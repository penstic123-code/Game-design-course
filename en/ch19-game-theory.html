Chapter 19

Game Theory & Cooperation

Everyone makes the best choice for themselves, so why does everyone end up worse off?

Learning goals

  • Write a game as “players, strategies, payoffs” and find its Nash equilibrium.
  • Explain why everyone being rational can still give a terrible result (the prisoner’s dilemma, the tragedy of the commons), and how the “shadow of the future” lets cooperation stand.
  • Say what question each of these concepts was invented to answer, and act as a “mechanism designer”: change the payoff structure so players want to cooperate, or deliberately leave a dilemma in place.
about 45 min
1

What it isA game: what you get depends on what others choose

Four players who don’t know each other form a party for a dungeon, and the chest drops just one piece of gear that all of them want. The system pops up two buttons: “Roll by the rules” and “Just take it.” Which do you press?

Don’t answer yet. Which one you press actually depends on which the other three press. If everyone rolls, each has a one-in-four chance, and all are content. If only you take it, the gear is yours and the others can only stare. But if all four think “the others will take it anyway,” four hands reach for the chest at once, the scene turns into chaos, the dungeon counts as failed, and nobody takes anything home.

Situations like “my result depends on your choice, and your result depends on mine” are what game theory studies. A game is itself a man-made arena of strategy, so a designer who understands game theory gets a toolbox for seeing through player behavior.

A game has three ingredients

🧑‍🤝‍🧑

Players

Who is making the decision? Two players, five fishermen, four teammates.

🎛️

Strategies

What options does each have? Cooperate or defect, roll or just take.

🏆

Payoffs

Under each combination of choices, how much does each person get? Numbers stand for “how much they want it.”

A game with only two players and two options each can be drawn as a 2×2 payoff matrix. The one below is the most famous, called the prisoner’s dilemma. In each cell the two numbers are A’s in blue and B’s in pink.

To read this picture, stand in A’s place. Suppose B cooperates: A defecting gets 5, more than the 3 for cooperating. Suppose B defects: A defecting gets 1, more than the 0 for cooperating. Whatever B chooses, defecting pays A more, and B, standing in their own place, thinks the same. So both defect and get 1 each, when cooperating could clearly have given them 3 each.

Fighting over gear in a dungeon: with “whoever grabs it keeps it,” looking only at themselves each person should grab. With all four grabbing, nobody fights the boss, the dungeon collapses, and everyone leaves empty-handed.
Bubble tea shops cutting prices on each other: if the other doesn’t cut, I cut and take their customers; the other thinks the same. In the end both drop to 8 yuan and both profits get thin, and only the customers benefit.
Group project: if each puts in a bit more, everyone shares the result but you bear the tiredness; if each puts in a bit less, the result is discounted. Everyone thinks “someone else will do it,” and the whole group gets a low grade.
A near miss: the lever door: a two-player puzzle with two levers beside a door, and as long as one person holds a lever, the door stays open for both. Here “not pulling” earns no more than “cooperating” (the payoff for defecting, T, does not exceed the payoff for cooperating, R), so it is not a prisoner’s dilemma. A dilemma needs “the temptation to defect” to be real.
2

What it isNash equilibrium and four common kinds of game

The lower-right cell of the prisoner’s dilemma has a name. If, in some combination of strategies, each person is already making the best response to what the others are currently choosing, and nobody can earn more by changing alone, that combination is called a Nash equilibrium.

There is a small trick for finding it: in each column, circle the larger of the row player’s payoffs; in each row, circle the larger of the column player’s payoffs. A cell that gets both circles is a (pure-strategy) Nash equilibrium. The payoff-matrix lab below marks them for you.

Meeting head-on on a narrow path: you keep right and I keep right, or you keep left and I keep left, are both equilibria, and neither wants to switch sides alone, or you collide. There can be more than one equilibrium, and sometimes custom has to choose among them.
Rock-paper-scissors: if I always play rock, the opponent switches to paper. A fixed play has no equilibrium, and the only stable point is for both to randomize with 1/3 each, which is called a mixed strategy.
A common misunderstanding: equilibrium means best: the only equilibrium of the prisoner’s dilemma, (1,1), is worse for both than (3,3). An equilibrium says only “nobody wants to move alone,” not that the result is good. Pareto optimality asks whether there is still room to make everyone better off.

Four typical kinds of game

The prisoner’s dilemma is only one of them. Change which payoffs are larger than which, and the same 2×2 grows four situations with completely different characters.

How it was inventedWhat questions were these concepts invented to answer?

The prisoner’s dilemma, Nash equilibrium, “tit for tat” sound like terms that fell from the sky. In fact each was forced into being by someone who ran into a problem they couldn’t solve. First a look at the whole relay:

Each solution leaves behind a new problem, and the next concept is born for it.

Story one: how the prisoner’s dilemma popped up

The dilemma at the time. In 1944 von Neumann and Morgenstern published Theory of Games and Economic Behavior, turning “what you get depends on my choice” into mathematics. What they could solve completely was zero-sum two-player confrontation: what you win I lose, and the best answer is “minimize the worst case.” But conflicts in the real world, such as arms races, trade and cooperation, are almost never zero-sum: everyone may get better together, or worse together.

The first attempt. In 1950 John Nash gave the Nash equilibrium: even if a game is not zero-sum, as long as everyone picks “the best response to others’ current choices,” a stable point must exist. But this brought a new trouble: would living people actually arrive at this stable point?

The key step. According to the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, in early 1950 Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher at the RAND Corporation devised a non-zero-sum 2×2 game and had two colleagues play it 100 times in a row, to see whether people would dutifully settle on the equilibrium. The results were later written into Flood’s RAND report (1952), and Nash even wrote comments on the draft. Then Albert Tucker, who had to explain this structure to psychologists at Stanford, made up a story of “two prisoners interrogated separately,” which is the form we know today. As for the name “Prisoner’s Dilemma,” the earliest printed use found so far is in Luce and Raiffa’s 1957 book, and it can’t be confirmed that Tucker himself coined it.

The 5/3/1/0 commonly used in textbooks today is a later standardization and not the original numbers of the experiment; the exact number of cooperations by those two subjects is reported differently in different retellings, so it is not quoted here.

🧩 Think first, then open: what is the smallest change that makes “both cooperate” an equilibrium too?

Go back to the lab above and, with the fewest changes, turn the prisoner’s dilemma into one where “both cooperating is also a Nash equilibrium.” Expand once you have thought of it.

A reference answer: just make “the temptation to defect” no larger than “the reward for cooperating,” that is T ≤ R. For example, change the two “5”s to 3: when the other cooperates, my cooperating (3) is no worse than defecting (3), so (cooperate, cooperate) becomes an equilibrium, the dilemma disappears, and the game turns into something like a stag hunt. What Flood and Dresher wanted to find out was exactly this kind of regularity: “change the structure and people’s behavior changes with it.”

Story two: Axelrod’s computer tournament

The dilemma at the time. Theory says a one-shot prisoner’s dilemma must land on (defect, defect). But in reality neighbors, colleagues and regular teammates often cooperate quite well. Where is the difference? One intuition: because we will meet again. But when we do meet again, what behavior strategy is best? Even mathematicians couldn’t solve it directly, because the opponent’s possible strategies are too numerous to count.

His approach. The political scientist Robert Axelrod thought of a way around the difficulty: don’t derive, hold a contest. He invited scholars to submit programs and had them play each other in the repeated prisoner’s dilemma, each pair playing 200 rounds. The first tournament had 14 programs (plus one purely random player), and the winner was the simplest one, submitted by Anatol Rapoport: tit for tat, which cooperates in the first round and then repeats the opponent’s previous move.

He tried again. Axelrod published the first round’s results and invited more people to “beat it.” The second round had 62 programs, many far more complicated, and tit for tat won again (two Axelrod papers in 1980; the 1984 The Evolution of Cooperation is the popular summary).

Story three: the tragedy of the commons, and why “everybody’s property” needed a name

The problem at the time. A pasture anyone can use, a sea anyone can fish, seems always to get used up. Why? Hardin traces it back to William Forster Lloyd’s 19th-century discussion of the “common pasture.” In 1968 the ecologist Garrett Hardin published “The Tragedy of the Commons” in Science and put this intuition into a clear structure: add one more sheep, the benefit goes to me and the cost is shared by all. This is the multiplayer version of the prisoner’s dilemma.

A new problem. Are there only the two roads of state regulation or privatization? The political economist Elinor Ostrom, in Governing the Commons (1990), studied many real communities: pastures, fisheries, irrigation systems. She found that many communities kept their common resources well managed for decades or centuries with rules they made, monitored and enforced themselves. She received the 2009 Nobel Prize in Economics for it (shared with Oliver Williamson).

Put the three stories back on one timeline and you see each step filling the gap the last one left:

1944 Von Neumann and Morgenstern publish Theory of Games and Economic Behavior, and zero-sum confrontation is “solved.” But what they couldn’t solve was exactly the most common kind of conflict in reality: everyone may get better together, or worse together.
1950 Nash publishes Equilibrium points in n-person games in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, proving that non-zero-sum games also have stable points. A stable point is like a ball at the bottom of a bowl: push it and it rolls back, and nobody wants to move alone.
Early 1950 Flood and Dresher at RAND have two colleagues play a non-zero-sum game 100 times in a row, to test whether people really arrive at equilibrium.
Afterward To explain the structure to psychologists at Stanford, Tucker makes up the story of “two prisoners interrogated separately.” The name is easy to remember and the structure is clear at a glance, and it spread from then on.
1968 Hardin publishes The Tragedy of the Commons (Science), turning “add one more sheep, the benefit is mine and the cost is shared” into a multiplayer prisoner’s dilemma everyone can understand.
1980 Axelrod’s two rounds of computer tournaments: the first with 14 programs plus 1 random player, each pair playing 200 rounds, the second with 62 programs, and tit for tat wins both (J. Conflict Resolution vol. 24).
1990 Ostrom publishes Governing the Commons, using many real community cases to refute “only regulation or privatization”; she wins the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2009.
1993 Nowak and Sigmund find in Nature that once someone slips, two tit-for-tats fall into endless mutual retaliation, while “win-stay, lose-shift” corrects itself and did better in their evolutionary simulations. In 2015 Rapoport, Seale and Colman wrote in PLoS ONE to warn against reading the tournament results as “tit for tat is right everywhere.”
4

Why it worksWhy do rational individuals head for bad outcomes?

Back to that (1,1). Why do two people who are clearly both smart land in a cell worse than cooperating?

The key is something called a dominant strategy: if an option is no worse than the others whatever the other chooses, it is dominant. In the prisoner’s dilemma, “defect” is dominant. Standing in A’s place, if the other cooperates, defecting gets me 5, more than cooperating’s 3; if the other defects, defecting gets me 1, more than cooperating’s 0. Both sides calculate this way, and both fall into the lower right.

So the bad result does not come from bad or stupid people but from the payoff structure: the benefit of defecting goes entirely to oneself, while the cost of defecting is borne by the other.

Two underlying principles: the trap of dominant strategies, and that an equilibrium always exists

Standard reasoning (a basic conclusion of game theory): a rational player won’t give up a dominant strategy, and when two players’ dominant strategies meet, the result is determined by them, even if it is worse for both than cooperating.

Nash’s proof: in 1950 Nash proved that as long as the players and strategies are finite in number (allowing mixed strategies, that is, choosing at random by probability), every game has at least one equilibrium. This theorem explains “why stable points are found everywhere,” but it only guarantees stability, not goodness. The stable point of the prisoner’s dilemma is exactly a bad result.

Sources: Nash (1950) Equilibrium points in n-person games, PNAS 36(1): 48–49; Flood & Dresher (1950, RAND), Flood (1952) RAND report; Luce & Raiffa (1957) Games and Decisions; for an overview see the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry “Prisoner’s Dilemma.”

This conclusion is very practical for designers: to change player behavior, don’t preach, change the payoffs.

The tragedy of the commons: a multiplayer prisoner’s dilemma

Hardin’s view: with no constraints, individuals using a shared resource head for overuse; his conclusion at the time leaned toward the need for outside coercion (state regulation or privatization). This is only Hardin’s view. Ostrom’s research shows that many communities have kept shared resources well managed over the long run with their own rules.

Sources: Hardin (1968) The Tragedy of the Commons, Science 162; Ostrom (1990) Governing the Commons; Ostrom won the 2009 Nobel Prize in Economics.

Why it worksMeeting again and again: how cooperation stands up

In real life we almost never meet an opponent only once. If the same people meet again and again, today’s betrayal must bear tomorrow’s retaliation. Then a one-shot prisoner’s dilemma becomes a repeated game.

Axelrod’s tournament gave a list of “what a good strategy looks like.” The programs that did well mostly had four traits: nice, never defecting first; retaliatory, hitting back at once when betrayed, not a pushover; forgiving, resuming cooperation as soon as the other turns back, without digging up old grudges; and clear, with simple, understandable behavior, so the opponent sees right away that “cooperation pays.” He also stressed not to envy the opponent’s score.

A counterintuitive detail: in any single one-on-one match, tit for tat never scored higher than its opponent, at most tying or losing slightly. It won on total score, because it kept up cooperation with many opponents.

Worldview principle: in repeated games, cooperation can “evolve”

This is Axelrod’s research conclusion. In 1981 he and the evolutionary biologist William Hamilton applied the same logic to the living world in Science: as long as individuals will meet again, reciprocity can hold its ground among a group of selfish individuals, with no one needing to give orders. In his 1984 The Evolution of Cooperation he also used a historical case: in the trench warfare of the First World War, British and German soldiers facing each other often reached a tacit understanding to fire less and not to shoot at each other’s supply times, forming “live and let live.” Axelrod, drawing on the historian Tony Ashworth’s research, took this as a real example of long-term interaction giving rise to cooperation.

Some disagree with this reading, though: political scientists such as Joanne Gowa argue that fitting the tacit understandings in the trenches into the prisoner’s dilemma is not rigorous. It can be taken as a very suggestive analogy, rather than a settled conclusion.

Sources: Axelrod & Hamilton (1981) The evolution of cooperation, Science 211: 1390–1396; Axelrod (1980) More Effective Choice in the Prisoner’s Dilemma, J. Conflict Resolution 24(3): 379–403; Axelrod (1984) The Evolution of Cooperation (including the chapter on trench warfare, based on Ashworth’s research).

For cooperation to stand, there is one more condition: there must be a next meeting, and it must matter enough. Axelrod called this the shadow of the future, written w: after each round there is a probability w of continuing to the next, so on average 1/(1−w) rounds are played.

If the opponent plays tit for tat, you can work it out: cooperating all along gives R per round, R/(1−w) in total; defecting forever gives T the first round and then only P per round, T + wP/(1−w) in total; alternating one defection and one cooperation gives (T + wS)/(1−w²). For cooperation to pay, you need w ≥ max[(T−R)/(T−P), (T−R)/(R−S)]. Putting in T=5, R=3, P=1, S=0 gives w ≥ max(1/2, 2/3) = 2/3, that is, an average of at least 3 more meetings.

Source: Axelrod (1984) The Evolution of Cooperation; the standard values are used here.

The three curves are drawn directly from the formulas above (T=5, R=3, P=1, S=0, the opponent playing tit for tat). When w is small, defecting wins; past the crossing at w = 2/3, cooperating throughout becomes best. In the strategy map later, you can feel the same thing with the “rounds per match” slider.

Anonymous ranked play: one meeting per game (w≈0), and blaming others, going AFK and stealing kills all become “rational.” Teammates have no tomorrow, and betrayal costs nothing.
A small shop on an old street: a one-off business at a scenic-spot gate easily overcharges, while a small shop on the old street dares not, because the customer will be back tomorrow and the neighbors will gossip. Fixed squads and friend lists stretch w, and the same people start looking after each other.
Reputation systems: honor levels, ratings, blacklists let “the next stranger” also see what you did this time, which is lending the shadow to strangers.
Counterexample: the endgame effect: on the last day of a season, everyone knows there will be no meeting tomorrow, w plunges, and as settlement nears AFKing, score-stealing and ditching teammates suddenly rise. Defect in the last round, and the round before gets dragged down too.

How to use itA designer is a “mechanism designer”

You have seen that behavior is forced out by the payoff structure. So think the other way round: if I want players to do this, how should the rules change? This branch of game theory is called mechanism design, and game designers do it every day.

There are only two directions: to make players cooperate, change the payoffs so defecting is no longer dominant; to get tension, do the reverse and keep the temptation well preserved.

To make players cooperate

Change the payoff structure→Defecting is no longer dominant→Cooperation becomes an equilibrium
  • Shared rewards: pay out only if the whole team clears, or tie part of individual payoff to the team’s result.
  • Repeat meetings: fixed squads, guilds, friend lists, to lengthen “the shadow of the future.”
  • Reputation: honor levels, ratings, blacklists, so that others’ “next time” can also see what you did this time.
  • Channels to punish betrayal: reporting, vote-kicks, point deductions, so that betrayal has a cost.

To create tension

Keep the temptation→Both cooperation and betrayal make sense→Mutual suspicion
  • Give betrayal a real temptation (T clearly larger than R).
  • Make it so people can’t see clearly what others chose (games like Werewolf and Among Us deliberately create uncertainty).
  • Make “cooperation” risky too (stag-hunt style: trusting the wrong person costs a lot).
  • Limit communication, or make promises unenforceable.
Splitting loot in a dungeon: “whoever grabs it keeps it” is a textbook prisoner’s dilemma. Common fixes are personal loot (each has their own share and the competition disappears) or a points system (today’s patience becomes tomorrow’s priority, stretching the shadow).
Social-deduction games like Among Us: deliberately keep the temptation (the impostor’s payoff is far above the crew’s) and cover up information, turning the dilemma into a source of fun.
A chore chart in a shared flat: replacing a vague “everyone pitches in” with a clear rotation and a charge is changing the payoff structure.
A near miss: a shared reward with invisible contributions: the whole team shares the reward, but nobody can see anyone’s contribution, so “free riding” appears: the one who is AFK gets the same reward as you. Sharing solves only half of it, and you still need contributions to be visible and betrayal to have a cost. A reporting system is the same: if nobody handles reports, the cost of betrayal is still 0.

Want to verify for yourself that “changing the rules changes behavior”? Go back to the pond and turn the rule knob to 2; or go back to the lab and lower T from 5 to 3.

Suggested experiments: ① Pull the rounds per match to 1: they meet only once, the peak jumps to the lower left (always defect), and this is the ordinary prisoner’s dilemma. ② Increase the rounds step by step: after a few rounds the peak slides to the lower right (tit for tat), and the “nice but retaliatory” region rises. ③ Set the slip rate to 5%: looking along the rightmost column (a=1), b=0 (pure tit for tat) is already worse than b≈0.2 (a bit forgiving); the highest point of the whole map may also jump to odd places, because the list includes exploitable opponents like “always cooperate,” and the shape of the mountain depends on the opponent list. ④ For the evolution, try each of the three initial populations: the result may depend on the initial proportions, so look at the chart honestly and don’t just trust the text.

Method: all strategies are probabilistic strategies that “look only at the last round,” and a match between two of them can be computed exactly as a Markov chain for the expected score per round, so there is no random jitter in the results; a slip means each move has that probability of being carried out as its opposite, and the other side sees the move as carried out.

Common misconceptions

  • “The prisoner’s dilemma shows human nature is evil.” No. The dilemma comes from the payoff structure, not from people’s character. Equally kind people placed in different payoff structures behave completely differently.
  • “A Nash equilibrium is the best outcome.” No. It is only the stable point where “nobody wants to change alone,” and the equilibrium of the prisoner’s dilemma is exactly bad for both.
  • “Tit for tat is always the strongest strategy.” No. Its strength is relative to that set of opponents; in one-on-one play it never beats its opponent either; and once slips are frequent you will see it suffer with your own eyes.

Reflection and debate

  • The limits of the model. In reality payoffs are not just scores: fairness, face and the urge to retaliate all change the “payoff.” Many experiments found that in social dilemmas like the prisoner’s dilemma people often choose to cooperate, and cooperation clearly increases once communication is allowed (see Balliet 2010, a meta-analysis of communication and cooperation, J. Conflict Resolution 54(1): 39–57). This doesn’t match the prediction that “rationality must defect,” and reminds us that the “rationality” in the model is too narrow.
  • Noise breaks tit for tat. You have already verified it in the strategy map: once slips appear, two tit-for-tats fall into a cycle of “mutual retaliation.” Nowak and Sigmund later proposed more forgiving strategies or “win-stay, lose-shift,” which did better in their simulations. But in a version where the two take turns, the result is different again, so the tournament’s conclusion is not final.
  • Results depend on the population and the list. Evolution results change with the initial proportions; the tournament winner depends on the entrant list. Rapoport, Seale and Colman (2015) also wrote to warn against treating Axelrod’s conclusions as universal theorems.
  • Hardin and Ostrom. “The tragedy of the commons needs outside coercion” is Hardin’s view; Ostrom’s research shows communities can govern themselves successfully too. Both views have empirical support, each applying under different conditions.
  • Should mechanisms be used to steer players? “Designers should use mechanisms to guide players toward cooperation, not preach” is a common view among mechanism designers. The objection is that treating players as variables to be tuned may turn the game into manipulation of behavior.

Key points

  • A game consists of players, strategies and payoffs; a Nash equilibrium is a combination in which nobody wants to change alone, and every finite game has at least one.
  • Four typical games: the prisoner’s dilemma (defecting dominates), stag hunt (trust needed), chicken (who yields first), zero-sum (pure confrontation).
  • Why they were invented: zero-sum theory could not handle conflicts where “everyone may get better or worse together,” so the Nash equilibrium came; RAND’s experiment and the prisoner story tested it; Axelrod used a tournament to answer “what strategy is good when repeated”; Hardin and Ostrom carried it to shared resources.
  • When rational individuals lead to bad outcomes, the root is the payoff structure: individual benefit and collective benefit have been split apart (the prisoner’s dilemma, the tragedy of the commons).
  • Repeated meetings change the game: strategies that are nice, retaliatory, forgiving and clear do well; cooperation needs a long enough shadow of the future (w ≥ 2/3 under the standard values); when slips are common, be more forgiving.
  • The designer is a mechanism designer: change the payoff structure (sharing, repetition, reputation, punishment channels, quotas) to bring about cooperation, or keep the temptation to create tension.
Players haven’t turned bad; the payoff structure makes defecting pay. To get cooperation, change the rules and let everyone “meet again.”

Pick a scene in a multiplayer game you know well where “personal interest and team interest conflict” (grabbing resources, grabbing kills, whether to save a teammate…). ① Write down the players, strategies and rough payoffs; ② judge whether it is more like a prisoner’s dilemma, a stag hunt or chicken; ③ propose one rule change and explain how it changes the payoffs so the behavior you want becomes an equilibrium; ④ think: did your change lengthen the “shadow of the future”?
