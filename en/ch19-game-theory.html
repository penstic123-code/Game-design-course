Chapter 19

Game Theory & Cooperation

Everyone makes the best choice for themselves, so why does everyone end up worse off?

Learning goals

  • Write a game down as “participants, strategies, payoffs,” and find its Nash equilibrium.
  • Explain why everyone making the best choice for themselves can still lead to a terrible result (the prisoner’s dilemma, the tragedy of the commons), and how the “shadow of the future” keeps cooperation going.
  • Say what question each of these concepts was proposed to answer. And play mechanism designer for once: change the scores in the rules so players want to cooperate, or leave a dilemma in on purpose.

1

What it isGame theory: your gains depend on what others choose

Four players who don’t know each other form a party for a dungeon, and the chest drops a single piece of gear that everyone wants. The system pops up two buttons: “Roll by the rules” and “Just take it.” Which one do you press?

The result depends on what the other three press. If all four roll, each has a one-in-four chance and everyone accepts it. If only one person just takes it, the gear is his and the other three get nothing. If all four think “the others will grab it anyway” and all four grab at once, the scene turns to chaos, the dungeon is declared failed, and nobody takes anything home.

In this kind of situation, what I get depends on how you choose, and what you get depends on how I choose. The mathematical theory that studies it is called game theory. A game is itself a man-made arena of strategic play, and a designer who understands game theory gains a set of tools for explaining player behavior.

A game has three ingredients

🧑‍🤝‍🧑

Players

The people who make decisions, for example two players, five fishermen, four teammates.

🎛️

Strategies

What options does each have? Cooperate or defect, roll or just take.

🏆

Payoffs

Under each combination of choices, how much does each person get? Numbers stand for “how much they want it.”

A game with only two participants and only two options each can be drawn as a 2×2 table called a payoff matrix. The one below is the most famous. A and B each choose “cooperate” or “defect.” In each cell there are two numbers: blue is what A gets, pink is what B gets.

To read this table, stand in A’s place first. If B cooperates, A gets 5 by defecting and only 3 by cooperating. If B defects, A gets 1 by defecting and only 0 by cooperating. So whatever B chooses, A gets more by defecting. B, standing in his own place, works out the same result. So both defect and each gets 1. Yet if both had cooperated, each could have got 3. Everyone’s arithmetic is right, yet together they all lose out. This kind of situation is the prisoner’s dilemma.

Concept card

Prisoner’s Dilemma

Prisoner’s Dilemma

Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher · designed this game in 1950; Albert Tucker told it as a story about prisoners

Whatever the other side chooses, defecting pays better for you. But if both defect, it is worse than if both had cooperated.

ExampleFighting over gear in a dungeon: all four rush to grab it, nobody fights the boss, the dungeon fails, and nobody gets anything.

Fighting over gear in a dungeon: when the rule is “whoever grabs it gets it,” everyone looks only at themselves and should grab. All four grab together, nobody goes to fight the boss, the dungeon fails, and nobody gets anything.
Milk-tea shops cutting prices on each other: if the other doesn’t cut, I cut and take their customers. The other thinks the same. In the end both drop to 8 yuan, both profits thin, and only the customers gain.
Group homework: each person who puts in extra effort shares the result with the whole group, while the tiredness is theirs alone. If each puts in less, the result is discounted. Everyone thinks “someone else will do it,” and the whole group gets only a low mark.
Almost, but not quite: the lever door: a two-person puzzle has two levers beside a door, and as long as one person holds a lever, the door stays open and both can pass. Here “not pulling” earns nothing more than “pulling,” and nobody gains by defecting, so it is not a prisoner’s dilemma. A prisoner’s dilemma needs a real “benefit of defecting.”

What it isNash equilibrium and four common kinds of game

The bottom-right cell of the prisoner’s dilemma has one feature: both chose to defect, and as long as the other stays put, whoever switches to cooperating gets less. Nobody can gain anything by “changing only their own choice.” Mathematically, a combination of choices like this is called a Nash equilibrium.

Concept card

Nash Equilibrium

Nash equilibrium

John Nash · 1950

In this combination of choices, as long as the others stay put, whoever switches their own choice gets no more.

Example“Both defect” in the prisoner’s dilemma: when the other defects, switching to cooperation only takes you from 1 point to 0.

There is a small trick for finding a Nash equilibrium. Step one: in each column of the table, circle the larger of A’s two numbers. Step two: in each row, circle the larger of B’s two numbers. A cell where both circles appear is a Nash equilibrium (we discuss only the case of always picking a fixed option, called a pure strategy). The payoff-matrix lab below marks them automatically.

Meeting head-on on a narrow path: two people walk toward each other on a narrow path. If the other keeps right, it is best for me to keep right too; if the other keeps left, it is best for me to keep left too. Both cases are equilibria, because if anyone switches sides alone, the two will collide. There can be more than one equilibrium, and which one is chosen sometimes depends on everyone’s habits.
Rock-paper-scissors: if I always play rock, the opponent will switch to paper. So no fixed way of playing is an equilibrium. The only stable point is for both sides to play at random, one third each of rock, scissors and paper. This way of choosing at random by probability is called a mixed strategy.
A common misunderstanding: equilibrium means best: the prisoner’s dilemma’s only equilibrium is (1,1), which is worse for both than (3,3). Equilibrium says only that “nobody wants to change alone,” not that the result is good. To ask “is there still a way to make everyone better off,” look at Pareto optimality.

Advanced: how the program judges the game type

Take the prisoner’s dilemma numbers as an example: what a lone defector gets, 5 (call it T), is greater than what each gets when both cooperate, 3 (R), which is greater than what each gets when both defect, 1 (P), which is greater than what a lone cooperator gets, 0 (S). How it was inventedWhat questions were these concepts invented to answer?

The prisoner’s dilemma, Nash equilibrium, “tit for tat”: these terms sound as if they came from nowhere. In fact each one was thought up because someone met a problem they could not solve. Look at the whole process first:

Each solution leaves behind a new problem, and the next concept is born for it.

Story one: how the prisoner’s dilemma popped up

In 1944, von Neumann and Morgenstern published Theory of Games and Economic Behavior, for the first time explaining in mathematics that “my gains depend on your choice.” What they could fully solve was only zero-sum two-person confrontation, in which whatever one side wins, the other loses. But the conflicts of the real world, such as arms races, trade and cooperation, are almost never zero-sum: both sides may get better together, or worse together.

Next, John Nash dealt with non-zero-sum conflicts. In 1950 he put forward the Nash equilibrium and proved that even when a game is not zero-sum, as long as everyone chooses “the best choice for themselves given what the others currently choose,” a stable outcome must exist. But this outcome exists only in mathematics: would real people really get there?

According to the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s account, in early 1950 Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher at the RAND Corporation (a US research institution) designed a non-zero-sum 2×2 game and had two colleagues play it 100 times in a row, to see whether people would land on the equilibrium. Later, Albert Tucker, needing to explain the structure to Stanford psychologists, made up a story about “two prisoners interrogated separately.” That gave the prisoner’s dilemma its name and its story.

The 5, 3, 1, 0 commonly used in textbooks today are a later tidied-up way of writing it, not the original numbers of the experiment. How many times the two subjects actually cooperated is reported differently in different sources, so it is not written here.

🧩 Think first, then open: what is the fewest numbers to change that make “both cooperate” an equilibrium too?

Back in the lab above, use the fewest changes to turn the prisoner’s dilemma into a situation where “both cooperate” is also a Nash equilibrium. Think it through before reading on.

Reference answer: make what a lone defector gets no longer more than what each gets when both cooperate. For example, change the two “5”s to 3. Then, when the other cooperates, cooperating gives me 3 and defecting is also 3, so cooperation costs nothing, and (cooperate, cooperate) becomes an equilibrium. The dilemma disappears and the game turns into something like a stag hunt. Change the numbers and people’s choices follow.

Story two: Axelrod’s computer tournament

Theory says that a prisoner’s dilemma played only once must end at (defect, defect). But in reality neighbors, colleagues and regular teammates often cooperate quite well. Where is the difference? One intuition: because they will meet again. But when they meet again, what strategy is best? Even mathematicians couldn’t work it out directly, because the strategies the opponent might use are too many to count.

The political scientist Robert Axelrod thought of a way around this difficulty: instead of deriving it, hold a competition. He invited scholars to submit programs, and had the programs play each other, 200 rounds per pair, under the rules of a repeated prisoner’s dilemma. The first competition had 14 programs plus one purely random player. The champion was the simplest one, submitted by Anatol Rapoport, called tit for tat: cooperate in the first round, then copy the opponent’s previous move in every round after.

Axelrod published the results of the first round and invited more people to “beat tit for tat.” The second round had 62 programs entered, many of them far more complex. Tit for tat won again (Axelrod 1980; the 1984 The Evolution of Cooperation is the summary written for the public).

Story three: the tragedy of the commons: why things anyone can use are easily ruined

A pasture anyone may graze, a sea anyone may fish, often gets ruined. Why? In 1968 the ecologist Garrett Hardin published “The Tragedy of the Commons” in Science and explained why: one more sheep, the benefit is all mine, and the cost of the ruined pasture is shared by everyone. Everyone reckons this way, and the pasture is eaten bare. It is the multiplayer version of the prisoner’s dilemma.

On Hardin’s view, only two roads seem left, state regulation or division into private property. Are there really only these two? The political economist Elinor Ostrom, in Governing the Commons (1990), examined many real communities: pastures, fisheries, irrigation systems. She found that many communities made their own rules, did their own monitoring and punished violators themselves, managing common resources for decades or even centuries. She won the 2009 Nobel Prize in Economics for this.

Put the three stories on one timeline, and each step fills the gap left by the one before:

1944 Von Neumann and Morgenstern publish Theory of Games and Economic Behavior, and zero-sum confrontation has a complete solution. But the most common kind of conflict in reality is still unsolved: both sides may get better together, or worse together.
1950 Nash publishes Equilibrium points in n-person games in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, proving that non-zero-sum games also have stable outcomes. A stable outcome is like a ball at the bottom of a bowl: push it and it rolls back, so nobody wants to change alone.
Early 1950 Flood and Dresher at RAND have two colleagues play a non-zero-sum game 100 times in a row, to test whether people really arrive at equilibrium.
Afterward Tucker, to explain the structure to Stanford psychologists, makes up the story of “two prisoners interrogated separately.” The story is easy to remember and the structure is clear at a glance, and this telling has spread ever since.
1968 Hardin publishes The Tragedy of the Commons (Science), explaining the multiplayer prisoner’s dilemma with “one more sheep, the benefit is mine, the cost is shared.”
1980 Axelrod runs two computer tournaments: the first with 14 programs plus 1 random player, the second with 62 programs, and tit for tat won both (J. Conflict Resolution, vol. 24).
1990 Ostrom publishes Governing the Commons, rebutting “only regulation or privatization” with many cases of real communities; in 2009 she wins the Nobel Prize in Economics.
1993 Nowak and Sigmund find in Nature that as soon as someone occasionally makes a wrong move (a slip), two tit-for-tat players fall into endless mutual retaliation. Win-stay, lose-shift can correct its own errors and does better in their evolutionary simulations. In 2015 Rapoport, Seale and Colman warned: the tournament conclusions should not be read as “tit for tat is best in every situation.”

Why it worksWhy do rational individuals head for bad outcomes?

Back to that (1,1). Two people who are both clearly clever: why do they end up in a cell worse than cooperation?

When the other cooperates, A gets 5 by defecting, more than the 3 for cooperating; when the other defects, A gets 1 by defecting, more than the 0 for cooperating. Whatever the other chooses, defecting gets more, and such an option is a dominant strategy; “defect” in the prisoner’s dilemma is one. A and B both reckon this way, and both land in the bottom-right corner.

Neither of the two is bad or stupid. The problem lies in the payoff structure: the benefit of defecting goes entirely to oneself, while the cost of defecting is borne by the other.

Two basic principles: the trap of dominant strategies, and the fact that an equilibrium always exists

The first (a basic conclusion of game theory): someone who thinks only of their own gains will not give up a dominant strategy. When two players’ dominant strategies meet, the outcome is decided by them, even if that outcome is worse for both than cooperating.

The second (Nash’s proof): in 1950 Nash proved that as long as the number of participants and options is finite, and choosing at random by probability is allowed (mixed strategies), every game has at least one equilibrium. This explains why stable outcomes can be found in all kinds of games. But it guarantees only stability, not a good result.

Why it holdsMeeting again and again: how cooperation lasts

In reality you almost never meet the same person only once. If the same people meet again and again, today’s defection will be retaliated against tomorrow. Then the prisoner’s dilemma played once becomes a repeated game.

Concept card

Repeated Game

Repeated game

Representative study: Robert Axelrod · the 1980 computer tournaments

The same people play the same game over and over. Today’s defection will be retaliated against tomorrow.

ExampleShops on an old street don’t dare to overcharge: the customer will come tomorrow, and the neighbors will talk.

Axelrod’s tournaments produced a list of “traits of a good strategy.” The programs that did well mostly had four traits. Nice: never defect first. Retaliating: hit back at once when betrayed. Forgiving: resume cooperation as soon as the other turns back. Clear: simple behavior, so the opponent sees at a glance that “cooperation pays.” He also stressed: don’t envy the opponent’s score.

A counterintuitive detail: in no single one-on-one match did tit for tat score higher than its opponent; at most it tied, or scored slightly lower. It won on total score, because it kept cooperation going with many opponents.

Worldview principle: in repeated games, cooperation can “evolve” on its own

In 1981 Axelrod and the evolutionary biologist William Hamilton carried the same reasoning into biology in Science. Their conclusion: as long as individuals meet again, reciprocity can persist among self-interested individuals without anyone giving orders. In his 1984 The Evolution of Cooperation Axelrod also gave a historical example. In the trench warfare of the First World War, British and German soldiers facing each other for long periods often reached a tacit understanding to fire less, a state of “live and let live.” Relying on the historian Tony Ashworth’s research, he took this as a real example of long-term interaction breeding cooperation.

Political scientists such as Joanne Gowa and others disagree with this reading. They think fitting the tacit understanding in the trenches to the prisoner’s dilemma is not rigorous, and at best a suggestive analogy.

Axelrod, Hamilton · 1981 · carried the logic of cooperation in repeated games into biology (The evolution of cooperation, Science 211: 1390–1396).

For cooperation to last, one more condition is needed: there will be a next meeting, and the next meeting matters to both sides. Axelrod called “how much the next interaction matters” the shadow of the future. The longer the shadow, the less worthwhile it is to defect today.

Let’s use concrete numbers. Suppose after each round there is a two-thirds chance of playing another, so on average 3 rounds are played. The opponent uses tit for tat. If I always cooperate, I get 3 points a round, 9 points in all on average. If I defect in round one, I get 5 points, then the opponent defects too and I get only 1 point a round, only 7 points in all on average. 9 against 7: cooperating pays better.

If the chance of meeting again is only one half, with 2 rounds played on average: always cooperating gets 6 points, always defecting also gets 6, a tie. With a lower chance, defecting pays better. Only at two thirds does always cooperating lose neither to always defecting nor to “alternating defection and cooperation.”

Advanced: derivation of the formula

Let w be the probability of continuing to the next round after each round, so on average 1/(1−w) rounds are played. With an opponent using tit for tat: cooperating forever totals R/(1−w). Defecting forever totals T + wP/(1−w). Alternating one defection and one cooperation totals (T + wS)/(1−w²). For cooperation to pay, you need w ≥ max[(T−R)/(T−P), (T−R)/(R−S)]. Putting in T=5, R=3, P=1, S=0 gives w ≥ max(1/2, 2/3) = 2/3, which means meeting on average at least 3 more rounds.

Source: Axelrod · 1984 · put forward the “shadow of the future” in The Evolution of Cooperation and worked out the conditions under which cooperation holds (standard values are used here).

The three curves are drawn with scores 5, 3, 1, 0, with the opponent using tit for tat, and the horizontal axis w is the probability of meeting again. When w is small, defecting scores highest. Once w passes 1/2, always cooperating overtakes always defecting. Once w passes 2/3, even “alternating defection and cooperation” can’t match always cooperating, and always cooperating becomes the best choice.

Anonymous ranked play: you meet a teammate only once a game, so the chance of meeting again is nearly 0. Blaming others, going AFK and stealing kills all become “worthwhile”: there is no tomorrow between teammates, and defection costs nothing.
A shop among old neighbors: a one-off business at a scenic spot’s gate easily overcharges, but a shop on an old street doesn’t dare, because the customer will come tomorrow and the neighbors will talk. In games, regular parties and friend systems make players more likely to meet again, and the same people start looking after each other.
Reputation systems: honor ranks, reviews and blacklists let the next stranger who meets this person also see what they did this time. This is lending the shadow to a stranger.
Counterexample: the endgame effect: on the last day of a season, everyone knows there will be no meeting tomorrow, the chance of meeting again plunges, and near settlement time, people who go AFK, grab points and sabotage teammates suddenly multiply. They defect in the last round, and the round before it gets dragged along too.

How to use itA designer is a “mechanism designer”

Player behavior is forced out by the arrangement of scores. Then think the other way around: if you want players to do this, how should the rules change? The branch of game theory that studies this problem is called mechanism design. Game designers do it every day.

There are two directions. To make players cooperate, change the payoffs so that defecting is no longer a dominant strategy. To create tension, do the opposite and keep the temptation to defect.

To make players cooperate

Change the payoff structure→Defecting is no longer dominant→Cooperation becomes an equilibrium
  • Shared rewards: the reward comes only if the whole team clears, or part of an individual reward depends on the team’s result.
  • Repeat meetings: fixed squads, guilds, friend lists, to lengthen “the shadow of the future.”
  • Reputation: honor ranks, reviews and blacklists let others see this time’s behavior next time too.
  • Channels to punish betrayal: reporting, vote-kicks, point deductions, so that betrayal has a cost.

To create tension

Keep the temptation→Both cooperation and betrayal make sense→Mutual suspicion
  • Give defection a real temptation: what a lone defector gets must be clearly more than what each gets when both cooperate.
  • Let players be unable to see others’ choices. Games like Werewolf and Among Us deliberately create uncertainty.
  • Make cooperation risky too (stag-hunt type: trusting the wrong person is very costly).
  • Limit communication, or make promises unenforceable.
Dividing loot in a dungeon: “whoever grabs it gets it” is a typical prisoner’s dilemma. There are two common fixes. One is personal loot: each person gets their own share, no grabbing. The other is a points system: giving up an item today earns priority tomorrow, which in effect lengthens the shadow of the future.
Social deduction games like Among Us: they deliberately keep the temptation (the impostor’s payoff for winning is far above the crew’s), then hide information, so the dilemma becomes a source of fun.
A chore chart in a shared flat: replacing a vague “everyone take initiative” with a clear rota is changing the payoff structure.
Almost, but not quite: a shared reward where contributions are invisible: the whole team shares a reward, but nobody can see how much effort each person put in. So someone free rides: the one who idles gets the same reward as everyone else. Sharing solves only half the problem; each person’s contribution must also be visible, and defection must have a cost. If nobody handles the reports, the cost of defecting is still 0.

Want to verify for yourself that “changing the rules changes behavior”? Go back to the pond and turn the rule knob to 2. Or go back to the lab and lower all the 5s for “lone defector” to 3.

How it is computed: every strategy looks only at the last round and chooses to cooperate or defect by probability. When two strategies play each other, a Markov chain (a mathematical tool that works out “the probabilities of the next step” from “the current state”) gives the expected score per round exactly, so the result has no random jitter. A slip means each action has a certain probability of being executed as its opposite, and the other side sees the action as executed.

Common misconceptions

  • “The prisoner’s dilemma shows human nature is evil.” No. The dilemma comes from the payoff structure, not from people’s character. Put equally kind people in different payoff structures and their behavior will be completely different.
  • “Nash equilibrium is the best result.” No. It is only a stable result in which “nobody wants to change alone,” and the prisoner’s dilemma’s equilibrium is bad for both.
  • “Tit for tat is always the strongest strategy.” No. Its strength is relative to that particular set of entrants. One-on-one, it never beats its opponent. With more slips it also loses out, which you can see with your own eyes in the strategy map.

Reflection and debate

  • The limits of the model. In reality payoffs are not just points: fairness, face and the urge to retaliate all change a person’s “payoff.” Large numbers of experiments find that people often choose to cooperate in social dilemmas, and when communication is allowed, cooperation rises markedly (see Balliet’s 2010 pooled analysis of many experiments, J. Conflict Resolution 54(1): 39–57). This does not match the prediction that “rational people must defect,” which suggests the “rationality” in the model is defined too narrowly.
  • Noise (slips) can wreck tit for tat. Once slips appear, two tit-for-tat players fall into a cycle of mutual retaliation, as the strategy map has already shown you firsthand. Nowak and Sigmund later proposed more forgiving strategies and win-stay, lose-shift, which did better in their simulations. If it is changed to “the two sides move in turn,” the result is different again. So the tournament conclusions are not the final answer.
  • Results depend on the starting population and the entrant list. Evolution results change with the starting proportions, and the tournament winner depends on the entrant list. Don’t take Axelrod’s conclusion as a theorem that holds everywhere (Rapoport, Seale and Colman, 2015).
  • Hardin and Ostrom. “The tragedy of the commons needs outside coercion” is Hardin’s view. Ostrom’s research shows that communities managing themselves can succeed too. Both views have facts behind them, each applying under different conditions.
  • Should mechanisms be used to steer players? The common view among mechanism designers: designers should use rules to steer players toward cooperation, not sermons. The objection: treating players as variables that can be adjusted turns the game into manipulation of behavior.

Key points

  • A game is made of participants, strategies and payoffs. A Nash equilibrium is a combination of choices in which “nobody can gain by changing their own choice alone”; a game with a finite number of options has at least one.
  • Four typical games: the prisoner’s dilemma (defecting dominates), stag hunt (trust needed), chicken (who yields first), zero-sum (pure confrontation).
  • Why it was proposed: zero-sum theory couldn’t handle the real-world conflicts where “both sides may get better together, or worse together,” hence the Nash equilibrium. RAND’s experiment and the prisoner story put it to the test. Axelrod used tournaments to answer “what strategy is good when people meet again and again.” Hardin and Ostrom applied it to common resources.
  • Everyone making the best choice for themselves and the result being poor has its root in the payoff structure: individual benefit and collective benefit have been pulled apart (the prisoner’s dilemma, the tragedy of the commons).
  • Meeting again changes the game: strategies that are nice, retaliating, forgiving and clear do well; cooperation needs a long enough shadow of the future (with the standard scores, the chance of meeting again must reach at least two thirds); when slips are frequent, strategies must be more forgiving.
  • The designer is a mechanism designer: promote cooperation by changing the payoff structure (shared rewards, repeated meetings, reputation, channels for punishment, quotas), or keep the temptation in to create tension.
The players haven’t turned bad; the payoff structure makes defection pay. To get cooperation, change the rules and let everyone “meet again.”
