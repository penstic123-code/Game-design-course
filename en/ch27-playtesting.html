Chapter 27

Player Research and Playtesting

Three months on a tutorial level, and a friend says “it’s pretty fun.” But in the recording, he was stuck for forty seconds.

Learning goals

  • Tell apart what playtests, usability tests, interviews, surveys and telemetry can each answer, and pick the right method for a specific question.
  • Explain why what people say often differs from what they do, why the players you see are only the ones who survived, and how think-aloud, neutral questions and observation get around these traps.
  • Use 1−(1−L)n to estimate “how many testers are enough,” state the premises of this rule of thumb, and say why small samples deceive.
About 45 minutes
1

What it isPlaytesting: watch people play, don’t ask whether they like it

Say you showed a friend the little game you made. He played a while and said with a smile: “It’s pretty fun!” You’re delighted. The next day you watch his screen recording: in the first level he paced back and forth in front of a door that wouldn’t open for forty seconds, and only got the key by accident. What he said was sincere, and also polite; only the recording shows where the game really gets stuck.

The professional approach is not to ask but to watch. A playtest lets real players play the prototype while the designer shuts up and observes, replacing “I think” with “I saw.” Once a prototype is built, it is what tells you where to change.

Playtesting has a close relative, usability testing. The two share a set of techniques and differ only in the question they ask.

Concept card

Usability Testing

Usability testing

John D. Gould, Clayton Lewis · Designing for Usability (1985) proposed “actually measure how people use it”

Give typical users a task and see whether they can finish it, how long it takes and where they go wrong; judge whether the product is easy to use from the behavior observed.

ExampleAsk someone who has never used it to “find the settings and turn the volume down,” stand by without a word, and note what they click first and how long they pause.

A usability test asks “is it smooth to use”: give the user a task (“buy a ticket”) and see whether they can finish it, how long it takes, and where they go wrong. A playtest asks more broadly: besides “is it smooth,” it asks “is it fun”: does the challenge suit them, does the pace drag, do emotions rise and fall.

Usability testPlaytest
Core questionCan they finish? Where do they get stuck?Do they want to go on? Where is it boring, frustrating, surprising?
What an “obstacle” meansMostly a defect to removeSome obstacles are the design (difficulty, puzzles); tell “good hard” from “bad stuck”

The difference between “good hard” and “bad stuck” is another way of saying “challenge matches skill” in flow: if the player knows what to do, why they failed and how to do better next time, it is good hard; if they don’t know the goal or why they died, it is bad stuck.

In the game: a playtest of a shooter: researchers watched players through one-way glass, recorded the routes they walked and where they died, popped up a question every few minutes in the game asking “how engaged and how frustrated are you now,” and had some players think aloud. According to a 2007 report in Wired, Microsoft’s game studios did this for Halo 3 (secondhand report; check the original for details).
Daily life: assembling for the first time: give a stranger an instruction sheet and a pile of parts, and stand by saying nothing. Seeing which board he puts on backwards is ten times more effective than asking “were the instructions clear?”
Daily life: revising a cover letter: have a friend read it and say what they think. When he reaches the third paragraph and stops to ask “what does ‘multi-dimensional empowerment’ mean?” you know at once which sentence to change.
Counterexample: sending the game to a roommate and asking “is it fun?” He is a friend, who doesn’t want to hurt you; he has heard the designer explain how to play; and he may well not be a target player at all. This has only the form of a playtest, without its substance.
2

What it isThe spectrum of methods: what they say vs what they do, one vs many

Since just listening to players is not enough, we need to know what other approaches are available. Research methods can roughly be placed on two axes: horizontally from “what players say (self-report)” to “what players do (behavior),” vertically from “why (few, in depth)” to “how many (large-scale statistics).”

None is “best”; they fill in for one another: telemetry says “40% drop out at level 3” (how many), observation says “because they can’t see that door” (why), and an interview adds “I thought it was background” (how he understood it).

👀 Observation

Watch what players do: pauses, repeats, hesitation, skipping. The closest thing to “the truth,” but you must restrain yourself from interrupting.

🗣️ Interview

Chat afterward: how he understood it and what he expected. Good for digging into motives and mental models, but he will give a “rationalized” account.

📋 Survey

Ask many people at once, easy to compare and quantify. But how the questions are asked and how the options are given decide what you get.

📈 Telemetry

The game records events itself: where players died, how long they were stuck, which level they quit. Many people, no disturbance, but no “why.”

Think-aloud: turning the words in the head into sound

Observation has a problem: you can see the player stop, but not what they are thinking. Think-aloud exists for this problem. The player is asked to say what is in their head as they play: “I see a door… it won’t open… do I need a key?” The researcher does not judge, explain or help, and only when the player is silent too long asks, “What are you thinking now?”

Concept card

Think-Aloud Protocol

Think-aloud protocol

K. Anders Ericsson, Herbert A. Simon · 1980, Verbal Reports as Data

Ask the subject to say, while doing something, what they are attending to and thinking at that moment, without explaining why. The words obtained can be treated as data.

ExampleA player stands before a locked door and says: “The door won’t open… there’s a wooden box beside it, I wonder if it can be pushed.” The designer knows on the spot that the player did not see the key hint.

Task design and neutral questions

  • Give tasks as “goals,” not “routes.” Say “please find a way through this level,” not “please go left, take the key and open the door.” The latter does the very thing you are testing for him.
  • Keep questions neutral. Ask “what did you think?” rather than “wasn’t it great?” Ask about real past behavior, not imagined future behavior (“what did you last pay for?” rather than “would you buy it?”).
  • Record in layers. Note the facts first (14:32, third time at the door), then the interpretation (maybe didn’t see the key), and don’t mix the two on one sticky note.

Testers who know they are being observed will behave differently from usual. So say at the start “we’re testing the game, not you”; this script can be used as is.

3

When to test, whom to test, how many to test

Research sits inside the iteration loop as a series of small experiments, not as a big exam taken at the end. The diagram below marks its place: it swaps “I guess” for “I saw,” so the next round of changes has evidence behind it.

When to test

The earlier the better, and the rougher the better. A level sketch on paper, or a graybox level of only blocks, can be playtested. The earlier you discover “nobody understands the goal,” the cheaper the fix. Later, test balance, feel and long-term experience.

Whom to test: target players, not friends

The ideal tester is a target player: someone who likes this kind of game, knows nothing about your game, and doesn’t know the designer. When recruiting, first write a “screening criteria” (what genres they have played, how many hours a week). Friends and colleagues can be used too, but know that they will be more polite and also more readily guess the designer’s intent. If the game has clearly different player groups (newcomers vs veterans), test them separately.

How many to test: “5 is enough”?

The most widely circulated rule of thumb in the industry is “test 5 people and that is enough,” and it is also the most misused. The arithmetic behind it is not complicated. Say each tester finds a given problem with probability L, and people are independent of one another. In their 1993 paper Nielsen and Landauer fitted data from several projects and got an average L of about 31%.

Then the probability that all 5 people miss the problem is 0.69 multiplied by itself 5 times, about 16%, so the probability that at least one finds it is 84% (Nielsen’s article writes about 85%). Raise the number of people to 15, and the probability that everyone misses it becomes negligible; the chance of finding it is about 99.6%.

Advanced: the general formula

The probability that at least one of n people finds a given problem is 1 − (1 − L)n. Here (1−L)n is the probability that all n people miss it, and its complement is “at least one finds it.”

L is an average that varies with product, task and population, and it has drawn quite a few challenges. Drag the sliders and run the simulation to see for yourself how sensitive it is.

Invention storyWhy do we have to “watch people use it”?

The designer’s blind spot: the maker sees the problems least

Suppose you are a software designer in the early 1980s who wrote a very “clever” interface. You use it fast, and colleagues say it’s good. After release, users ask for help in droves. What do you do? There are three common old approaches : read more design guidelines; have colleagues try it; get it perfect in one go and ship, then see. Their shared flaw is that the judges all know the system as well as the designer, and can’t see the world of “someone seeing it for the first time.”

To see that world, you have to let “first-timers” use it and observe them while they do. But how do you know what is going on in their head? Ask them? Whether verbal reports can be trusted was something psychologists argued over.

First try for yourself: how many problems do you think 5 people can find?

Before looking at history, make a prediction. Suppose in the typical case each tester can find a given problem with probability 31%. What percent of all problems can 5 people find together? Drag the slider, write down your intuition, then reveal.

Try it: write down your guess and reveal it, and see how many percentage points it differs from the theoretical value. Then think: why is intuition often too low?

From “verbal report” to “usability test”: a timeline

1977 Nisbett and Wilson publish “Telling More Than We Can Know” in Psychological Review: when people explain the causes of their own behavior, they mostly apply a commonsense theory of causes and do not really look inward. The problem: can verbal reports still be trusted?
1980 Ericsson and Simon (same journal) publish “Verbal Reports as Data”: when subjects are asked to report what they are attending to right now, the verbal report can serve as data; when asked to explain causes or guess at processes, it is unreliable. Saying aloud what you are attending to generally doesn’t change the thinking itself. The key insight: don’t ask “why”; have them say “what are you thinking.”
1985 Gould and Lewis publish “Designing for Usability” in Communications of the ACM, proposing three principles: focus on users from the start and keep doing so; measure how people actually use it; design iteratively (prototype, test, revise, test again). This line later became known as user-centered design, and critics (such as Cockton 2008) have challenged its account of its own effectiveness.
1992–93 Virzi (1992, Human Factors) reports that about 4–5 subjects find about 80% of usability problems, with diminishing returns afterward, and the most severe problems are usually found early. Nielsen and Landauer (1993, INTERCHI) wrote the process as a mathematical model: multiply together the chance that each of n people misses it, then take the complement. From 11 studies they estimated an average L of about 31%.
2000 Nielsen publishes the column “Why You Only Need to Test with 5 Users,” stressing that rather than one big test with 15 people, do three small rounds of 5 with design changes in between. He also notes that the formula holds only for the same kind of users with similar ways of using it. Different user groups need 3–4 of each.
2001–03 The rebuttals come: Spool and Schroeder (2001) observed 49 users doing the same task on 4 real websites and argued that finding 85% of problems takes far more than 5 people (as later retold: the first 5 found only about 35%). Faulkner (2003) tested 60 people on a timesheet application and then drew random groups of 5 from them: the average was close to 85%, but the share of problems found by different groups of 5 ranged from 55% to 99%; for groups of 10 the lowest was 80%, for 20 the lowest 95%.
2007 In the games industry: according to reports by Wired and NPR, the user research team at Microsoft’s game studios used one-way-glass observation, route and death-location statistics, random pop-up questionnaires and think-aloud during Halo 3 development. NPR says that after many players didn’t know what to do, the team made the objective prompt flash on screen. (Secondhand reports; check the originals for details.)
Designer can’t see blind spots→Need to see how newcomers use it→Asking “why” isn’t reliable→Switch to observation + think-aloud→How many to test?→Small samples + several rounds

Back to the guess: many people guess 50% or even lower, because intuition says “only 31% of people find it, so five together surely can’t reach 85%.” But 31% is per person. For a problem to be missed by everyone, the probability shrinks as it is multiplied across people: all 5 missing it is only 0.69 to the fifth power, about 16%. People are used to adding probabilities and not used to multiplying them, so they guess low.

The rule is a lovely model, but its conditions are strict and it is disputed. The reason for several rounds is a different one: each round fixes the most obvious problems, and only then do the subtler ones come into view.

5

Why it holdsWhat people say is often not what people do

Principle one: people know less about their own mental processes than they think

After reviewing a large number of experiments, Nisbett and Wilson (1977) proposed that when people explain the causes of their own behavior, they mostly rely on a commonsense theory of “what usually causes it,” not on direct observation of their actual process. So when you press for causes or ask what they like, the answer is often a plausible-sounding story. This paper was very influential and also controversial; later assessments are mixed. The safer reading is: be careful asking for causes.

Ericsson and Simon (1980) went on to draw a distinction that laid the foundation for think-aloud: “what do you see and think now” reflects what is attended to at the moment and is usually reliable; “why” requires after-the-fact reasoning and is less reliable.

Sources:
Nisbett and Wilson · 1977 · reviewed experiments and showed that people often cannot say what really caused their behavior (Telling more than we can know, Psychological Review 84, 231–259).
Ericsson and Simon · 1980 · distinguished “reporting what is being attended to” from “explaining causes,” and treated the former as usable data (Verbal reports as data, Psychological Review 87; revised as a book, Protocol Analysis, MIT Press, 1993).

Principle two: bias, the data is already skewed before it is collected

Not only do players say things crooked: observers, designers and the way people are chosen also skew the data. Four are common:

  • Confirmation bias (observer): believing “the goal is clear enough,” you more easily explain a player’s wandering as “he’s enjoying the scenery.”
  • Social desirability bias (player): facing the people who made the game, most don’t want to say “boring,” and friends least of all.
  • Curse of knowledge (designer): knowing the key is behind the wooden box, you think “it’s obvious.”
  • Sampling bias (choosing people): testing only friends, only veterans, or only active users misses those who have already been driven away, and this trap is bigger.

Sources:
Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber · 1989 · showed experimentally that people who know something find it hard to ignore what they know, and misjudge others (the curse of knowledge; The Curse of Knowledge in Economic Settings, Journal of Political Economy 97(5): 1232–1254).
Confirmation bias and social desirability bias are commonly accepted concepts in psychology and user research, not attributed to a single source.

Concept card

Curse of Knowledge

Curse of knowledge

Colin Camerer, George Loewenstein, Martin Weber · 1989, The Curse of Knowledge in Economic Settings

Once you know something, it is hard to imagine what it is like not to know it, and so hard to judge how someone else will think or act.

ExampleA designer who has built a level for three months knows with eyes closed that the key is behind the wooden box, and cannot understand why players don’t find it.

Confirmation bias: the player going in circles: believing “the level has a real atmosphere,” you see his being lost as “enjoying the scenery,” until the recording shows him turning back three times to look for the exit.
Social desirability: saying “it’s pretty good” aloud: the player has sweat on the forehead and a tight grip, yet facing the people who made the game still says “it’s pretty good.” Behavior is more honest than answers.
Curse of knowledge: the key is “obviously obvious”: the designer knows the key is behind the wooden box, and the box even glows, as obvious as can be. In the player’s eyes there is only an ordinary box.
Almost, but not quite: facts and interpretations on one sticky note: the observation was recorded carefully, but “walked to the door three times” and “maybe didn’t see the key (my guess)” are mixed together, so later you can’t tell which sentence was seen and which was made up.
6

Why it holdsWhat you see is only the part that survived

There is another, subtler kind of skew: the problem lies in who answers, and how players answer matters less.

Principle three: survivorship bias

During the Second World War the US military wanted to add armor to bombers, but armor is heavy and can only go on the vital spots. The military tallied the planes that returned to base and found the most bullet holes on the wings and fuselage, and the fewest around the engines. The intuitive conclusion: add armor where the bullet holes are many. The statistician Abraham Wald saw it the opposite way: the statistics contain only the planes that flew back. Planes hit in the engine never returned, which is why they show “no bullet holes” in the statistics. The places to armor were those that looked untouched.

This is survivorship bias: a filtered sample leads people to misjudge the whole. In game research it can be seen everywhere: you interview those still playing, while those scared off are long gone; you see the clear-run recordings, while players who didn’t finish have none.

Sources:
Abraham Wald · 1943 · studied at the Statistical Research Group of Columbia University how to estimate the survival probability of planes that were shot down, which is the origin of the bullet-hole story.
Marc Mangel and Francisco Samaniego · 1984 · introduced and organized Wald’s work (“Abraham Wald’s work on aircraft survivability,” Journal of the American Statistical Association 79(386): 259–267).
The bullet-hole story is widely told and often simplified; here it is used as a metaphor, so check the original for details. The American Mathematical Society’s column discusses the origins of the legend.

In the game: interviewing only active players: ask people still playing “what’s the most fun,” and everyone says “the combat is great.” But those who left on day three are not on the list; they were driven off by the first ten minutes.

But out of a hundred emails only three come back, and the repliers are a small group who like to reply, so you still don’t hear the voice of most departed players. The remedy is to recruit actively and pay them.
7

Why it holdsWith few people, luck passes for a pattern

Flip a coin five times and get four heads: would you suspect the coin? Probably. But for a perfectly fair coin, the chance of four or more heads in five flips is 6/32, close to one in five. Five flips can’t support any conclusion at all.

But people stubbornly believe that “a small sample should look like the whole.” In 1971 Tversky and Kahneman half-jokingly called this belief “the law of small numbers,” as if small samples obeyed the law of large numbers too.

Principle four: small samples fluctuate a lot

Tversky and Kahneman (1971) surveyed professional psychology researchers with a questionnaire and found that they too often overestimated the representativeness of small samples and made research decisions accordingly: with too small a sample, conclusions are held up by luck. This is an influential view.

Back to playtests: Faulkner drew many groups of 5 for the same application, the average found about 85%, yet some groups found only 55%. The average is right, but you hold only one group of 5, and don’t know whether you drew good luck or bad. That is why you need several rounds, retesting after each round of fixes.

In A/B tests the problem is sharper. By a rough estimate from Lehr’s rule of thumb (for 80% power and 5% significance), to reliably tell a 5% conversion rate from 6%, you need about 8000 people per group. With only 50 people per group, “version B has 2 more people” is just noise. Statistical significance only says “not very likely pure luck,” not “the difference is large and worth acting on.”

Advanced: how this number is estimated

Lehr’s rule of thumb: the number per group is about 16·p(1−p)/δ². Here p is the average of the two conversion rates, 5.5%, and δ is their difference, 0.01. Plugging in gives 16×0.055×0.945÷0.0001≈8300, of the order of 8000. This is a rough estimate, not an exact calculation.

Sources:
Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman · 1971 · surveyed researchers and found that people believe a small sample should resemble the population and overestimate its representativeness (“Belief in the law of small numbers,” Psychological Bulletin 76(2): 105–110).
Lehr’s rule of thumb can be found in sample-size estimation in statistics textbooks.

In the game: an A/B test, 50 per group: version B got 2 more clicks than A, and the team held a celebration. Random fluctuation alone can produce such a gap.
Daily life: three wins in a row and “my luck’s in”: three is a sample, not a pattern. The lucky are usually also the result of random fluctuation.
Playtest: 4 of 5 people didn’t see that door: this is not a law, but it is enough to go and check, because the cost is low. And “all 5 had no problem” should not reassure you.
Data is not insight: staring at “time spent”: telemetry shows “40% drop out at level 3” but no reason. Once time spent is taken as a target, you may make more dragging levels, which is just what Goodhart’s law describes. The most reliable combination: telemetry finds “where,” observation and interviews explain “why,” and after a change use data to confirm.

Check it yourself: a 10-minute mini-experiment

Experiment: “predicted vs actual.” Find someone who has not seen the prototype and first ask: “Do you think you can figure out how to begin within 1 minute? Where would you click?” Write it down. Then let him really play, and only watch without a word. Most people’s predictions are rosier than reality. Write down the gap: this is one replication of the gap between “say” and “do.”

How to use itThe script for a 45-minute playtest

① Preparation (the day before)

  • Write down 2–3 specific questions (“Can a newcomer find the key within 2 minutes?”).
  • Recruit 3–5 target players; have screen recording ready.
  • Play through it yourself first so you don’t waste time on bugs.

② Opening script (2 minutes)

  • “We’re testing the game, not you.”
  • “Getting stuck, disliking something, not understanding: all of it is useful information.”
  • “Please say what you’re thinking as you play. I won’t answer questions, but I’ll write them down.”

③ Observation (25 minutes)

  • The designer shuts up. If asked “how do I play here?” answer “what do you think?”
  • Note facts: time, position, action.
  • When silent too long: “What are you thinking now?”

④ Wrap-up interview (8 minutes)

  • Open question: “What left the deepest impression just now?”
  • Replay key moments: “You paused here for a while; what were you thinking?”
  • Only at the end ask “Is there anything else you’d like to say?”

Synthesis: affinity diagram + severity ranking

After a playtest there is a big pile of scattered observations. The affinity diagram is a way to sort them: write each observation on a sticky note (for example “3/5 people lingered at the door for more than 10 seconds”), put similar ones together, and give each pile a name (“goal unclear”). Then give each pile two scores: how many people hit it (frequency) × how serious the consequence (severity: merely annoying / stuck / gave up). Fix the ones with high frequency and high severity first. Some findings hit only one person and can still be fatal, such as a lost save, so don’t look only at head counts.

Concept card

Affinity Diagram

Affinity diagram (KJ method)

Jiro Kawakita · developed the KJ method in the 1960s; published Hassōhō in 1967

Write scattered observations on cards one by one, group them by similarity, and name each group so that the common themes show.

ExampleFive players’ recordings contain 7 notes like “paced in front of the door” and “read the hint again and again”; they go into one pile named “goal unclear.”

Players’ suggestions are symptoms, not prescriptions: if 3 people say “make it easier,” making every level easier is wrong. The recording shows they died at the same spike spot, and what is really needed is “a warning before the spikes.”
Counterexample: doing everything they say: change whatever players say, and after one round the game has become the “lowest common denominator,” and the original design with personality is gone. The designer’s job is to find the cause, not to carry out suggestions.

Common misconceptions

  • “Testing 5 people is enough.” This is a simplification of a conditional rule of thumb: it assumes similar player groups, equivalent problems, and retesting after fixes. With different groups or a complex product, 5 is far from enough (Faulkner’s worst group of 5 found only 55%).
  • “Players say they want it, so I’ll build it.” Players are good at saying “what bothers me” and bad at designing solutions. Treat suggestions as symptoms.
  • “Friends tried it, no problem.” They are more polite and understand the designer better, so every problem gets underestimated.
  • “The data shows X, so we’ll do X.” Data says only “where,” not “why.” Optimizing a single metric easily falls into the Goodhart trap.
  • “Ask why, let them explain it themselves.” “Why” invites after-the-fact rationalization. Ask “what were you thinking, where were you looking” instead.
  • “Everyone I interviewed said it’s good.” Think of the ones who left: they are not on the interview list.

Reflections and controversies

  • The “5 people” dispute. Nielsen’s view is that “several small tests beat one big test.” Critics (Spool and Schroeder, Faulkner and others) point out that L is not fixed and differs greatly across studies, and that results of different groups of 5 can be worlds apart. The dispute has no conclusion yet.
  • Where L comes from. The 31% is the average over the projects Nielsen studied back then. Lewis (1994) and others point out that small-sample estimates of L tend to be high (as later retold). So L can’t be used as is; look at your own project.
  • Does think-aloud change how people play? Ericsson and Simon’s theory holds that as long as the report is of what is being attended to, thinking generally isn’t changed; but in games, talking while playing may make people more focused or more distracted, so some researchers also run a “silent group” as a control.
  • Does research kill “authorship”? One view holds that over-reliance on testing grinds a game down to a “lowest common denominator”; another holds that research only provides evidence, and the decision stays with the designer. This is a disagreement about design philosophy, with no standard answer.
  • Ethics. When telemetry collects player behavior data, inform players and obtain consent, and mind privacy.

Key points

  • A playtest has target players really play while the designer shuts up and observes; it shares techniques with usability tests, but a playtest also asks “is it fun.”
  • The methods complement one another: telemetry knows “where and how many,” observation + think-aloud knows “where it gets stuck,” interviews know “how he understood it,” and surveys suit comparison at scale.
  • What people say ≠ what they do: self-report is affected by social desirability, observers by confirmation bias, designers by the curse of knowledge, and the sample may be left with only survivors; get around them with behavior, neutral questions, independent coding and active recruiting.
  • Think-aloud asks “what are you thinking,” not “why did you”; this comes from Ericsson and Simon’s research on verbal reports.
  • The probability model for finding problems: with each person finding a problem at L≈31%, 5 people find about 84%. But L varies by project, and results of groups of 5 fluctuate widely (small samples let luck pass for a pattern). The core is several small tests + retest after fixing.
Don’t ask players whether it’s fun; watch where they get stuck; mind who is missing from the sample; a few people tested over several rounds improve a game better than one big test.
