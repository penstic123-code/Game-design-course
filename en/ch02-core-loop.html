Chapter 2

The Core Loop

The engine behind “one more round,” and why it keeps turning.

Learning goals

about 50 min
  • Write a game’s core loop as a “verb chain,” and use the “break-the-loop experiment” to say what happens when each link is missing.
  • Tell the four reinforcement schedules apart, and say why Skinner set out to study “how rewards are handed out.”
  • Use “reward prediction error” (Schultz’s monkey experiment) and the goal gradient to explain why a fixed reward soon stops feeling like anything, and why a progress bar pulls.
1

What it isThe core loop: the circle that makes you play one more

At one in the morning you tell yourself: last game, then bed. The game ends, and what you click is “play again.”

This is not wholly a matter of willpower. The end of the last game quietly handed over a reason for the next: you almost won, the newly unlocked character hasn’t been tried, and the tension of a health bar down to its last notch hasn’t worn off.

Draw the structure of “the end joins the beginning” and you get a circle. The chain of things a player repeats most often in a game is called the core loop. Pac-Man’s loop is: eat dots → dodge ghosts → eat a power pellet → eat the ghosts → clear the board, then the next level.

Concept card

Core loop

Core loop

A common term in the game design industry, with no single originator

The chain of things a player does again and again in a game, ending back at the start for another round.

ExamplePac-Man: eat dots, dodge ghosts, eat a power pellet, eat the ghosts, clear the board, then the next level.

Designers write a loop down as a string of verbs, called a verb chain. Chapter 1 showed that a game needs feedback, and the core loop joins that feedback into a circle.

The player does something, the game responds, and the response brings a reward. The reward makes the player a little stronger, and being stronger brings new goals, so the loop returns to the start.

The arrows form a circle: a reward makes the next action stronger, or opens new choices, so the player wants another go.

A game has more than one circle. Every second you jump, hit and dodge; every few minutes you finish a match; every few days you save enough for a new piece of gear. Three circles nested one inside another, turning faster the further in:

This gives an iron rule: if the innermost micro loop isn’t fun, no number of outer layers will turn. Nobody puts up with a dull jumping feel for the sake of a “weekly season reward.”

Plants vs. Zombies: collect sun → plant plants → hold off zombies → get new plants → grow stronger formations. With every level won, the hand you hold gets richer.
Minecraft (survival mode): gather → craft → build → explore farther → gather new materials. The urge to travel comes from “needing new materials,” so the loop is closed by need.
Daily life: scrolling short videos: swipe down → watch one → (now and then) hit something especially to your taste → swipe down again. Note that “now and then”: the reward doesn’t come every time.
Vocabulary app check-ins: learn 20 words → mini quiz → streak +1 → want to protect the streak → learn again tomorrow. The app turns “growth” into an ever-lengthening number.
Counterexample: a cutscene that doesn’t return to the start: a piece of story you watch once and that’s that, with no reason to “do it again.” The arrow runs to the end, and no line loops back to the start, so it isn’t a loop.
Counterexample: a loop whose action is dull: click → get 1 coin → buy click power → click again. The circle is closed, but “click” has no fun in it, and the reward is just bribing the player. How fragile such a loop is, the break-the-loop experiment lets you see for yourself.
2

What it isHow rewards are handed out: four reinforcement schedules

A slot machine is harder to walk away from than a vending machine. A vending machine delivers every time a coin goes in, while a slot machine is “maybe the next pull wins.” The two give about the same thing, and the difference lies in the way the reward comes.

Psychologists have studied the rules for “under what conditions a reward is given,” called a schedule of reinforcement. Two questions sort them into four kinds: does the reward depend on “how many times you did it” or “how much time has passed”? And is that number fixed, or does it change each time?

Concept card

The rules for “when a reward is given.” By count or by time, fixed or variable, they fall into four kinds.

ExampleGacha pulls are variable ratio: a rare card comes up once in a certain number of pulls on average, and before each pull there is no telling whether this one will hit.

Tap one to look:
Where the red star lands is the character of this rule. The two bottom rows run horizontally in time, so whether clicks are sparse or dense also shows.
  • Fixed ratio (FR): a reward after every N responses. Every 5 monsters killed drops a chest, and 10 shards exchange for a character.
  • Variable ratio (VR): a reward once in N responses on average, but never knowing which one. Gacha pulls, monster drops, pull-to-refresh in social apps.
  • Fixed interval (FI): after each fixed stretch of time, the first response earns a reward. Energy refills every 6 hours; a daily login bonus.
  • Variable interval (VI): the time gaps are random. A rare boss that respawns at unpredictable times; a reply to a message that could arrive at any moment.
Counterexample: continuous reinforcement (a reward every time): a vending machine, “one click always gets 1 coin.” A reward every time is the likeliest to leave people feeling nothing, and the first to be noticed when it stops one day.
Payday is FI: on a fixed date every month. It doesn’t come early, and when the day arrives it always comes.
Daily life: fishing is close to VI: nobody knows when a fish will bite, and you can only keep watch, waiting steadily.
Games: monsters dropping gear is close to VR: every monster has a chance to drop something good, but nobody knows which one will.
3

How it was inventedThe Skinner box: how did anyone discover “how rewards are handed out” mattered?

The problem: how do you measure how rewards shape behavior?

In 1898 the American psychologist Edward Thorndike put hungry cats into “puzzle boxes,” with fish outside and a latch inside. The cat scratched and clawed at random, happened to knock the latch loose, and escaped a little faster the next time. From this he proposed that behavior with good consequences gets kept (later called the law of effect).

But the maze cage had a flaw: the experimenter noted down one time only after each cat escaped, and had to stay and watch, so the data were sparse and coarse. To study “how rewards change behavior bit by bit,” such a cage was not enough.

B. F. Skinner, as a graduate student at Harvard, took a different approach: a lever in a box, and each time a rat pressed it the machine dropped a pellet of food and automatically drew a mark on a paper tape. Nobody had to keep watch, no press was missed, and for the first time behavior could be recorded continuously and quantitatively. Later he mainly used pigeons, replacing the lever with a key to peck.

Then came an unexpected little episode. According to Skinner’s 1956 recollection, the food pellets then had to be made by hand, tiring and in short supply. He thought: instead of giving one on every press, how about one every minute? The rat didn’t give up; it kept pressing steadily. Even without a reward every time, the behavior held up, and the rhythm of pressing was very different from when it was rewarded every time.

From this the two men wanted to find out whether “when to give” decides behavior more than “how much to give.” Around 1949–1955 they systematically tried various reward rules on pigeons, and gathered the results in the 1957 book Schedules of Reinforcement.

1898 Thorndike puts cats into puzzle boxes and records how long they take to escape: behavior is shaped by its consequences. He later sums this up as the law of effect.
1930s Skinner builds the “operant chamber”: a lever, automatic food delivery, an automatic paper tape. For the first time behavior can be measured continuously and quantitatively.
1950s Skinner and Ferster compare reward rules on a large scale with pigeons, and publish Schedules of Reinforcement in 1957.
Afterward “Intermittent rewards are harder to extinguish than a reward every time” became a classic conclusion of the psychology of learning. Game designers borrow it to design drops, gacha and timed rewards.

Why it worksWhy are variable rewards so “sticky”?

Back to those pigeons. After the rewards were stopped altogether, the pigeons that had always had “a reward on every 5th peck” soon stopped pecking, while those who never knew which peck would pay were still pecking. Cut off from food in the same way, they responded very differently.

Uncertainty makes it hard to tell “not yet” from “never again”

In classic animal experiments, variable schedules produce behavior that is steadier and that takes longer to die out once the reward stops. This is slower extinction, which textbooks call the “partial reinforcement extinction effect.”

Variable ratio also produces a very high, steady response rate. Fixed interval often shows the scalloped curve of “lazy right after being paid, frantic as the time nears.”

The usual explanation: if it always paid on every 5th try before, “6 misses in a row” is an obvious signal that the reward has been withdrawn. If it used to sometimes take many tries before one paid, “another miss” says nothing, and the animal keeps trying. This is the mechanism seen in the simulation above: FR gives up quickly once the reward stops, and VR holds out longer.

The link to the loop: for the “reward” link in a core loop, which way you hand it out changes the flavor of the whole loop. “One click, one coin” is continuous reinforcement. Change it to “each click has a chance to drop a golden mushroom” and it becomes variable ratio.

Sources:
Ferster and Skinner, 1957, who systematically compared reward rules on pigeons and found that variable schedules are harder to extinguish than fixed ones (Schedules of Reinforcement).
Skinner, 1956, his own recollection of how he found intermittent rewards (American Psychologist 11: 221–233, “A case history in scientific method”). The account of the discovery above comes from this recollection.
These regularities come mainly from pigeons and rats, and in people they are also shaped by language, rules and expectations.
Gacha / loot boxes: whether each pull gives a rare item can’t be predicted (VR). What players remember is “I nearly got it last time,” and nobody remembers “I’ve already pulled dozens of times.”
Pull-to-refresh in social apps: you don’t know whether this refresh brings what you wanted to see, so you refresh once more.
Counterexample: the fixed daily login gift: fixed interval plus continuous reinforcement, exactly the same every day, quickly absorbed by expectation, with no reason to stay once you have claimed it. So games often mix a little random surprise into fixed rewards.

How it was inventedWhat is dopamine “computing”? From surprise to prediction error

The problem: if rewards make us happy, why does the same reward every day stop feeling like anything?

In the late 1960s, learning psychologists found something odd. Pair a light with a shock and an animal normally learns “light on = a shock is coming.” But if the shock has already been fully announced by another signal, a new signal added on top is not learned at all. This is called blocking (Leon Kamin, 1969).

So being present together is not enough for an animal to remember, and surprise is what makes it learn. In 1972 Robert Rescorla and Allan Wagner wrote this idea as a formula: new expectation = old expectation + a small share of (actual − expected).

Take a number: the expectation is 100 and the actual is 300, a gap of 200, so the next expectation is nudged up by a small step. The bigger the gap, the more is learned; with no gap, there is nothing to learn.

That formula was then only a mathematical conjecture, and nobody had found its shadow in the brain. Not until the 1990s did Wolfram Schultz and colleagues record a matching signal from the dopamine neurons of monkeys.

Before the monkey has learned “light → juice,” an unexpected juice makes these neurons fire fiercely. After it has learned, the response moves to the moment the light comes on, and the juice itself hardly causes a response. If the light comes on and the juice doesn’t come, at the moment the juice should have appeared, the firing actually falls below normal. In 1997 Schultz, Dayan and Montague published a review in Science presenting it as “dopamine signals the reward prediction error.”

Why it worksOnly surprise registers: reward prediction error

The same hundred gold: the first time you get it you are delighted; get it on time every day and a week later you feel almost nothing; one day it suddenly drops 300 and you are “high” again.

The money didn’t change; the expectation in your head did. What the brain cares about is “how much better than expected.”

With the same “expect 100,” what is actually received decides whether anything is felt.

Surprise = actual − expected

Reward prediction error is (actual − expected). Better than expected is positive, “a surprise”; exactly as expected is zero, “nothing felt”; worse than expected is negative, “disappointment.” Expectation is slowly pulled toward the actual reward by experience, so a fixed reward gets “eaten” by expectation, and only change and surprise can produce positive errors again and again.

The mainstream view in neuroscience holds that the activity of midbrain dopamine neurons correlates strongly with “prediction error,” and is more like a learning signal (“better than I thought, remember it”). Researchers (such as Kent Berridge and Terry Robinson) also point out that “wanting” and “liking” can be separated: the dopamine system is more tied to “wanting,” which is not enjoyment itself. “Dopamine = pleasure” is a common oversimplification.

The link to the loop: a fixed reward makes the loop “go flat.” “Growth” too must keep supplying something new, or the reward gets eaten by expectation.

Sources:
Kamin, 1969, the blocking experiment: a stimulus that is already announced teaches nothing new.
Rescorla and Wagner, 1972, a learning model: learning is driven by prediction error.
Schultz, Dayan and Montague, 1997, a review of dopamine neuron recordings in monkeys: firing corresponds to the reward prediction error (Science 275: 1593–1599, “A Neural Substrate of Prediction and Reward”).
Berridge and Robinson, 2016, separating “wanting” from “liking” (American Psychologist 71: 670–679). The prediction-error hypothesis is the current mainstream explanation, but some researchers see it differently.

A fixed 100 coins every day: after a week expectation has caught up with reality, the surprise drops toward zero, and players “feel nothing.”
Daily life: salary and year-end bonus: payday was expected long ago, while an unexpected year-end bonus makes people far happier.
Loot drops: most of the time you get ordinary gear and occasionally a purple one, and that one surprise is the positive error.
Counterexample: a big reward is not a big surprise: give 1000 gold every time and expectation soon catches up to 1000. By the logic of prediction error, this is more likely to leave people feeling nothing than “usually 100, occasionally 500.” (This is deduction, not measurement.)

Why it worksMore: why does a progress bar pull? The goal gradient and endowed progress

With 8 squares already stamped on a loyalty card, you feel more like filling in the last 2. A task interrupted halfway keeps nagging at the mind.

Retailers everywhere hand out loyalty cards, but nobody knew how to design them for the best effect. In 2006 the marketing scholars Joseph Nunes and Xavier Drèze ran a field experiment at a car wash: 300 cards were handed out at random, half reading “8 empty squares, fill them for a free wash” and half reading “10 empty squares, but 2 already stamped.”

Both cards required 8 paid washes to redeem. In nine months of tracking, those given the card with “2 squares already stamped” redeemed at about 34%, and the blank card at about 19%. They called this the endowed progress effect: a task that looks “already begun” is more likely to be finished.

The two cards cost exactly the same money and trips; the only difference is that one “looks like it has already begun.”

The closer to the goal, the harder you push; unfinished things are hard to put down

The goal gradient means that the nearer the finish, the harder people (and animals) push. The psychologist Clark Hull first studied it in rats: the closer to the food, the faster they ran. In 2006 Kivetz and colleagues also found, in real coffee-shop loyalty-card data, that the nearer a customer got to a free cup, the more often they bought coffee.

The Zeigarnik effect (1927) is often put as “unfinished things are remembered better.” A 2025 meta-analysis (Ghibellini & Meier) found that the “remembered better” part is hard to replicate reliably. What is steadier is the tendency to go back and keep doing a task after an interruption, known as the Ovsiankina effect. So the more reliable statement is that unfinished tasks make people want to come back.

The link to the loop: progress bars, experience bars and chapter checklists make visible the “growth” link, bringing every small action a little closer to the goal and joining short loops into long ones. It is also double-edged, and when abused it becomes a “hook” people can’t put down.

Sources:
Hull, 1932, a study of running speed in mazes with rats: the closer to the end, the faster they run (the paper “The goal-gradient hypothesis and maze learning,” Psychological Review 39).
Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng, 2006, an analysis of coffee-shop loyalty-card data: the closer to a free cup, the more frequent the purchases (Journal of Marketing Research 43: 39–58).
Nunes and Drèze, 2006, who handed out 300 loyalty cards at random at a car wash: cards with 2 squares already stamped were redeemed at about 34%, blank cards at about 19% (Journal of Consumer Research 32: 504–512).

Common misconceptions

  • “The bigger the reward the better”: expectation absorbs rewards, and what really moves players is surprise and visible progress, with the size of the amount secondary.
  • “Dopamine is pleasure”: it is more a signal of “prediction error, learning, wanting,” and a different thing from “liking.”
  • “Variable rewards are always good”: they can make people unable to stop, and are also the mechanism of overindulgence and gambling-like play, so they must be used responsibly.
  • “The Zeigarnik effect: unfinished things are remembered better”: recent meta-analyses have not reproduced this reliably; the steadier finding is “wanting to go back and continue after an interruption.”

Reflection and debate

The classic conclusions on reinforcement schedules come mainly from animal experiments, and human behavior is also shaped by rules, language and social context. These mechanisms can explain “why it is easy to repeat,” and cannot show that “this game is fun.” If the action itself is dull, even the cleverest reward schedule only “drags” the player along, and the “dull action, maxed-out reward” preset in the break-the-loop experiment is an example.

The dopamine prediction-error explanation is the mainstream view in neuroscience, but some scholars stress that dopamine is also tied to motivation and salience. Replications of the Zeigarnik effect are inconsistent too.

Think about it: if a loop holds players with variable rewards, once the rewards stop, will the players stay?

Key points

  • A core loop is the thing the player does again and again, described by a “verb chain.” The loop must be complete: action → feedback → reward → growth → new goal.
  • First make the “seconds-long” small loop fun, then layer longer loops on top. If the action is dull, no number of rewards can save it.
  • The rule for handing out rewards (the reinforcement schedule) sets the rhythm of behavior: variable beats fixed at resisting extinction, and is also easier to abuse.
  • Skinner’s operant chamber made “how rewards are handed out” something that could be studied quantitatively for the first time.
  • The brain is most sensitive to “better than expected” surprise (prediction error, Schultz’s monkey experiments); expectation absorbs fixed rewards.
  • The closer to the goal, the more people want to finish; endowed progress makes people more willing to complete, and visible progress joins short loops into long ones.
A good loop is held up by “interesting action,” with rewards adding to it through surprise and progress; no pile of fixed gold, however big, can compare with them.
