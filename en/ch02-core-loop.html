Chapter 2

The Core Loop

The engine behind “one more round,” and why it keeps turning.

Learning goals

about 50 min
  • Write a game’s core loop as a verb chain, and use the “break the loop” experiment to say what happens when each link is missing.
  • Tell the four reinforcement schedules apart, and explain why Skinner set out to study “how rewards are handed out.”
  • Use reward prediction error (Schultz’s monkey experiments) and the goal gradient to explain why fixed rewards stop feeling like anything, and why progress bars pull.
1

What it isThe core loop: the circle that makes you play one more round

It is one in the morning and you tell yourself: last round, then bed. The round ends, and the button you press says “Play again.”

That is not entirely a question of willpower. The end of the last round quietly handed you a reason for the next one: you nearly won, a newly unlocked character is waiting to be tried, and the tension of a health bar down to its last notch has not worn off yet.

Draw this “ending feeds the beginning” structure and you get a circle. The chain of things a player repeats most is called the core loop. In Pac-Man it goes: eat dots → dodge ghosts → grab a power pellet → eat the ghosts → clear the board, then the next level.

Designers like to pin it down with a string of verbs, called a verb chain. Remember the “feedback” pillar from Chapter 1? A loop is feedback joined into a circle: you do something, the game responds, the response brings a reward, the reward makes you a little stronger, and being stronger opens up a new goal, so you are back at the start.

The arrows form a circle: a reward makes the next action stronger, or opens new choices, so the player wants another go.

But a game has more than one circle. Every second you jump, hit and dodge; every few minutes you finish a match; every few days you save up enough for a new piece of gear. Three circles nest one inside another, and the further in you go, the faster they turn:

This leads to a firm rule of design: if the innermost micro loop isn’t fun, no number of outer layers will make it turn. Nobody puts up with a dull jump just for “this week’s season reward.”

Plants vs. Zombies: collect sunlight → plant → hold off the zombies → get new plants → build stronger formations. Five verbs joined end to end, and every level won enriches the hand of cards you hold.
Minecraft (survival mode): gather → craft → build → explore farther → gather new materials. The urge to travel comes from “needing new materials,” so the loop is closed by need.
Scrolling short videos: swipe → watch one → (now and then) hit one that suits you perfectly → swipe again. Notice that “now and then”: the reward does not come every time, and that detail is about to matter a lot.
Vocabulary app check-ins: learn 20 words → mini quiz → streak +1 → want to protect the streak → learn again tomorrow. The app turns “growth” into an ever-lengthening number.
A cutscene that never loops back: a story you see once and that is over, with no reason to “do it again.” The arrows go straight to an end with no line curling back to the start, so it is not a loop.
A loop whose action is dull: click → get 1 coin → buy click power → click again. The circle is closed, but “click” is no fun at all, and the reward is just bribing the player. How fragile such a loop is will show up in the “break the loop” experiment later.

What it isHow rewards are handed out: four reinforcement schedules

Why is a slot machine harder to walk away from than a vending machine? Put in a coin and the vending machine always delivers, while the slot machine might pay out on the very next pull. The two machines give about the same thing; what differs is the way the reward comes.

Psychologists call the rule for when a reward is given a reinforcement schedule. Two questions sort them into four kinds: does the reward depend on “how many times you did it” or “how much time has passed”? And is that number fixed, or does it change each time?

Tap one to look:
Every row is the same 22 actions; the only difference is where the red stars land.
  • Fixed ratio (FR): a reward after every N responses. Every 5 monsters killed drops a chest, and 10 shards exchange for a character.
  • Variable ratio (VR): a reward once in N responses on average, but never knowing which one. Gacha pulls, monster drops, pull-to-refresh in social apps.
  • Fixed interval (FI): after each fixed stretch of time, the first response earns a reward. Energy refills every 6 hours; a daily login bonus.
  • Variable interval (VI): the time gaps are random. A rare boss that respawns at unpredictable times; a reply to a message that could arrive at any moment.
Continuous reinforcement (a reward every time): a vending machine, “click once, always get 1 coin.” A reward every time is actually the easiest thing to stop noticing, and the first thing you notice when it stops.
Payday is FI: on a fixed date every month. It doesn’t come early, and when the day arrives it always comes.
Fishing is close to VI: nobody knows when the fish will bite, so all you can do is keep watch and wait it out.
Monsters dropping gear is close to VR: every monster has a chance to drop something good, but nobody knows which one will.

How it was inventedThe Skinner box: how did anyone discover “how rewards are handed out” mattered?

The problem: how do you measure how rewards shape behavior?

In 1898 the American psychologist Edward Thorndike put hungry cats into “puzzle boxes,” with fish outside and a latch inside. The cat scratched and clawed at random, happened to knock the latch loose, and escaped a little faster the next time. From this he proposed that behavior with good consequences gets kept (later called the law of effect).

But a puzzle box had a drawback: the experimenter got one time recorded each time a cat escaped, and had to stand by the whole while, so the data were sparse and coarse. To study how rewards change behavior bit by bit, a box like that was not enough.

As a graduate student at Harvard, B. F. Skinner took a different approach: put a lever in the box, so that each press made a machine drop a pellet of food and automatically draw a mark on a paper tape. Nobody had to watch, no press was ever missed, and for the first time behavior could be recorded continuously and quantitatively. Later he mostly switched to pigeons, with a pecking key in place of the lever.

Then came a small, unexpected twist. By Skinner’s own 1956 recollection, the food pellets had to be made by hand then, which was tiring and never enough. So he wondered: what if instead of giving one for every press, he gave one every minute? The rats did not give up. They kept pressing, steadily. Even without a reward every time, the behavior held up, and the rhythm of pressing was very different from when every press paid off.

That made him and his colleague Charles Ferster want to know whether “when the reward comes” shapes behavior more than “how much comes.” From around 1949 to 1955 they systematically tried all sorts of reward rules on pigeons, and collected the results in Schedules of Reinforcement, published in 1957.

1898 Thorndike puts cats into puzzle boxes and records how long they take to escape: behavior is shaped by its consequences. He later sums this up as the law of effect.
1930s Skinner builds the “operant chamber”: a lever, automatic food delivery, an automatic paper tape. For the first time behavior can be measured continuously and quantitatively.
1950s Skinner and Ferster compare reward rules on a large scale with pigeons, and publish Schedules of Reinforcement in 1957.
After that “Intermittent rewards are harder to extinguish than a reward every time” becomes a classic result of learning psychology, and game designers borrow it to design drops, gacha pulls and timed rewards.

Why it worksWhy are variable rewards so “sticky”?

Back to those pigeons. After all rewards stop, a pigeon that had always been rewarded “every 5th peck” soon stops pecking, while a pigeon that never knew which peck would pay keeps pecking. Both had their food cut off, so why such a difference in reaction?

Uncertainty makes it hard to tell “not yet” from “never again”

In classic animal experiments, variable schedules produce behavior that is steadier and takes longer to die out once the rewards stop. This slower extinction is called the “partial reinforcement extinction effect” in textbooks. Variable ratios also produce high, steady response rates, whereas fixed intervals often show a scalloped curve: slacking right after a reward and pecking frantically as the time nears.

One common explanation: if you used to be paid on every 5th response, then “six misses in a row” is an obvious signal that the reward has been withdrawn. But if it was always paid only after a varying, sometimes long, run of tries, “another miss” tells you nothing, and the animal keeps trying. This is just what you saw in the simulation above: FR gives up quickly when the rewards stop, while VR holds out longer.

Link to the loop: for the “reward” link in a core loop, which schedule you pick changes the whole flavor of the loop. “Click once, get one” is continuous reinforcement; change it to “each click has a chance of dropping a golden mushroom” and it becomes a variable ratio.

Sources: C. B. Ferster and B. F. Skinner, Schedules of Reinforcement (Appleton-Century-Crofts, 1957); Skinner’s 1956 article “A case history in scientific method” (American Psychologist 11: 221–233) tells how the discovery came about, in Skinner’s own recollection. These regularities come mostly from pigeons and rats; in people they are also shaped by language, rules and expectations, so they will not be exactly the same.
Gacha pulls / loot boxes: whether each pull gives a rare item is unpredictable (VR). What players remember is “I almost got it last time,” not “I’ve already pulled dozens of times.”
Pull-to-refresh in social apps: you don’t know whether this refresh will show what you wanted to see, so you refresh once more.
A fixed daily login gift: fixed interval plus continuous reinforcement, the same every day, so it is quickly absorbed into expectation and gives no reason to stay after collecting it. That is why games often mix a bit of random surprise into fixed rewards.

How it was inventedWhat is dopamine “computing”? From surprise to prediction error

The problem: if rewards make us happy, why does the same reward every day stop feeling like anything?

In the late 1960s, learning psychologists found something odd. Pair a light with a shock and an animal normally learns “light on = a shock is coming.” But if the shock has already been fully announced by another signal, a new signal added on top is not learned at all. This is called blocking (Leon Kamin, 1969).

In other words, learning is not “remember whatever appears together,” but “surprise” drives learning. In 1972 Robert Rescorla and Allan Wagner wrote this idea as a formula, simplified: new expectation = old expectation + a small share of (actual − expected). The bigger the gap, the more is learned; when the gap is zero, there is nothing to learn.

But at the time the formula was only a mathematical guess, and nobody had found its shadow in the brain. Then in the 1990s, Wolfram Schultz and colleagues recorded signals matching it from dopamine neurons in monkeys:

Before a monkey has learned “light → juice,” an unexpected drop of juice makes these neurons fire strongly. After learning, the response moves to the instant the light comes on, and the juice itself barely triggers a response. If the light comes on but the juice doesn’t, then at the moment the juice should have appeared the firing actually drops below its usual level. In 1997 Schultz, Dayan and Montague published a review in Science that presented this as “dopamine signals a reward prediction error.”

Why it worksOnly surprise registers: reward prediction error

The same hundred coins: the first time you get them you are delighted; if they come on schedule every day, a week later you barely feel them; then one day 300 drops out of nowhere and you are on a high again.

The money did not change; the expectation in your head did. What the brain cares about is “how much better than I expected.”

With the same “expected 100,” what the actual amount is decides whether you feel anything.

Surprise = actual − expected

Reward prediction error (actual − expected): better than expected is positive, a “surprise”; exactly as expected is zero, “nothing felt”; worse than expected is negative, a “disappointment.” And since expectation is slowly pulled toward the actual reward by experience, a fixed reward gets “eaten” by expectation, and only change and surprise can produce positive error again and again.

The mainstream view in neuroscience is that the activity of midbrain dopamine neurons closely tracks this “prediction error,” and works more like a learning signal (“better than I thought, remember this”). Researchers such as Kent Berridge and Terry Robinson also point out that “wanting” and “liking” can be separated: the dopamine system has more to do with “wanting,” which is not the same as enjoyment itself. So “dopamine = pleasure” is a common oversimplification.

Link to the loop: this explains why a fixed reward makes a loop “go flat,” and why “growth” has to keep supplying something new, or the reward gets eaten by expectation.

Sources: Kamin (1969), the blocking experiment; Rescorla & Wagner (1972), the learning model; Wolfram Schultz, Peter Dayan and P. Read Montague, “A Neural Substrate of Prediction and Reward,” Science 275: 1593–1599 (1997); Kent Berridge and Terry Robinson, “Liking, wanting, and the incentive-sensitization theory of addiction,” American Psychologist 71: 670–679 (2016). The prediction-error hypothesis is the leading explanation today, but it is not without controversy.
A fixed 100 coins every day: after a week expectation has caught up with reality, the surprise drops toward zero, and players “feel nothing.”
Monthly salary versus a year-end bonus: you saw payday coming long ago, but an unexpected bonus makes you much happier.
Loot drops: most of the time you get ordinary gear and occasionally a purple one, and that one surprise is the positive error.
A big reward is not a big surprise: if you hand out 1000 coins every time, expectation soon climbs to 1000, and by the logic of prediction error that feels like even less than “usually 100, sometimes 500.” (This is a deduction, not a measurement.)

Why it worksMore: why does a progress bar pull? The goal gradient and endowed progress

With 8 squares already stamped on a loyalty card, you want to fill the last 2 more; when a task is interrupted halfway, it nags at the back of your mind.

Retailers everywhere hand out loyalty cards, but nobody knew how to design them for the best effect. In 2006 the marketing scholars Joseph Nunes and Xavier Drèze ran a field experiment at a car wash: 300 cards were handed out at random, half reading “8 empty squares, fill them for a free wash” and half reading “10 empty squares, but 2 already stamped.”

Both cards needed eight paid washes to redeem. Over nine months of follow-up, about 34% of those given the “2 already stamped” card redeemed it, against about 19% with the blank card. They called this the endowed progress effect: making a task feel already “started” makes it more likely to be finished.

The two cards cost exactly the same money and trips; the only difference is that one “looks like it has already begun.”

The closer to the goal, the harder you push; unfinished things are hard to put down

Goal gradient: the closer to the finish, the harder people (and animals) push. The psychologist Clark Hull proposed it around the 1930s using rats: the nearer the food, the faster they run. In 2006, Ran Kivetz and colleagues found the same thing in real coffee-shop loyalty-card data: the closer customers got to a free coffee, the more often they bought coffee.

The Zeigarnik effect (1927) is often summed up as “unfinished things are remembered better,” but be careful: a 2025 meta-analysis (Ghibellini & Meier) found that the “remembered better” part is hard to reproduce reliably, whereas the tendency to go back and continue after an interruption (the Ovsiankina effect) is more robust. So the safer way to say it when designing is: being unfinished makes people want to come back, not remember more.

Link to the loop: progress bars, XP bars and chapter checklists make the “growth” link visible, so every small action brings the finish line closer and joins short loops into long ones. It is a double-edged sword, and when abused it becomes a “hook” that people can’t put down.

Sources: Clark Hull, the goal gradient (1930s; accounts differ on the exact year); Ran Kivetz, Oleg Urminsky and Yuhuang Zheng, Journal of Marketing Research 43: 39–58 (2006); Joseph Nunes and Xavier Drèze, Journal of Consumer Research 32: 504–512 (2006), the car-wash experiment; Bluma Zeigarnik (1927); Ghibellini & Meier, Humanities and Social Sciences Communications (2025, meta-analysis). The car-wash figures of 34% and 19% come from secondary accounts and have not been checked against the original.
Coffee-shop loyalty cards: the closer to a free cup, the more often people come.
XP bars in games: “one more round and I level up,” round after round.
A book interrupted: called to dinner at the key scene, you will most likely go straight back to it afterward. Not necessarily remembering it better, but wanting to go on.
Progress you can’t see: a long to-do list with no progress display, or a progress bar that is always “almost there” and then resets, makes players feel cheated rather than pulled along.

Common misconceptions

  • “The bigger the reward, the better”: expectation absorbs rewards, and what really moves players is surprise and visible progress, not the raw amount.
  • “Dopamine is pleasure”: it is closer to a signal of “prediction error, learning and wanting,” and is not the same thing as “liking.”
  • “Variable rewards are always good”: they can make people unable to stop, and that is also the mechanism behind compulsive play and gambling-like design, so use them responsibly.
  • “The Zeigarnik effect: unfinished things are remembered better”: recent meta-analyses have not reproduced this reliably; the steadier finding is “wanting to go back and continue after an interruption.”

Reflection and debate

The classic findings on reinforcement schedules come mainly from animal experiments, and human behavior is also shaped by rules, language and social context. These mechanisms can explain “why it is easy to repeat,” but not directly “whether this game is fun”: if the action itself is dull, even the cleverest reward schedule is just “dragging” the player along, and the “dull action, maxed-out reward” preset in the break-the-loop experiment is an example.

The prediction-error account of dopamine is the mainstream view in neuroscience but not the only one; some scholars stress that it is also tied to motivation and salience, and results on the Zeigarnik effect are inconsistent too. Treat these as inspiration for design, not as guarantees.

Think about it: if your loop holds players with variable rewards, will they still stay once the rewards stop?

Key points

  • The core loop is what players do again and again, described as a verb chain; a loop should be complete: action → feedback → reward → growth → new goal.
  • Make the second-by-second small loop fun first, then layer longer loops on top; if the action is dull, no amount of reward can save it.
  • The rule for handing out rewards (the reinforcement schedule) sets the rhythm of behavior: variable beats fixed at resisting extinction, and is also easier to abuse.
  • Skinner’s operant chamber made “how rewards are handed out” something that could be studied quantitatively for the first time.
  • The brain is most sensitive to “better than expected” surprise (prediction error, Schultz’s monkey experiments); expectation absorbs fixed rewards.
  • The closer to the goal, the more people want to finish; endowed progress makes people more willing to complete, and visible progress joins short loops into long ones.
A good loop is held up by “an interesting action,” with surprise and progress adding points to the reward, rather than an ever-bigger fixed pile of coins.
