Chapter 6

Flow & Difficulty

Not too hard, not too easy, just within reach; and what players actually remember.

Learning goals

about 40 min
  • Name the three conditions flow needs, and tell which one a game moment is missing.
  • Explain why “scaffolding” has to be withdrawn as skill grows, and design a schedule for removing hints for newcomers.
  • Use the “peak–end rule” to arrange a level’s highlight and its closing, and know what it can and can’t account for.
1

What it isThe flow channel: why three hours feel like three minutes

You have probably had an evening like this: you meant to play just one more level, and when you looked up it was dark outside. Dinner went cold and you didn’t notice, your phone rang and you didn’t hear it, and three hours felt like three minutes.

This state of total absorption, where you forget the time, is called flow in psychology. The condition for it sounds plain: the thing in front of you has to be a bit hard, but hard in just the way you can reach.

Picture a channel. The horizontal axis is your skill and the vertical axis is the challenge of the task. When the challenge is far above your skill, you panic; when it is far below, you doze off; when the two roughly match, you are in the channel. This state is called challenge–skill balance.

The diagram below is that channel. Drag the dot, or move the two sliders, and see which zone you are in.

Try it: first drag the dot into the anxiety zone at upper left, then to the boredom zone at lower right, and finally back to the green channel. Once you have visited all three zones, a check mark appears here.

One thing is easy to overlook: the player’s skill keeps growing. Yesterday’s “just right” is a bit easy today, so the challenge has to climb too, or the dot will slowly slide horizontally into “boredom.”

Tetris: the pieces fall a little faster with each level while your hands quietly get practiced. Every level is a bit harder than the last yet you can just about keep up, so many people lose track of time as they play.
Rock climbing: a good route is like a string of holds just within reach; the next one needs a stretch on tiptoe and a straight arm, but not a flying leap. With the right route, a person can’t stop all day; with one far beyond you, your arm shakes and all that is left in your head is “how do I get down.”
A veteran dropped into the starter village: with challenge far below skill, he won’t get angry, only sleepy. Many games’ “difficulty options” exist so that veterans don’t fall asleep here.
The difficulty is right, but nobody tells you where to go: this is the most “almost right” case. The dot is in the channel, but you wander around an open world, and half an hour later you close the game. Flow needs more than the right difficulty.
2

How it was inventedPeople who couldn’t stop, and weren’t paid for it

Why painters skip meals

In the 1960s in Chicago, a young psychologist named Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi was doing his doctoral research, watching art students paint. He saw something that psychology at the time couldn’t easily explain: these people painted for hours at a stretch, until they forgot to eat and forgot to be tired. Nobody was paying them wages, and nobody was urging them on.

The mainstream in psychology then was behaviorism, and its answer was blunt: people do things for rewards, whether money or grades. But where was these painters’ reward? The canvas wasn’t finished, and no reward had come yet.

He and his colleagues took this question to chess players, rock climbers, dancers and surgeons. Before reading on, guess what they mostly said when asked “what is the moment you enjoy most when doing this?”

1960s Csikszentmihalyi watches art students in Chicago. What puzzles him is that they are so deeply absorbed, yet the cause is nothing given from outside: it seems that the act of painting itself has caught them.
1970s His team widens the interviews to chess players, rock climbers, free dancers and surgeons, asking about their most enjoyable moments. Different people say almost the same thing, and several of them use the same metaphor: it is like being carried along by a current. This is where the word “flow” comes from.
1975, 1990 In 1975 he publishes Beyond Boredom and Anxiety. The title already gives the core: an absorbed person has to avoid two pits at once, boredom and anxiety. His 1990 book Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience brings these ideas to general readers.
Today Game designers who read this find it is almost the very problem they solve every day: the player’s skill keeps changing, so how do you make the difficulty change along with it and keep people in the channel?
3

Why it worksIs the right difficulty enough?

Drawing the channel is easy; the hard part is asking: if the difficulty is right, is absorption guaranteed? The open world where you wandered around has already given one counterexample. Think of another: every step makes you wait ten seconds with a spinner before you know the result. However well matched the difficulty, it is hard to sink in.

The three conditions of flow

Csikszentmihalyi summed up the experience of people in flow in eight or nine features. The ones game design cites most are the three he calls “conditions”: clear goals (you always know what to do now), immediate feedback (you always know how you are doing) and a balance of challenge and skill. Total absorption and losing track of time are the results once the conditions are met.

The “three conditions” are a common simplification in game design, not the full list from the original book. Researchers have since reworked flow into models made specifically for assessing games, such as GameFlow.

Sources: Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, Beyond Boredom and Anxiety (1975) and Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience (1990).

5

What it isThe zone of proximal development and scaffolding: how many hints to give

Almost every designer who has made a tutorial level has stepped into the same pit: give too few hints and newcomers leave at level two; give too many and players become machines that “press the button the hint points to,” and at level ten, with no hints, they are lost.

How many hints should you give? Educational psychologists have been wondering about this for a long time. The Soviet psychologist Vygotsky gave a very useful way to put it: a task is either one you can do alone, or one you can do with someone’s help, or one you can’t do however you’re helped. Real learning happens in the middle band.

That band is called the zone of proximal development. The temporary support given within it is called scaffolding, like the frame put up when constructing a building, which comes down once the building stands.

🧱 Three bands: can do alone, can do with help, can’t do yet

The yellow band is the zone of proximal development. As skill grows it all moves right, so the scaffolding has to move with it and finally come down.

Learning to ride a bike: a grown-up first runs along holding the saddle, the child wobbles, and the adult grips firmly only when it looks like a fall. A few days later the hand only rests lightly, and a few days after that the child glances back and finds nobody there.
A tutorial level: the first time, a big arrow points straight at it; the second time, only a little icon is left; the third time, nothing, and you’re on your own. The hints are withdrawn one notch at a time, and the player hardly notices.
Hints always full: at level 30 the arrow is still flashing on every screen. The player never has to think and so never learns. This isn’t helping them, it is playing for them.
6

How it was inventedTwo children with the same score

What the intelligence tests missed

In the early twentieth century, schools liked to score children with intelligence tests: shut a child in a room, have them answer questions alone, and see what age level they reach. Lev Vygotsky (1896–1934) saw a loophole: it measured only what a child can do “now,” not what they could learn “very soon.”

He gave an example: two children both test at the level of an eight-year-old. Then he gives both harder problems, with a little guidance alongside. Guess how high each can be brought?

1930s Vygotsky writes: two children both answer alone at an eight-year-old level, but with some guidance one can do twelve-year-old problems and the other reaches only nine. Their scores working alone are the same, yet their true potential differs greatly. He calls the gap between “what they can do alone” and “what they can do with help” the zone of proximal development. (This is his own example, not a controlled experiment.)
1976 David Wood, Jerome Bruner and Gail Ross study how tutors help three- to five-year-olds build a block pyramid: break the task down, point out the key spots, demonstrate once, and step back when the child can do it alone. They call this temporary help that gets taken away “scaffolding.”
Today Tutorial levels, aim assist, easy modes and fading hints are in essence all one knob: turn it slowly from “full” to “none.” The one difficulty is how fast to turn it.

Why it worksWhy scaffolding has to come down

The zone of proximal development and scaffolding

Vygotsky’s core judgment is that learning doesn’t happen where you “already can,” nor where you “absolutely can’t,” but where you “can reach if you stretch.” Scaffolding temporarily turns a task that was out of reach into one within reach; once you can do it alone, it should come down, or you stay stuck in the state of “only works with help.”

The sketch model below writes this idea as the simplest rule: the speed at which a learner’s skill grows depends on how far the “effective difficulty with hints” sits above current skill. About 12 points above is easiest to learn from, and too close (too easy) or too far (too hard) both give slow learning. It is just a simplified assumption, used to compare three hint strategies.

Sources: Lev Vygotsky, Mind in Society (1978, English collection); David Wood, Jerome Bruner and Gail Ross, “The role of tutoring in problem solving,” Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry 17(2): 89–100 (1976). How it was inventedWhat you remember is not what you went through

There is an experience like this: a level is great all the way through, but the last moment you die for no clear reason, and you tell your friend “this level is terrible.” Yet for the ten minutes before, you were smiling.

Are you lying? No. Your memory does the scoring, and memory scores with a different algorithm. The psychologist Daniel Kahneman and colleagues noticed that when people look back on an episode, the score they give is often not the sum of every moment but built on a few snapshots.

Suffer 30 seconds more, and be more willing to do it again

To test this, the researchers thought up a rather cruel method: have people put a hand into cold water. In the first trial, the hand goes into water at about 14 °C for 60 seconds. In the second, 60 seconds again, and then 30 seconds more, during which the water is quietly warmed by about a degree: still uncomfortable, but less cold.

After both are over, you are asked to pick one to repeat. Guess first: which would you choose?

1993 Kahneman, Barbara Fredrickson, Charles Schreiber and Donald Redelmeier run the cold-water experiment above. The result: most people chose the one that had 30 seconds more suffering in total. The extra stretch was uncomfortable too, but it felt better than what came before, so the feeling left at the end in memory was better.
1996, 2003 Could the lab result be a coincidence? Redelmeier and Kahneman went to a hospital and made the same observation in patients undergoing colonoscopy, finding that patients’ ratings of the whole procedure depended mainly on the most painful moment and how it felt at the end, and little on how long it lasted. In 2003 they ran a randomized trial with 682 patients: for half of them the procedure was extended at the end by a short stretch of mild discomfort. The group that suffered more afterward actually found the whole procedure not so bad.
Later These findings came to be popularly called the “peak–end rule.” For designers it gives a concrete handle: you don’t have to make every second the best, but the strongest moment and the final wrap-up must shine.
Beating the final boss: the boss falls, confetti bursts across the screen, and the music swells. Even if the first half dragged a little, what the player takes away from the level is this moment.
A meal: the early dishes were so-so, then a good piece of cake comes last, and on the way home you will most likely say “that was a good meal.” It is no coincidence that restaurants save dessert for last.
Killed instantly in the final second: the line climbs all the way and then drops straight down at the end. The whole level’s rating is dragged away by that one blow. What needs fixing is the ending, not cutting the fun stretches before it.
9

Why it worksThe peak–end rule: why memory plays favorites

The peak–end rule

Kahneman’s view is that we really have two “selves.” One lives in the moment and feels each instant; the other looks back afterward, gives the whole experience a score, and decides whether to come again. Retrospective evaluation is often closer to the average of the most intense moment (the peak) and the feeling at the end (the end), and is relatively insensitive to how long it lasted, which is called duration neglect.

One thing to keep apart: this is a finding about remembered evaluation, not “the experience at every moment.” Looking at the total amount of experience, longer good times are still more; it is just that when people recall, and when they decide “whether to play again,” they depend more on the peak and the end. In later research the strength of this effect varies with the situation.

Sources: Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber and Redelmeier, “When more pain is preferred to less: Adding a better end,” Psychological Science 4(6) (1993); Redelmeier and Kahneman, Pain 66(1): 3–8 (1996); Redelmeier, Katz and Kahneman, “Memories of colonoscopy: a randomized trial,” Pain 104: 187–194 (2003). The “peak–end rule” is a popular name later people gave this kind of finding.

Common misconceptions

  • “The harder, the more immersive.” No: a challenge far beyond skill is anxiety; immersion comes from a “match,” and also needs a goal and feedback.
  • “The more hints, the friendlier.” Hints that are always full make learning stall; good hints are withdrawn as skill grows, or left to the player to choose.
  • “As long as the ending is good, the middle doesn’t matter.” The peak–end rule describes remembered evaluation, not that the process is unimportant. If the process is too poor, players leave on the spot and never reach the ending.
  • “Flow is always good.” Flow only says “deeply engaged”; it does not mean “good for you.” Designs that make people unable to stop rely on it too.

Reflection and debate

Flow research depends on respondents’ reports of their own states, so measurement is not easy, and the “challenge–skill” diagram does not suit every person or every activity. Some criticize game models like GameFlow for oversimplifying a complex experience.

The game designer Raph Koster proposed another explanation in A Theory of Fun for Game Design (2004): much of the fun of a game is the feedback of the brain learning new patterns, and once a game is fully mastered it becomes a “solved problem” and is no longer fun. This is his view; it can explain “getting bored once you’re good,” but not necessarily the pleasures of socializing or of the senses.

“Scaffolding” comes from education, and players don’t always want to be “taught”: too much hand-holding spoils the pleasure of exploring, and how to balance it is a design trade-off. Think about it: in a game you have played, which “ending” changed your view of the whole game? Was that the peak–end rule, or something else?

Key points

  • Flow = a match between challenge and skill, plus a clear goal and immediate feedback; if any one is missing, absorption is discounted.
  • Csikszentmihalyi found flow in “people who can’t stop even without rewards”; a player’s skill keeps growing, so difficulty has to grow with it.
  • A good difficulty curve hugs the growth of skill and has ups and downs: apply pressure, release, apply pressure again.
  • The zone of proximal development is the band of “can do with someone’s help”; scaffolding goes only in this band and is withdrawn as skill grows.
  • When players rate a level, the peak and the ending carry a lot of weight; but this is a finding about memory and can’t be used to cut corners on the process.
Keep players always “just within reach”: difficulty a bit above skill, a goal and feedback, hints withdrawn step by step, and the strongest moment and the ending done well.
