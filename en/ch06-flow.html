Chapter 6

Flow & Difficulty

Not too hard, not too easy, just within reach; and what players actually remember.

Learning goals

about 40 min
  • Name the three conditions flow needs, and judge which one a moment in a game is missing.
  • Explain why “scaffolding” should be withdrawn step by step as skill grows, and design a rhythm of withdrawing hints for beginners.
  • Use the “peak-end rule” to arrange a level’s highlight and ending, and know what it governs and what it doesn’t.
1

What it isThe flow channel: why three hours feel like three minutes

Many people have had a night like this: they only meant to play one more level, and when they look up, it is dark outside. Dinner went cold unnoticed, the phone rang unheard, and three hours felt like three minutes.

This state of full absorption in which time is forgotten is called flow in psychology. The condition for it is plain: the thing in front of you should be a little hard, hard in just the way you can reach.

Put skill on the horizontal axis and the challenge of the task on the vertical, and a channel appears in the middle. When challenge is far above skill, people panic; far below, people grow sleepy; only when the two are about even are they in the channel. This is challenge–skill balance.

Concept card

Flow

Flow

Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi · Beyond Boredom and Anxiety, 1975; Flow, 1990, for general readers

A state of full absorption in which time is forgotten. Challenge close to skill, plus a clear goal and immediate feedback, makes it easy to enter.

ExampleIn Tetris the blocks fall a little faster with every level while the hands quietly get practiced, and many people lose track of time as they play.

A veteran dropped into the starter village: with challenge far below skill, he won’t get angry, only sleepy. Many games’ “difficulty options” exist so that veterans don’t fall asleep here.
Difficulty just right, but nobody says where to go: this is the most “nearly right” kind. The dot is in the channel, yet in an open world you wander all over, and half an hour later the player closes the game. Flow needs more than just-right difficulty.
2

How it was inventedPeople who couldn’t stop, and weren’t paid for it

Why painters skip meals

In the 1960s in Chicago, a young psychologist named Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi was doing his doctoral research, watching art students paint. He saw something that psychology at the time couldn’t easily explain: these people painted for hours at a stretch, until they forgot to eat and forgot to be tired. Nobody was paying them wages, and nobody was urging them on.

Then the mainstream of psychology was behaviorism, and its line was blunt: people act for rewards, either money or points. But where were these painters’ rewards? The canvas wasn’t finished, and no reward had come yet.

With this question he and his colleagues went and asked chess players, rock climbers, dancers and surgeons. Don’t read on yet; guess how they would mostly answer when asked “what moment do you enjoy most when doing this?”

1960s Csikszentmihalyi watches art students in Chicago. What puzzles him is that they are so deeply absorbed, yet the cause is nothing given from outside: it seems that the act of painting itself has caught them.
1970s His team widened the interviews to chess players, rock climbers, free dancers and surgeons, asking them for the moments they enjoyed most. Different people described almost the same thing, and several used the same metaphor: like being carried along by a current. The word “flow” came from this.
1975, 1990 In 1975 he publishes Beyond Boredom and Anxiety. The title already gives the core: an absorbed person has to avoid two pits at once, boredom and anxiety. His 1990 book Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience brings these ideas to general readers.
Today Game designers who read this find it is almost the very problem they solve every day: the player’s skill keeps changing, so how do you make the difficulty change along with it and keep people in the channel?
3

Why it worksIs the right difficulty enough?

Drawing the channel is easy, but just-right difficulty does not guarantee immersion. The open world where you wander all over was already a counterexample. Think of another case: you wait ten seconds, spinning, after every step to learn the result, and however suitable the difficulty, it is hard to sink in.

The three conditions of flow

Csikszentmihalyi summed up the experience of people in flow in eight or nine features. The three most often cited in game design are those he calls “conditions”: clear goals (always knowing what to do now), immediate feedback (always knowing how you are doing) and a balance of challenge and skill. Total absorption and forgetting time are the result once the conditions are met.

The “three conditions” are a common simplification in game design, not the full list from the original book. Researchers have since reworked flow into models made specifically for assessing games, such as GameFlow.

Sources: Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, Beyond Boredom and Anxiety (1975) and Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience (1990).

5

What it isThe zone of proximal development and scaffolding: how many hints to give

Almost every designer who has made a tutorial level has fallen into the same pit. Too few hints, and a newcomer gets stuck in level two and leaves. Too many, and players become machines that “press the button the hint says,” and are lost by level ten when there are no hints.

How many hints should there be? Educational psychologists have long thought about this question. The Soviet psychologist Vygotsky had a very useful way of putting it: a thing is either doable alone, doable with someone’s help, or undoable however you are helped. Real learning happens in the middle band.

The middle band is the zone of proximal development. The temporary support given in this band is scaffolding, like the frame put up to build a house, taken down once the building stands.

Concept card

Zone of proximal development

Zone of proximal development (ZPD)

Lev Vygotsky · proposed in the 1930s; the English collection Mind in Society appeared in 1978

The band between what a person can do alone and what they can do with someone’s help. Learning happens in this band.

ExampleLearning to ride a bicycle: an adult holds the seat, the child wobbles, and the adult grips only when a fall looks near. The child can’t ride alone but can with help, and that is the zone of proximal development.

🧱 Three bands: can do alone, can do with help, can’t do yet

The yellow band is the zone of proximal development. As skill grows it all moves right, so the scaffolding has to move with it and finally come down.

Learning to ride a bicycle: an adult first runs holding the seat, the child wobbles, and the adult grips firmly only when it looks like a fall. A few days later, the hand rests lightly. A few days after that the child turns to look back and finds nobody behind.
The tutorial level: the first time, a thick arrow points straight out the direction. The second time, only a small icon is left. The third time, nothing, and the player does it alone. The hints are taken away one notch at a time, and the player hardly notices.
Hints always full: at level 30 the arrow is still flashing on every screen. The player never has to think and so never learns. This isn’t helping them, it is playing for them.
6

How it was inventedTwo children with the same score

What the intelligence tests missed

In the early twentieth century, scoring children with intelligence tests was in fashion at schools: shut the child in a room, have them answer questions alone, and see what age level they reach. Lev Vygotsky (1896–1934) felt there was a loophole: the test measured only what the child can do “now,” not what they could “learn right away.”

He gave an example: two children both tested at an eight-year-old level. Then both were given harder problems with a little guidance beside them. Guess how high they could be taken.

1930s Vygotsky wrote: two children both at an eight-year level on their own, and with guidance one could do twelve-year problems and the other only reached nine. Their scores working alone were the same, yet their real potential differed a great deal. He called the gap between “what can be done alone” and “what can be done with help” the zone of proximal development. (This is his own example, not a controlled experiment.)
1976 David Wood, Jerome Bruner and Gail Ross study how tutors help three- to five-year-olds build a block pyramid: break the task down, point out the key spots, demonstrate once, and step back when the child can do it alone. They call this temporary help that gets taken away “scaffolding.”
Today Tutorial levels, aim assist, easy mode and fading hints are all the same knob: turned slowly from “full” to “none.” The one hard part is how fast to turn it.

Scaffolding

Scaffolding

Why it worksWhy scaffolding has to come down

The zone of proximal development and scaffolding

Vygotsky’s core judgment is that learning happens neither where one “already can” nor where one “can’t at all,” but where one “can reach it with a stretch.” Scaffolding temporarily turns an out-of-reach task into a reachable one.

Once the learner can do it alone, scaffolding should be withdrawn, or they will stay stuck in the state of “only works with help.”

The illustrative model below writes this idea as the simplest rule: the speed at which the learner’s skill grows depends on how much the “effective difficulty with hints” exceeds current skill. About 12 points above is easiest to learn; too close (too easy) or too far (too hard) and learning is very slow. This is only a simplifying assumption, for comparing three hint strategies.

8

How it was inventedThe cold-water experiment: how memory scores an experience

There is a kind of experience: a level is fun all the way through, but dies senselessly on the very last move, and you tell a friend “this level is terrible.” Yet you had been smiling for the whole ten minutes before.

Is that lying? No. Memory is the one scoring, and memory uses another algorithm. The psychologist Daniel Kahneman and colleagues noticed that when people look back on an experience, the score is often not the moments added up, but a grab of a few fragments.

Suffer 30 seconds more, and be more willing to do it again

To test this, the researchers thought up a rather cruel method: have people put a hand into cold water. In the first trial, the hand goes into water at about 14 °C for 60 seconds. In the second, 60 seconds again, and then 30 seconds more, during which the water is quietly warmed by about a degree: still uncomfortable, but less cold.

After both are over, people are asked to choose one to redo. Guess first: which would you choose?

1993 Kahneman, Barbara Fredrickson, Charles Schreiber and Donald Redelmeier run the cold-water experiment above. The result: most people chose the one that had 30 seconds more suffering in total. The extra stretch was uncomfortable too, but it felt better than what came before, so the feeling left at the end in memory was better.
1996, 2003 Could the lab result be a coincidence? Redelmeier and Kahneman went to a hospital and made the same observation in patients undergoing colonoscopy, finding that patients’ ratings of the whole procedure depended mainly on the most painful moment and how it felt at the end, and little on how long it lasted. In 2003 they ran a randomized trial with 682 patients: for half of them the procedure was extended at the end by a short stretch of mild discomfort. The group that suffered more afterward actually found the whole procedure not so bad.
Later Such findings came to be popularly called the “peak-end rule.” For designers it gives a concrete handle: you needn’t make every second the best, but the strongest moment and the final wrap-up must shine.
Beating the final boss: the boss falls, confetti bursts across the screen, and the music swells. Even if the first half dragged a little, what the player takes away from the level is this moment.
A meal: the earlier dishes were so-so, and then a good piece of cake comes last, and on the way home you will probably say “that was a good meal.” A restaurant saving dessert for last is no coincidence.
Killed in one hit in the last second: the line climbs all the way and then drops straight down at the end, and the rating of the whole level is pulled away by that one blow. What needs fixing is the ending, and there is no need to cut the fun stretches before it.
9

Why it worksThe peak–end rule: why memory plays favorites

The peak–end rule

Kahneman’s view is that people actually have two “selves.” One lives through the moment, feeling each instant. The other looks back afterward and gives the whole experience one score, deciding whether to do it again.

Retrospective evaluation is closer to the average of the most intense moment (peak) and the feeling at the end (end), and is relatively insensitive to how long it lasted, which is called duration neglect.

Keep this straight: this is a finding about memory evaluation, not “the experience of each moment at the time.” Looking at the total of the experience, a longer good time is still more. It is only that when people recall and decide “play again or not,” they lean on the peak and the end. In later research the strength of this effect varies with the situation.

Sources:
Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber and Redelmeier, 1993, in which people put a hand in cold water, once for 60 seconds and once for 90 seconds with a slightly warmer end: most chose the one with more suffering (“When more pain is preferred to less: Adding a better end,” Psychological Science 4(6)).
Redelmeier and Kahneman, 1996, observing colonoscopy patients: ratings depended mainly on the most painful moment and the end (Pain 66(1): 3–8).
Redelmeier, Katz and Kahneman, 2003, a randomized trial with 682 patients: a short stretch of mild discomfort added at the end of the procedure made ratings better (Pain 104: 187–194). “Peak-end rule” is a later popular name.

Common misconceptions

  • “The higher the difficulty, the more immersive.” No: a challenge far above skill is anxiety. Immersion comes from “matching,” and still needs goals and feedback.
  • “The more hints the friendlier.” Hints always at full make learning stall. Good hints withdraw as skill grows, or are left for the player to choose.
  • “As long as the ending is good, the middle can be anything.” The peak-end rule describes memory evaluation, and the process still matters. If the process is too poor, players leave on the spot and never reach the ending.
  • “Flow is simply a good thing.” Flow shows only “very engaged,” which doesn’t mean “good for the person.” Designs that make people unable to stop rely on it just the same.

Reflection and debate

Flow research depends on respondents’ reports of their own states, so measurement is not easy, and the “challenge–skill” diagram does not suit every person or every activity. Some criticize game models like GameFlow for oversimplifying a complex experience.

In A Theory of Fun for Game Design (2004), the game designer Raph Koster offered another explanation: the fun of a game is largely the brain’s feedback as it learns new patterns, and once fully mastered, the game becomes a “solved problem” and is no longer fun. This is his view. It can explain “getting tired once practiced,” while the pleasure of socializing and of the senses lies outside what it explains.

“Scaffolding” comes from education, and players don’t always want to be “taught.” Too much hand-holding spoils the pleasure of exploring, and how to balance is a design trade-off.

Think about it: in a game you have played, which “ending” changed your view of the whole game? Was that the peak-end rule, or something else?

Key points

  • Flow = a match between challenge and skill, plus a clear goal and immediate feedback; if any one is missing, absorption is discounted.
  • Csikszentmihalyi found flow in “people who can’t stop even without a reward.” A player’s skill keeps rising, so the difficulty must rise too.
  • A good difficulty curve hugs the growth of skill and has ups and downs: apply pressure, release, apply pressure again.
  • The zone of proximal development is the band of “can do with someone’s help”; scaffolding goes only in this band and is withdrawn as skill grows.
  • When players rate a level, the peak and the ending carry a lot of weight; but this is a finding about memory and can’t be used to cut corners on the process.
Keep players always “just within reach”: difficulty a bit above skill, a goal and feedback, hints withdrawn step by step, and the strongest moment and the ending done well.
