Chapter 15

Balance & Economy

Balancing is turning knobs without end, and behind every knob there is a pipe.

Learning goals

  • Tell two approaches to balance apart (counters, and the faucet–bucket–sink pipes), and say why every game economy has to be designed with a “sink.”
  • Explain three odd phenomena: why the same reward feels like less each time, why numbers should grow by percentages, and why an average can lie.
  • Use the economy pipe diagram and the unit-balancing table to tune numbers yourself, and say what broke and why.
about 50 min
1

What it isBalance does not mean “equally strong”

Say you make a fighting game with three characters whose stats are high in some places and low in others. In the first week the forum erupts: “A is too strong, nerf it!” So all three characters get exactly the same stats. The arguing stops, but a few days later almost nobody picks a character with any care.

The reason is easy to see: if every pick is the same, picking means nothing. Balance means every choice has its own use and none is always the best, and equal numbers are not balance.

There are two common ways: one makes options counter each other, and the other keeps control of what flows in and out of a resource. The first comes first.

It tackles a stubborn trouble: as soon as one option’s numbers are slightly higher, clever players all go for it. Trim it a little, and the next “strongest” pops up, and you never catch up. Counters change the idea: don’t compare who is bigger, compare who faces whom. A non-transitive relation means the position of “strongest” does not exist at all, and players can only watch what the opponent plays and then decide what to play themselves.

Rock-paper-scissors (daily life): nobody is the strongest, so every round is about guessing and bluffing the other side. Drawn out, it is a circle that closes on itself, with no “highest point” anywhere in it.
The unit triangle (games): cavalry beat archers, archers beat spearmen, spearmen beat cavalry. Whatever the opponent fields, you switch to the counter, and that is why scouting before a battle means something.
Fire, water and grass counters (games): water beats fire, grass beats water, fire beats grass. Putting a team together becomes a puzzle, not piling up the highest numbers.
Two failed kinds of “balance”: on the left every character has the same stats, which looks fair, yet the choice means nothing. On the right one character is better at everything and everyone queues up to pick it. In the picture neither side is balanced: one lacks difference and the other lacks counters.
2

What it isFaucet, bucket, sink: where money comes from and where it goes

The second approach manages the flow of things in and out. Think of a wallet: pay comes in, rent and meals go out, and what remains sits in the account. Gold, stamina and materials in a game work the same way. Some people draw it as plumbing: what comes in is the faucet, what goes out is the sink, and what collects in between is the bucket.

Concept card

Faucets and Sinks

Faucets and sinks

Industry vocabulary of game economy design, with no single originator; behind it is the stock-and-flow thinking of system dynamics

A faucet is a source that lets currency or resources into the game, and a sink is where they leave it. The two ends have to match for the water level to stay steady.

ExampleFighting monsters and doing quests are faucets for gold; repairing gear, buying potions and paying tax are sinks.

The water level in the bucket is simply inflow minus outflow, accumulated bit by bit. If the faucet is wider than the sink, the level climbs all the way. If the sink is wider than the faucet, the bucket runs dry. To keep the level at a comfortable height, the two ends have to roughly match.

Pay and rent (daily life): pay is the faucet, rent and meals are the sinks, and savings are the bucket. If pay rises while prices don’t, savings grow and grow. The other way round, the account is empty every month.
Gold in a role-playing game (games): killing monsters and doing quests are the faucet, while repairing gear, buying potions and paying tax are the sinks. With good sinks, gold stays useful for the whole game.
The stamina bar (games): natural recovery is the faucet, and sprinting and attacking are the sinks. If recovery is too fast, fights lose their tension. Too slow, and the player is always at “empty bar.”
Only a faucet, no sink: rewards are handed out generously, but nobody thought about where the money would be spent. Players pile up more and more, potions are priced at five digits and nobody cares, and every price loses its meaning.
3

How it was inventedWhy is a sink a must?

Let players earn, and then what?

The first thing early online-game makers thought of was “let players earn”: monsters drop gold, quests pay gold, which sounds generous. But once gold can buy and sell things, it is no longer just a score. It is money, and it follows the rules of money.

2000 Diablo II was released. The story that circulated in the player community is that gold quickly became worthless, and people traded in rare items instead, such as a ring called the Stone of Jordan. Later a duplication exploit flooded the game with this “currency” and prices collapsed. According to the game wiki, patch 1.10 added quests that consume such rings, meant to shrink the stock, which amounts to opening a new sink.
2001 The economist Edward Castronova published a working paper that treated Norrath, the world of EverQuest, as a real economy and estimated it: he put its per-capita output between that of Russia and Bulgaria. The estimation method is disputed, but it made people take seriously that “a virtual economy is a real economy.”
Since then Online games have gained fixed sinks: repair fees, auction-house fees, the cost of upgrading and rerolling gear, and limited-time consumables. Designers no longer look only at “how much to give,” but at three things together: faucets, sinks and stock.
Concept card

Virtual Economy

Virtual economy

Edward Castronova · 2001 working paper that treated Norrath, the world of EverQuest, as a real economy and estimated its size

Currency and items in a game follow the rules of money: there is supply, demand, inflation and collapse, so they have to be designed as a real economy.

ExampleIn Diablo II gold quickly lost its value, and players traded in rare rings as currency instead.

Predict first, then see. Imagine the simplest rule: money makes money, so the stock grows by 2% each day, while a fixed 50 is spent each day. Work it out: when the stock is exactly 2500, the 50 it gains equals the 50 spent, and the water level doesn’t budge at all. That is the critical point. Now take two players, one starting at 2400 and one at 2600, only 8% apart. What happens after 200 days?

The key insight: the sink must follow the water level

As soon as the faucet is tied to the stock, a knife-edge critical line appears in the economy: just above it the stock snowballs, and just below it the stock keeps shrinking. A fixed-size sink can’t stop this growth, because the sink doesn’t get bigger when the water level gets higher. For the economy to be stable, the sink must be tied to the stock: the richer you are, the more is taken back. Auction-house cuts, taxes charged as a share of assets, and repair costs that rise with gear level all follow this idea.

Sources: Castronova, “Virtual Worlds: A First-Hand Account of Market and Society on the Cyberian Frontier” (2001 working paper, SSRN); the account of Diablo II comes from the player community and game wikis, not from an official economy report.
4

Why it worksWhy does a small difference snowball into a big one?

Behind the two players’ story above is the most basic pair of ideas in systems thinking. A snowball rolling downhill picks up snow more easily the bigger it gets, and so grows bigger still. The result feeds back and pushes the cause, and such a reinforcing loop exists in economies too: the more money, the more output, and so more money.

A thermostat in a room is the opposite: when the temperature rises the air conditioner cools it, and when it falls the air conditioner stops, so the result feeds back and holds the cause down. This is a balancing loop. The richer someone is, the more tax they pay, which is also a balancing loop, and it pulls the water level back.

To keep the water level in the plumbing steady, a balancing loop strong enough to match has to sit next to the reinforcing loop.

Stocks, flows and feedback loops

Split a system into “what is stored (stock)” and “how fast it flows in and out (flow),” then look at which flows are affected back by the stock. This is the basic skill of system dynamics. A reinforcing loop magnifies tiny differences exponentially, and a balancing loop pulls the system toward a target value. The tipping point above appears where the two forces exactly cancel: stock = fixed expense ÷ growth rate.

Sources: Jay Forrester, Industrial Dynamics (MIT Press, 1961); Donella Meadows, Thinking in Systems (Chelsea Green, 2008). The pipes in a game are a teaching application of them.

Once the sink is well designed, there is another problem: players hate it. Losing the same 100 gold feels much worse as a “repair fee” taken directly by the system than as “this quest gave 100 less.” Even when you have more gold than you can spend, being forced to pay still makes you frown.

This reaction, where “losing hurts more than gaining”, is loss aversion, much studied in psychology.

Loss aversion

Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky proposed in their 1979 prospect theory that people are more sensitive to losses than to gains. In 1992, from the choices of 25 graduate students, they estimated that the median weight of a loss is about 2.25 times that of an equal gain. The lesson for design is that the same sink feels far better when it is packaged as “spending money to get something” than as “being docked.” That is why shops, upgrades and fees work better than “fines.”

This conclusion is disputed, though. In 2018 David Gal and Derek Rucker argued that the existing evidence does not support “losses generally loom larger than gains.” Many phenomena credited to loss aversion can also be explained by other mechanisms such as inertia. In situations like “getting something and then having it taken away,” the pain is very noticeable. The figure 2.25 is not a constant.

Sources: Kahneman & Tversky, “Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk”, Econometrica 47(2), 1979; Tversky & Kahneman, “Advances in Prospect Theory”, Journal of Risk and Uncertainty 5(4), 1992; Gal & Rucker, “The Loss of Loss Aversion”, Journal of Consumer Psychology 28(3), 2018.

Why it worksWhy does the same reward feel like less each time?

The first time you clear a level and get 100 gold, you cheer. The 30th time you get the same 100, you barely react. Not one digit of the reward changed. What changed is your expectation.

This leads to a very practical trouble: the easiest way for a designer to please players is to make the reward bigger. But the players’ “baseline” rises with it, the next reward isn’t thrilling enough, so it is raised again, until the numbers run out of control.

Hedonic adaptation

People gradually adapt to a repeated stimulus and treat it as a new “baseline,” and from then on only the part above the baseline is felt as pleasure. This phenomenon is hedonic adaptation, vividly called the “hedonic treadmill”: however fast you run, happiness stays in the same place.

The chart below is a schematic model: the expectation baseline A moves 30% of the way toward the reward R each time, the experience = R − A (not below 0), and the first experience is set at 100%. Each kind of reward has its own baseline, and after a stretch of not touching it, the baseline falls back by 10% per time unit.

━ Gray: no design ━ Blue: gap widened to 5 (two consecutive rewards 5 time units apart; all other curves use 1) ━ Green: rotate 3 kinds of reward ┅ Red dashed: raise the stakes each time

Sources:
Brickman and Campbell · 1971 · introduced the “hedonic treadmill.”
Brickman, Coates and Janoff-Bulman · 1978 · compared lottery winners, accident victims left disabled and a control group · often cited, but with a small sample and no prior baseline, and the authors themselves called it preliminary.

Every way out in the figure is cheaper than “raising the stakes.” Space the rewards out so the baseline has time to fall back. Stagger the kinds of reward so each baseline gets a rest. And give a reward a new meaning, such as a one-of-a-kind title.

A favorite dish (daily life): on the first day it is amazing, after a week of it you are sick of it; after a month’s gap it tastes good again. The gap lets the baseline fall.
Daily login rewards (games): the same 100 gold is a surprise on day 1 and a “click-through chore” by day 30.
Chests and new gear (games): a newcomer cheers at the first piece of purple gear; late in the game, gear of the same quality is just salvage material.
“Not exciting enough, so make the number bigger”: the reward doubles each time, the gold columns grow taller, and the player’s face doesn’t change. The baseline rises every time, and that is how inflation comes about.
6

Why it worksExtension: why should numbers grow by percentages, and why do they blow up?

Swap a 100-damage sword for a 101-damage one and you hardly notice. Swap a 10-damage sword for an 11 and it feels “clearly stronger.” The same +1 feels very different. Light a candle in a dark room and it is suddenly bright. Light ten in broad daylight and you can’t tell the difference.

What people notice in “how much changed” is the ratio, not the difference. That is why many games write level-ups as “+10% per level” and not “+5 points per level.”

The Weber–Fechner law

The smallest difference a person can notice is roughly proportional to the original amount of the stimulus (Weber). So the subjective feeling is roughly proportional to the logarithm of the stimulus (Fechner). What a player feels as “how much stronger” depends on relative change.

Advanced: written as a formula

feeling ≈ k·ln(stimulus), where k is a constant. Each time the stimulus doubles, the feeling grows by a fixed amount.

Chart: start at 10, 30 levels, and both routes end at the same point (155). Blue: a fixed +5 per level. Red: a fixed +r% per level. The vertical axis is “how many % each level improves on the previous one.” The relative gain of linear leveling keeps getting smaller, so players feel it flatten out.

This is an influential empirical rule, and also disputed: Stevens (1957) argued that a power function fits many experiments better, and the “Weber fraction” also differs with sense and context.

Sources: Ernst Weber’s studies of weight discrimination in the 1830s–40s; Gustav Fechner, Elemente der Psychophysik (1860); S. S. Stevens (1957).

But growing by a percentage amounts to exponential growth. Everyone has heard the old riddle: the water lilies in a pond double their area every day and cover the whole pond on day 30, so when do they cover half of it? The answer is day 29. For the first 28 days you can barely see any change in the pond, and the last two days turn everything upside down.

“Experience bonuses that stack by percentages” and “gold output proportional to the number of buildings you own” in games have exactly this structure. Once the faucet is tied to the stock, the critical line from the last section appears.

Exponential growth and compounding

Linear growth adds a fixed amount each step. Exponential growth multiplies by a fixed factor each step, which is what “compound interest” means. A deposit at 7% a year roughly doubles in 10 years.

There is a rule of thumb called the “rule of 72”: the number of steps needed to double is about 72 ÷ the growth rate (in %).

Advanced: the exact doubling time

With a growth rate of r per step, steps to double = ln2 / ln(1+r). At r = 7% that is 10.2 steps, very close to 72 ÷ 7 ≈ 10.3.

Exponential growth is not the enemy: a growth system is naturally exponential in shape. What to do is give it a cap, a decay, or a sink tied to the stock.

Sources: exponential growth and compound interest are basic mathematics; the contrast between “population grows geometrically while food grows arithmetically” is widely known through Malthus’s An Essay on the Principle of Population (1798).

Why it worksExtension: why can an average lie?

In a bar are 99 ordinary people and 1 billionaire. Work out the “average wealth” and the number is startlingly high, yet not one ordinary person in the bar is anywhere near that average.

Spending in games is often like this: a few players bring in most of the revenue, and most spend nothing at all. Judging the experience of most players by “average spend per player” is like judging an ordinary person by the average wealth in a bar. A distribution with “a few huge and most tiny” is, mathematically, a power law.

How did people find out that “a few take the lion’s share”?

Economists at the end of the 19th century wanted to know how a nation’s wealth is spread across its people. The usual picture then was a bell curve: most people in the middle, very rich and very poor both rare, like height. If that were true, “average wealth” would stand for most people.

Guess first. Line up a society’s wealth from high to low. What share of total wealth do you think the richest 20% own? (If it were a bell-shaped distribution, about 35%–40%.)

Advanced: how the share is computed

In a Pareto distribution, the richest top fraction q of people hold a share q(α−1)/α of the total. For example, with α = 1.16 and q = 0.2, 0.20.138 ≈ 0.80, so the top 20% hold about 80%.

Below is a reasonably safe timeline. “80/20” is only a rule of thumb, and secondary sources word it differently: in practice it might be 70/30 or 90/10.

1896–1897 In his Cours d’économie politique, Vilfredo Pareto gathered income and property statistics from many countries and periods, and found that “the higher the income, the fewer the people” roughly follows a power function, not a bell curve. The usual retelling is “about 80% of the land in Italy was held by about 20% of the people,” but sources do not agree on where that specific figure comes from.
Around 1940 The quality-management scholar Joseph Juran, inspired by Pareto, proposed “the vital few and the useful many” for tracking down quality problems: a few kinds of defects cause most of the loss, so fix those first. Sources give two years, 1937 and 1941, for when he read Pareto. The names “Pareto principle” and “80/20 rule” became popular later.
2004–2006 In a 2004 article and a 2006 book, Chris Anderson used “the long tail” to describe the distribution in online retail where “hits are few and niche items are enormously many,” and the niche items together are also a big slice of business.

Power laws and the long tail

Most individuals are tiny and a very few are huge. The “80/20 rule” is its mnemonic and the “long tail” is another way of saying it. The warning for design is to look at the mean, the median and the quantiles together when reading data.

Advanced: the mean and median of a Pareto distribution

For a Pareto distribution (minimum value 1): mean = α/(α−1), median = 21/α. When α is close to 1, the mean is pulled very high by a very few large values and the sample mean is extremely unstable, while the median is much steadier.

This is a common empirical pattern, and the exact ratio varies with the game and the payment model. Some studies also point out that much data that “looks like a power law” is closer to a log-normal distribution. Height is bell-shaped, and the average does stand for the typical person.

Sources:
Pareto · 1896–97 · compiled income and property statistics from several countries · the higher the income, the fewer the people, roughly a power function (Cours d’économie politique).
Anderson · 2004, 2006 · used the “long tail” to describe internet retail (the Wired article “The Long Tail” and the book of the same name).
Clauset, Shalizi and Newman · 2009 · tested power laws in empirical data (“Power-law distributions in empirical data,” SIAM Review).

Why it worksExtension: why does a price “look like a good deal”?

At a buffet the first plate tastes best, the second is fine, and by the fourth you’re eating because you “don’t want to waste it.” With the same food, the first portion is the most useful and each later one less so. In economics this is diminishing marginal utility.

It explains why carrying three potions feels safe while carrying twenty won’t be used any more, and why a cap on the backpack is actually reasonable.

Diminishing marginal utility

There are counterexamples: when collecting a set, the last piece is the most valuable, since only a complete set earns the reward. Designers make use of this.

Sources: Daniel Bernoulli (1738) used logarithmic utility to explain the St. Petersburg paradox; in the 1870s the “marginal revolution” of Jevons, Menger and Walras made it a foundational concept of economics.

Once you know why a player stops at a certain quantity, the next question is how to lay out prices. The same ¥68 gift pack looks cheap next to a ¥648 pack and looks expensive next to a ¥6 pack.

Anchoring

When judging a number, people are pulled by the number they saw first, even when it has nothing to do with the question. This is the anchoring effect. A shop with the “original price crossed out, now ¥899,” or the first offer in a negotiation, relies on it.

Inventing an astronomical price almost nobody buys, just to raise the sense of a “good deal,” has gone beyond a pricing technique and become misleading.

Source: Tversky and Kahneman · 1974 · the “wheel of fortune” experiment · an irrelevant number also pulled people’s estimates (“Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases,” Science). Later studies mostly support the existence of anchoring, with the size of the effect varying by context and person.

Five rules of thumb for balance

  • Draw the relationship diagram first (who counters whom, where the money comes from and goes), then adjust the numbers.
  • Change only one or two knobs at a time and test before changing again, or you won’t know which one did the work.
  • When reading data, look at the mean, the median and the quantiles together.
  • Vary the rhythm and the kinds of reward instead of only raising them.
  • Give every faucet a sink that grows with the stock, and package the sink as “buying something,” not “confiscation.”

Common misconceptions

  • “A 50% win rate means balanced.” In a counter design the goal is equal win rates across options. Beyond that, look at how players actually spread across the options, and whether “the choice is meaningful.”
  • “Looking at the mean is enough”: under a power-law distribution the mean is pulled up by a few extreme values, and the median is what stands for the “typical player.”
  • “If a reward isn’t exciting enough, raise the number”: under hedonic adaptation, raising it only buys a brief freshness and also pushes up inflation.
  • “Exponential growth must be avoided.” Exponential growth is the natural form of a growth system. The key is to know where it goes out of bounds, and to pair it with a cap, decay or a sink.
  • “The 80/20 rule is a law.” It is a rule of thumb, and the ratio differs by field.

Reflection and debate

How thorough is hedonic adaptation? “People always return to their original level of happiness” is a very influential view. Later research found that adaptation differs across events and people, and some changes are long-lasting. The model in the chart is only a teaching simplification.

The analogy to virtual economies has limits. The “currency” in a game is shaped by designers who can change the rules at any time and by players who are not fully rational, so the conclusions of real-world economics can’t be applied directly. Castronova’s estimation method is itself disputed.

Is a sink “fleecing the players”? Packaging a sink as a product makes it easier on players. But if the packaging exists only so that people don’t notice money being taken, where is the line between that and taking it openly?

Key points

  • Balance does not mean “the same”; it means every choice has a reason to exist. Counters make the position of “strongest” disappear.
  • In an economy, check that faucets, buckets and sinks correspond; a reinforcing loop magnifies small differences, so a sink that grows with the stock is needed to balance it.
  • Loss aversion: the same amount hurts more when taken than when never received; a sink is best packaged as an exchange.
  • Hedonic adaptation: the same reward depreciates, and widening the gap and adding variety is cheaper than just raising it.
  • Players feel relative change, so use percentage bonuses; exponential growth, once tied to the stock, produces a critical line.
  • A power law makes the mean misleading, so look at the median too; “80/20” is only a rule of thumb.
Balance has to follow how people feel, and setting the numbers to “equal” is not enough. Players perceive in proportion, adapt, and fear loss more, while the loops and power-law distributions in an economy magnify everything.
