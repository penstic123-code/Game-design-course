Chapter 11

Level Design & Teaching

The best tutorial is one where the player never notices they’re in class.

Learning goals

about 45 min
  • Design a teaching level with “introduce → practice → vary,” and say why “teaching without text” was invented.
  • Explain the zone of proximal development and scaffolding, and judge whether a challenge is “just within reach.”
  • Use cognitive load and spaced repetition to plan “how many things to teach at once, and how long before they return,” and build a clearable teaching mini-level by hand.
1

What it isThe three-step method of teaching players

In the first level of Super Mario Bros. (1985), there is not one word on the screen. The player stands on flat ground, and something to the right comes ambling toward them. The player has no idea what it is, walks forward, touches it, and dies. Try again, press jump, land on its head, and it squashes flat.

From start to finish nobody said “press the key to jump, and stomping an enemy destroys it,” and the player has already learned it. This level is held up by many as a model teaching level.

Behind it is a very plain structure that can be summed up in three steps. First introduce something new in a safe place, letting the player meet it alone. Then practice it repeatedly with a little risk. Finally combine it with other things, or overturn the player’s expectation. The player’s experience: understanding it, using it, then mastering it.

Teaching principle: first let the player watch, then let the player do, and finally let the player create. Text is the last resort.
Teaching “stomping”: first place one slow monster on open ground (introduce), then a row of three (practice), and finally put the monster at a jump’s landing spot with moving platforms below (vary). Each step adds only a little.
Teaching a child to ride a bike: first ride on flat ground with two training wheels (introduce), take one off and ride in circles (practice), finally remove them, go up a little slope and dodge obstacles (vary).
A new mechanic debuts as a “family bucket.” Monsters at the landing spot, moving platforms, spikes and a countdown, all heaped together. The player can’t tell which new thing killed them and only feels “this game is hard,” and all they learn is frustration.
2

What it isPacing and “is the risk worth it?”: tension and release, and risk and reward

Teaching is only half of a level. The other half is getting the player all the way through without tiring or boredom: tension and release, and something to choose.

On pacing, a level that stays tense the whole way is more than players can bear. Chapter 6 covered flow: when the challenge far exceeds ability, the result is anxiety. A level that is flat the whole way does not work either, because the player has no decisions to make and is left only with boredom. A good level breathes: a stretch of tension, a rest, another stretch of tension, and each one a little higher than the last.

Risk and reward also need to be paired. Put a coin next to a spike: take it, or not? That is how a choice arises. To get the reward one has to take a risk, and whether to take it is up to the player.

A coin placed beside a trap: take it and there is a reward, but you might fall in. The player has to weigh their own skill, and so a choice appears.
Interval training: sprint for 30 seconds, walk for 60, then go again. Sprinting all the way just makes people quit midway. Put a safe camp after a tough battle in a level, and the next fight feels even tenser.
High pressure all the way, not one rest point: players tire and perform worse, and even the best design is worn out. Conversely, a level that is flat all the way and has no risk leaves the player with no decisions to make either.
3

How it was inventedHow “wordless teaching” and “spaced review” were thought up

Story one: nobody reads the manual, so how do you teach players?

In 1980s console games, how to play was generally written in the manual that came in the box. But players who got a cartridge mostly just plugged it in and played. Arcades were blunter still: once you put in the coin you had to play, and nobody wanted to read a page of words first. The designer faced a plain problem: if the player doesn’t read text, how can they learn to jump, stomp, bump and dodge within a few dozen seconds?

The most direct way is to open with a screen of text, or lay hints over the picture. But players skip it with a keypress. Even if they read it, they only “know,” and the hands don’t yet “can.”

So there was a change of mind: let the level itself be the teacher. Put the new thing in a safe place and let the player run into it, try it, and see the consequence at once.

According to media reports of the September 2015 Eurogamer video with Nintendo’s Shigeru Miyamoto and Takashi Tezuka, 1-1 was designed to “teach the player how to play.” The first enemy walks slowly toward you, and touching it kills you, which forces the player to work out that they need to jump.

The Super Mushroom looks like an enemy, and newcomers often try to jump over it. But the low ceiling overhead leaves the player able only to bump the bricks, and after watching it emerge from inside, they understand it is something good.

Wikipedia’s “World 1-1” entry (citing Eurogamer’s report) mentions that in 1-1 some pits have ground beneath them, so you can fall in and climb back out.

Since then “introduce in a safe environment, then add risk, finally combine and vary” has become the common practice of level teaching. Education scholars have also drawn similar learning principles from games.

Story two: why are learned skills “returned to the teacher” after a few days?

Nineteenth-century psychologists wanted to know: can memory be measured like a physical quantity? But everyday memory is too messy: memory of a song is mixed with emotion, associations and how many times it has been heard, and can’t be compared.

Memorizing poems or texts doesn’t work either: everyone’s familiarity with the content differs, so the results can’t be compared.

Hermann Ebbinghaus came up with a way: make a batch of meaningless syllables (such as “DAX” and “BOK”) that nobody knows, so nobody has an advantage. Using himself as the subject, he memorized a list, relearned it after different delays, and used “how much time relearning saved compared to the first time” (the savings method) to measure how much memory remained.

He put his results in the 1885 Über das Gedächtnis (On Memory). The conclusion: forgetting is fast at first, then slow, dropping steepest just after learning and flattening ever more afterward. In 2015 Murre and Dros redid the experiment with a similar method, and the results resembled Ebbinghaus’s original data. They also pointed out that the curve may have a small rise near 24 hours.

Since forgetting is fast, the remedy is to review just before it is forgotten. Cognitive psychology has repeatedly found the “spacing effect”: spreading review over several points in time sticks better than repeating all at once. In a level, this means a mechanic that has been taught should reappear in a new form every few levels.

Why it worksPut it in a safe place first: keep the cost of failure small

Think back to the little pits in 1-1 that have a floor. Fall in, lose nothing, climb out and jump again. When something new appears for the first time, letting the player afford to fail is the precondition for teaching to work at all.

Remember the kitten pouncing on yarn? Missing it doesn’t mean going hungry. The young spend much time on “useless” roughhousing, and one explanation is that play lets them rehearse the skills they’ll need later, where the cost of failure is very low.

Low-cost trial and error: the other face of play

In a 1972 paper the American psychologist Jerome Bruner argued that childhood is so long partly to give the young time to try out combinations without serious consequences, and play is such a “testing ground.”

The 2001 review by Špinka and colleagues mentioned earlier also stresses that animals at play deliberately handicap themselves and create small surprises on purpose, as if training flexible responses to the unexpected.

Applied to levels, the meaning is plain: the cheaper failure is, the more the player dares to try and the faster they learn. The cost includes whether the retry button is visible, whether you walk a long stretch again, and whether you lose things. Press the cost of the first few failures to the lowest, and raise it once the player has the skill.

Whether play really has such a function is still debated among scholars. Here only the point that “low-cost trial and error helps learning” is borrowed.

One hit kills on first touching a new hazard, then a 30-second respawn animation: the player never gets a chance to “just try it,” every trial and error costs a fortune, so they tread timidly and learn slower.
5

Why it worksJust within reach: the zone of proximal development and scaffolding

Teaching a friend who has never played games, you wouldn’t make them fight a boss at once. You would let them first wander freely on an empty field, then place one slow enemy, then two… all the time doing one thing: making every step “a little more” than what they can do now.

Too easy and they get bored, too hard and they give up. What teaches the most is the stretch in the middle.

The zone of proximal development (ZPD), scaffolding, and “learning by doing”

The Soviet psychologist Lev Vygotsky sorted tasks into three kinds: those inside the zone of proximal development (doable with a little help), those already mastered, and those far beyond ability. Learning happens mainly in the first kind. Later, Wood, Bruner and Ross (1976) used “scaffolding” for help that is temporary and gets taken away. In games, a safe arena, slowed enemies and highlighted hints are all scaffolding.

Seymour Papert stressed learning in the course of making and playing with your hands. James Gee summed up a set of “learning principles of good games,” such as “well-ordered problems” (early problems lay the groundwork for later ones) and “pleasantly frustrating” (hard, but feeling doable). The mainstream view in educational psychology: for beginners, doing it yourself with guidance is usually better than mere listening.

“Introduce → practice → vary” can be seen as the game version of scaffolding: the scaffold is thickest at the introduction (safe, in isolation), is withdrawn bit by bit during practice, and is almost gone at the variation.

6

Why it worksTeach one at a time: cognitive load

Take a few phone calls at once, remember a few new names, and solve a problem, and the head turns to mush. In a game tutorial that pops up “jump, dash, grapple, inventory, crafting” all at once, the player’s reaction is: can’t remember any of it, then closes the window.

Chapter 10 explained that too much information on an interface leaves people at a loss. Teaching has the same problem: the more new things appear at once, the less room the player’s head has for them.

Cognitive load theory

The amount of information a person can hold and work on in the head at the same time while doing something is very limited, and this temporary “holding area” is called working memory in psychology. Miller (1956) stressed that people can hold about 7±2 “chunks,” while Cowan (2001) argued that once chunking and similar factors are excluded the figure is closer to about 4 chunks. The exact number is disputed; that it is “very limited” is agreed.

John Sweller’s 1988 paper pointed out that the usual way of solving problems takes up a lot of working memory, leaving little spare for “learning.” It later grew into cognitive load theory: the more new things introduced at once, and the more complex each is, the higher the total load and the worse the learning.

There is good news too: skilled people pack several operations into one chunk. A veteran has long seen “move + jump” as one thing, so they can layer new things on faster.

Sources: Miller (1956), Psychological Review 63(2), 81–97; Cowan (2001), Behavioral and Brain Sciences 24(1), 87–114; Sweller (1988), Cognitive Science 12(2), 257–285. One room, one thing: a teaching level that is often praised has each of its first few rooms introduce a single mechanic (like the start of Portal), layering the next once it is learned.
Teaching an elder to use a smartphone: today teach only “making a call,” next time “sending a picture.” Explain ten functions in one go and all you get is “I can’t remember.”
A near-miss: “newcomers can be taught only one at a time” does not mean “veterans too.” Players familiar with similar games have packed “move + jump” into one chunk and can layer new things faster.
7

Why it worksCome back after a while: spaced repetition and deliberate practice

The “dash jump” learned in level 1 is not used for the next ten levels, and when level 11 asks for it, the hands have gone rusty. Turn it around: if it comes back in a new form every few levels, it stays in memory.

This matches the forgetting curve above: forgetting is fast first and then slow, so the earlier the first review comes, the better the deal.

The forgetting curve, the spacing effect and deliberate practice

Ebbinghaus (1885) found that memory without review decays quickly at first and then more slowly. A fairly solid finding in cognitive psychology is the spacing effect. Cepeda and colleagues’ 2006 meta-analysis pooled 184 papers and 317 experiments and found spaced review beats massed review. They also found that the “best gap” gets longer as the time you need to remember gets longer.

Advanced: how the forgetting curve is written as a formula

It is often simplified to the exponential curve R = e^(−t/S). R is how much is remembered, t is the time that has passed, and S is “stability”; the larger S is, the slower the forgetting. For example, with S = 3 days, after 3 days R = e^(−1) ≈ 37%. Exponential decay is a rough approximation of real forgetting.

Ericsson and colleagues proposed deliberate practice in 1993 after studying how experts develop. It means practicing at a difficulty slightly above the current level, with feedback, and not merely repeating, which echoes the zone of proximal development of the previous section.

“An old mechanic returns after a gap” is spaced repetition built into levels: level 2 teaches the dash jump, level 4 simply reuses it, level 8 combines it with moving platforms, level 15 makes it one link of a boss fight…

Common misconceptions

  • “Teaching means popping up text instructions.” Text is often the last resort; “demonstrating” with terrain, layout and safe trial and error is usually more effective.
  • “The harder, the more you learn.” Too hard triggers giving up, and learning relies on “just within reach,” not “the hardest.”
  • Tidy percentages like “listening retains 10%, learning by doing retains 90%.” The “learning pyramid” figures circulating online lack a reliable source and should not be taken seriously. What can be said reliably is that “active participation is usually better than passive listening.”
  • “Spaced repetition = mechanical repetition again and again.” The point is spacing the time and often using it with a twist, not repeating the same thing ten times.
  • “Being able to afford failure means no penalty.” The cost rises along with skill: small at first, higher once learned, or the challenge has no weight.

Reflection and debate

The zone of proximal development is a qualitative concept, and it is hard to measure exactly “how far off counts as within reach.” The word “scaffolding” comes from a 1976 paper by Wood and colleagues and was only later linked to the ZPD, and some scholars criticize this merging as too casual.

Kirschner, Sweller and Clark (2006) argued that for beginners, exploratory teaching with almost no guidance is worse than explicit instruction. This is their view, and it has drawn rebuttals. The three-step method here belongs to “guided exploration.” Gee’s learning principles come from analysis and observation of games, more a suggestive theory than the conclusion of a strict controlled experiment.

Research on deliberate practice is disputed too: in 2019 Macnamara and Maitra tried to replicate the 1993 violinist study and found the explanatory power of practice much smaller than the original reported. Figures such as cognitive load’s “4 chunks” are likewise disputed, and players’ experience varies widely.

Much of what is said about the “design intent” of Mario 1-1 comes from media retellings and later interpretations, with no design documents from the time to check against. If a player simply loves “being thrown into the deep end,” should a tutorial level still look after them?

Key points

  • The three teaching steps: introduce (safe) → practice (with risk) → vary (combine / reverse).
  • Why it was invented: players don’t read manuals, so let the level itself be the teacher (teaching without text). People also forget quickly, so bring an old mechanic back after a while with a new twist.
  • First make sure it can be cleared, then tune the difficulty; place rewards and risk; give the level rhythm (tense → rest → tenser).
  • Why it works: put new things first where failure is cheap; keep the challenge in the “zone of proximal development”; teach one at a time to avoid cognitive overload; and bring old mechanics back after a while to fight forgetting.
A good teaching level: introduce where the cost of failure is very small, make every step a little more than the player can do now, add one new thing at a time, and bring the old skill back after a spell.
