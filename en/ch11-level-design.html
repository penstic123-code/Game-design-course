Chapter 11

Level Design & Teaching

The best tutorial is one where the player never notices they’re in class.

Learning goals

about 45 min
  • Design a teaching level with “introduce → practice → vary,” and say why “wordless teaching” had to be invented.
  • Explain the zone of proximal development and scaffolding, and judge whether a challenge is “just within reach.”
  • Use cognitive load and spaced repetition to plan “how many to teach at once, and how long before they come back,” and build a playable teaching mini-level by hand.
1

What it isThe three-step method of teaching players

In the first level of Super Mario Bros. (1985) there is not a single word on the screen. You stand on flat ground, and something on the right is ambling toward you. You don’t know what it is, so you probably walk forward, bump into it, and die. You try again, press jump, land on its head, and it flattens.

From start to finish nobody told you “press the button to jump, and stomping an enemy defeats it,” yet you have learned it. Many people regard this level as the model of a teaching level.

Behind it is a very plain structure that can be summed up in three steps: first introduce the new thing in a safe place and let the player meet it alone; then practice it again and again with a little risk; finally combine it with other things, or reverse the player’s expectation. The player’s experience is: see it, use it, master it.

Teaching principle: first let the player watch, then let the player do, and finally let the player create. Text is the last resort.
Teaching “stomping”: first place one slow monster on open ground (introduce), then a row of three (practice), and finally put the monster at a jump’s landing spot with moving platforms below (vary). Each step adds only a little.
Teaching a child to ride a bike: first ride on flat ground with two training wheels (introduce), take one off and ride in circles (practice), finally remove them, go up a little slope and dodge obstacles (vary).
A new mechanic debuts as a “family bucket.” Monsters at the landing spot, moving platforms, spikes and a countdown, all heaped together. The player can’t tell which new thing killed them and only feels “this game is hard,” and all they learn is frustration.
2

What it isPacing and “is the risk worth it?”: tension and release, and risk and reward

Teaching is only half of a level. The other half is getting the player all the way through without tiring or boredom: tension and release, and something to choose.

Pacing first. A level that is tense all the way, the player can’t sustain; remember flow? A challenge far above ability is anxiety. A level that is flat all the way won’t do either: the player has no decisions to make, and boredom is all that is left. A good level is like breathing: tense for a while, a breath, tense again, and each time a little higher than the last.

Now risk and reward. Put a coin next to some spikes: take it or not? That is how a choice comes into being. Wanting the reward means taking a risk, and whether to risk it is up to the player.

A coin placed beside a trap: take it and there is a reward, but you might fall in. The player has to weigh their own skill, and so a choice appears.
Interval training: sprint for 30 seconds, walk for 60, then go again. Sprinting all the way just makes people quit midway. Put a safe camp after a tough battle in a level, and the next fight feels even tenser.
High pressure all the way, not one rest point: players tire and perform worse, and even the best design is worn out. Conversely, a level that is flat all the way and has no risk leaves the player with no decisions to make either.
3

How it was inventedHow “wordless teaching” and “spaced review” were thought up

Story one: nobody reads the manual, so how do you teach players?

Home-console games in the 1980s usually described how to play in the manual in the box. But players who got a cartridge mostly just plugged it in and played; arcade games were blunter still, and once you put in a coin you had to get going, with nobody wanting to read a page of text first. Designers had a plain problem: if players don’t read text, how can they learn to jump, stomp, bump and dodge in a few dozen seconds?

The most direct method was to open with a screen of text, or overlay hints on the picture. But players skipped it with one button press; and even if they read it, they only “knew,” and the hands hadn’t “learned.”

So there was a change of mind: let the level itself be the teacher. Put the new thing in a safe place and let the player run into it, try it, and see the consequence at once.

According to media reports of a 2015 talk between Nintendo’s Shigeru Miyamoto and Takashi Tezuka, 1-1 was designed just this way: the first enemy walks slowly toward you, and bumping into it kills you, forcing you to see that you must jump; the Super Mushroom looks like an enemy, but the low ceiling overhead leaves you only able to hit the bricks, and watching it come out from inside tells you it is a good thing; there is ground under the first pit, so you can fall in and climb back out, while the second is truly bottomless, and Tezuka said that newcomers mostly fall into the first one. (These are retellings, and I couldn’t check the original video directly, so go by the original interview.)

Since then “introduce in a safe environment first, then add risk, then combine and vary” has become the common approach to level teaching. Later, education scholars also drew similar learning principles from games, as the “Why it works” sections below explain.

Story two: why are learned skills “returned to the teacher” after a few days?

Nineteenth-century psychologists wanted to know: can memory be measured like a physical quantity? But everyday memory is too messy: your memory of a song is mixed with emotions, associations and the number of times you’ve heard it before, so it can’t be compared.

Memorizing poems or texts doesn’t work either: everyone’s familiarity with the content differs, so the results can’t be compared.

Hermann Ebbinghaus came up with a way: make a batch of meaningless syllables (such as “DAX” and “BOK”) that nobody knows, so nobody has an advantage. Using himself as the subject, he memorized a list, relearned it after different delays, and used “how much time relearning saved compared to the first time” (the savings method) to measure how much memory remained.

He wrote up his results in Über das Gedächtnis (Memory) in 1885. The conclusion: forgetting goes fast first, then slow, with the steepest drop right after learning and an ever flatter curve afterward. In 2015 Murre and Dros redid the experiment with a similar method, and the results resembled Ebbinghaus’s original data; they also noted that the curve may have a small rebound near 24 hours.

Since forgetting is fast, the remedy is to review before it is forgotten. Cognitive psychology has since found the “spacing effect” again and again: spreading review over multiple points in time sticks better than repeating all at once. For a level designer, translated: a mechanic that has been taught should come back a few levels later in a new guise.

Why it worksPut it in a safe place first: keep the cost of failure small

Think back to that small pit in 1-1 with a floor. Fall in, and you lose nothing: just climb out and jump again. This is no coincidence: when a new thing first appears, letting the player afford to fail is the precondition for teaching to work at all.

Remember the kitten pouncing on the ball of yarn? Missing doesn’t mean going hungry. One explanation for why the young spend so much time on “useless” roughhousing is that play lets them rehearse, where the cost of failing is very low, the skills they will need later.

A level’s first puzzle is in fact the player’s first “playground.”

Low-cost trial and error: the other face of play

The American psychologist Jerome Bruner argued in a 1972 paper that one reason childhood is so long is to give the young time to try out all kinds of combinations without serious consequences, and play is just such a “test plot.” The 2001 review by Špinka and colleagues mentioned earlier also stresses that animals at play often handicap themselves on purpose and create small mishaps, as if training a flexible response to the unexpected.

Applied to levels, the meaning is plain: the lower the cost of failing, the more the player dares to try, and the faster they learn. The cost includes whether the retry button is visible, whether you must walk a long way again, and whether you lose things. Keep the cost of the first few failures as low as possible, and raise it once the player has the skill.

To be clear, whether play really has this function is still debated in the field; what is borrowed here is the relatively safe part, that “low-cost trial and error helps learning.”

Sources: Jerome S. Bruner, “The nature and uses of immaturity,” American Psychologist 27(8) (1972); Špinka, Newberry & Bekoff, “Mammalian play: training for the unexpected,” The Quarterly Review of Biology 76(2): 141–168 (2001); for the level practice, see the retelling of 1-1 above.
The first pit in 1-1 has a floor: falling in just means climbing back. Only once you can jump does a truly bottomless pit appear. The penalty rises along with the skill.
A flight simulator: pilots practice emergency landings in the simulator, and a mistake just blacks out the screen. The truly deadly things are first practiced in a place where nobody dies.
One hit kills on first touching a new hazard, then a 30-second respawn animation: the player never gets a chance to “just try it,” every trial and error costs a fortune, so they tread timidly and learn slower.

Why it worksJust within reach: the zone of proximal development and scaffolding

Teaching a friend who has never played games, you wouldn’t make them fight a boss right away. You’d let them wander around open ground first, then add a slow-moving enemy, then two… You’ve actually been doing the same thing the whole time: making each step “a little more” than they can do now.

Too easy and they get bored, too hard and they give up. What teaches the most is the stretch in the middle.

Psychology gave this stretch a name long ago.

The zone of proximal development (ZPD), scaffolding, and “learning by doing”

The Soviet psychologist Lev Vygotsky split tasks into three kinds: those in the zone of proximal development (doable with a little help), those already mastered, and those far beyond ability. Learning mainly happens in the first. Later, Wood, Bruner and Ross (1976) used “scaffolding” for temporary help that gets taken away. In games it is a safe field, a slowed-down enemy, a highlighted hint.

Seymour Papert stressed learning in the process of doing and playing with your hands; James Gee summed up a set of “learning principles of good games,” such as “well-ordered problems” (early problems lay the groundwork for later ones) and “pleasantly frustrating” (hard, but feeling doable). The mainstream view in educational psychology is that, for beginners, doing it themselves under guidance is usually better than just listening.

“Introduce → practice → vary” is the game version of scaffolding: the scaffolding is thickest at introduction (safe, alone), slowly comes down during practice, and is almost gone at variation.

6

Why it worksTeach one at a time: cognitive load

Take several phone calls at once, memorize several new names, and solve a problem all at the same time, and most likely everything gets muddled. When a game tutorial pops up “jump, dash, grapple, inventory, crafting” all at once, the player’s reaction is often: can’t remember any of it, and closes it.

Remember how too much information on an interface leaves people at a loss? Teaching has the same flaw: the more new things appear at once, the less the player’s head can hold.

Cognitive load theory

The amount of information a person can hold and process in their head at once is very limited, and this “temporary store” is called working memory. Miller (1956) stressed that what people can hold is about 7±2 “chunks,” and Cowan (2001) argued that, with factors like chunking excluded, it is closer to about 4 chunks. The exact figure is disputed, while “very limited” is the consensus.

John Sweller’s 1988 paper pointed out that conventional problem solving uses up a lot of working memory, leaving little to spare for “learning,” and this later grew into cognitive load theory: the more new things are introduced at once and the more complex each is, the higher the total load, and the worse the learning.

Conversely there is good news: skilled people pack several operations into one chunk. A veteran has long seen “move + jump” as one thing, so they can layer new things on faster.

Sources: Miller (1956), Psychological Review 63(2), 81–97; Cowan (2001), Behavioral and Brain Sciences 24(1), 87–114; Sweller (1988), Cognitive Science 12(2), 257–285. The curves in the “learning curve lab” later are a sketch model made up for this course to show the shape, not data from these papers.
One room, one thing: a teaching level that is often praised has each of its first few rooms introduce a single mechanic (like the start of Portal), layering the next once it is learned.
Teaching an elder to use a smartphone: today teach only “making a call,” next time “sending a picture.” Explain ten functions in one go and all you get is “I can’t remember.”
A near-miss: “newcomers can be taught only one at a time” does not mean “veterans too.” Players familiar with similar games have packed “move + jump” into one chunk and can layer new things faster.

Why it worksCome back after a while: spaced repetition and deliberate practice

You learned the “dash jump” in level one, didn’t use it for the next ten levels, and in level eleven you’re asked to use it and your hands have probably gone rusty. Conversely, if every few levels it appears in a new guise, you keep remembering it.

This matches the forgetting curve described earlier: forgetting is fast first and slow later, so the sooner the first review comes, the better the return.

The forgetting curve, the spacing effect and deliberate practice

Ebbinghaus (1885) found that without review, memory decays fast first and slow later, often simplified to the exponential curve R = e^(−t/S) (R is how much is remembered, and S is “stability,” the larger the slower the forgetting). A fairly solid finding in cognitive psychology is the spacing effect: the 2006 meta-analysis by Cepeda and colleagues pooled 184 articles and 317 experiments and found spaced review beats massed review, with the “best interval” growing longer as the time you need to remember grows.

In 1993, Ericsson and colleagues, studying the development of experts, proposed deliberate practice: not mere repetition, but practice slightly above the current level and with feedback, which echoes the zone of proximal development in the previous section.

“An old mechanic comes back after a while” puts spaced repetition into a level: learn the dash jump in level 2, reuse it simply in level 3, add a twist in level 6, and bring it back as a combination in level 12…

Common misconceptions

  • “Teaching means popping up text instructions.” Text is often the last resort; “demonstrating” with terrain, layout and safe trial and error is usually more effective.
  • “The harder, the more you learn.” Too hard triggers giving up; learning depends on “just within reach,” not on “the hardest.”
  • Tidy percentages like “lecture keeps 10%, learning by doing keeps 90%.” The “learning pyramid” numbers going around online have no reliable source and shouldn’t be taken at face value; all that can be said is that “active participation is usually better than passive listening.”
  • “Spaced repetition = mindless repeated repetition.” The key is spacing in time and often using it again with a twist, not repeating one thing ten times.
  • “Being able to afford failure means no penalty.” The cost rises along with skill: small at first, higher once learned, or the challenge has no weight.

Reflection and debate

The zone of proximal development is a qualitative concept, and it is hard to measure precisely “how far counts as within reach”; the term “scaffolding” comes from the 1976 paper by Wood et al. and was only later linked to the ZPD, and some scholars criticize this merging as too casual.

Kirschner, Sweller and Clark (2006) argued that for novices, nearly unguided discovery teaching is worse than explicit instruction. This is their view, and it drew rebuttals too; the three-step method here counts as “guided exploration.” Gee’s learning principles come from analysis and observation of games and are more like a thought-provoking theory than the conclusion of a strict controlled experiment.

Research on deliberate practice is disputed too: in 2019 Macnamara and Maitra tried to replicate the 1993 violinist study and found the explanatory power of practice much smaller than the original reported. Figures such as cognitive load’s “4 chunks” are likewise disputed, and players’ experience varies widely.

Many accounts of the “design intent” of Mario 1-1 come from media retellings and later interpretation, and don’t necessarily equal the design documents of the time. What do you think: if a player just likes “being thrown into the deep end,” should a teaching level still look after them?

Key points

  • The three teaching steps: introduce (safe) → practice (with risk) → vary (combine / reverse).
  • Why they were invented: players don’t read manuals, so the level itself becomes the teacher (wordless teaching); and people forget quickly, so old mechanics are brought back after a while in a new guise.
  • First make sure it can be cleared, then tune the difficulty; place rewards and risk; give the level rhythm (tense → rest → tenser).
  • Why it works: put new things first where failure is cheap; keep the challenge in the “zone of proximal development”; teach one at a time to avoid cognitive overload; and bring old mechanics back after a while to fight forgetting.
A good teaching level = introduce first where failure costs very little, make every step a little more than the player can do now, add only one new thing at a time, and bring the old skills back on stage now and then.
