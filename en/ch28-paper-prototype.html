Chapter 28 · Hands-On

The Paper Prototype Workshop

A sheet of paper, a die and a few cards, and tonight you can be playing your own game.

⏱ About 60 minutes (hands-on included)

Learning goals

  • Explain where paper prototypes came from and what problem they solve, and use a 6-step process to make a paper prototype you can play tonight.
  • Use a component list, card and die-face templates and a playtest log to turn “I feel” into “I recorded.”
  • Explain why paper prototypes work (including the evolutionary function of play and the “curse of knowledge”), and know what they cannot verify.
1

What it isDon’t write code yet: six steps make one lap

Say you have an idea: on a 5×5 map, players dodge a cat to grab cheese. On Friday evening you eagerly get started and spend three evenings drawing beautiful illustrations for 30 cards, and even laminate them.

On Sunday you invite a friend to play, and before the first game is over, your friend asks: “After I draw this card, can I use it right away?” The designer is stunned, because the rules never said. The card-drawing rule doesn’t hold up, and 30 beautiful cards are already printed.

Three evenings of drawing, voided by one question in a minute. Had you written the card names in pencil on sticky notes, tearing one up and rewriting it would take ten seconds. A version made from the cheapest materials like this is a paper prototype, and what it does is find out as fast as possible whether “this play is any fun.”

Concept card

Paper Prototype

Paper prototype

Use the cheapest materials, such as paper, cards and dice, to make a version that can be played on the spot, first answering “is this play any fun,” and only then deciding whether to write code.

ExampleThirty card names written on sticky notes: the first game shows that “can a drawn card be used at once” was never settled, and tearing one up to rewrite it takes ten seconds.

The method is simple. Six steps make one lap, and after the changes you go around again:

🎯

One-sentence play

“On a 5×5 map, dodge your opponent and be first to take 3 pieces of cheese.” If you can’t say it, you haven’t thought it through yet.

🧑‍🎮

Play it yourself first

Playing alone already catches quite a few holes: rules that contradict each other, or a step that can’t be carried out.

📝

It only counts if it’s written down

Memory flatters playtests. Recording in a table is how you see the problems that keep coming back.

2

What it isFidelity: rough enough to “just answer the question”

“Paper” is only one material; the yardstick for a prototype is fidelity. The more it looks like the finished thing, the more it can verify, but every change also costs more. The chart below lays out that exchange:

Concept card

Prototype Fidelity

Prototype fidelity (lo-fi / hi-fi)

Marc Rettig · Prototyping for Tiny Fingers (1994) argued for making low-fidelity (lo-fi) prototypes first, since high-fidelity (hi-fi) ones are slow to make and hard to change (whether he coined “lo-fi” could not be confirmed)

How closely a prototype resembles the final product. The closer it is, the more it can verify, and the more it costs to make and to change a version.

ExampleTo know whether the rules hold up, paper is enough; to know whether jumping feels good, paper cannot answer, and you need a running grey-box digital version.

So the rule of thumb is: use the lowest level that can just answer the question. To know whether the rules hold up, paper is enough. To work out numbers and economy, one spreadsheet is enough: lay the numbers out in rows and columns, and changing one cell shows the consequences. To know whether jumping feels good, you have to move to the right. Of the five examples below, the first three are the right use and the last two are mismatches:

The Alpha playtest version of Magic: The Gathering: the 120 cards mentioned in the earlier discussion of iteration were a handmade paper prototype. What needed checking was “is drawing cards and dueling fun,” and paper cards were enough; changing a card took one pen.
The paper iteration of Armello: digital games start from paper too. As said earlier, its team iterated on paper for several months, with “hosts” working out the hidden rules for the computer. What the digital version would have to write, human brains carried first.
A cardboard sofa when renovating: cut cardboard to the size of the sofa, set it down on the floor plan, and see how wide the aisle is left. If it doesn’t fit, move it, at no cost. The principle is the same as a game prototype.
Counterexample: drawing beautiful card faces first (hypothetical): spend three evenings illustrating and laminating 30 cards, and only at the first playtest find the draw rule doesn’t work. The art is wasted, and you can’t bear to change the rules. A paper prototype should be so rough that “tearing it up doesn’t hurt.”
Counterexample: using paper to check jump feel: paper has no real-time hand-eye coordination, so a question like “does the jump feel good” can’t be answered by paper; get a running digital prototype as soon as possible. With the wrong tool, no amount of roughness helps.

Invention storyWhy did people start “with paper first”?

First experience for yourself the trouble it solves. Suppose you have 6 hours to validate a new game, and every step uses up time. Click the buttons to spend time and see how many rounds of “change rules → playtest” you can finish. (The times are illustrative numbers meant to compare orders of magnitude, not measured.)

Think: with the same 6 hours, where does spending all the time teach you the most about whether “this play works”? (“Rule problems found” counts 1 per playtest round, also illustrative.)

Try it: click only “change rules on paper” until the 6 hours are used up, and count how many rounds you played. Then restart, mix clicks of “change rules in code” and “draw card faces,” and compare the number of rounds.

From the board-game table to the phone screen, the same trouble

Making a game or piece of software that runs is expensive, and the two questions you most want answered, “are the rules fun” and “can people read the interface,” can’t be answered before it is made. If you find out it’s wrong after finishing, a change costs the most.

The earliest method came from board games, which were always paper, cards and dice; people who design them naturally use handmade prototypes. Magic: The Gathering is a case: according to Garfield, the 1991 Alpha was handmade cards, friends’ playtests pushed changes round after round, and the game was released in 1993.

Software interface design arrived at the same method by another path, the “low-fidelity prototype.” In April 1994, Marc Rettig published “Prototyping for Tiny Fingers” in Communications of the ACM. The article describes how to make interface prototypes out of paper for real users to try, which the author called lo-fi prototypes. According to the abstract, the article also suggested assigning roles during testing: a greeter, a facilitator, someone playing the “computer,” and an observer who takes notes. In 2003 Carolyn Snyder published the book Paper Prototyping, which systematized the approach.

Prototypes in physical form have a widely told story too. It is reported that Palm’s Jeff Hawkins once whittled a piece of wood to the size of the handheld device he imagined, carried it in his shirt pocket, and “pretended” to take notes on it with a pen to get a feel for holding it. Donna Dubinsky recalled that he would pretend to take notes this way in meetings. How long he carried it is reported differently by different sources (from a week to several months). The Computer History Museum (CHM) holds a Palm prototype from about 1995.

The same thought lies behind all of these: what has to be checked is the idea, not the quality of the making. Strip away everything that doesn’t affect “rules and choices,” and changing a rule takes one pen and one minute, so limited time buys more rounds of playtesting. Digital game teams later borrowed this too, as with Armello mentioned earlier.

4

Why it holdsWhy does a “rough” prototype work better?

Roughness makes changes cheap, and puts the judgment on the rules

  • Cheap trial and error: paper can be torn, scribbled on and rewritten, a new version costs almost nothing, so people dare to change a lot and are more willing to throw things out. This is of a piece with the iteration logic of “build one, playtest, change.”
  • Physical feel lowers the barrier to expressing ideas: people who can’t program or draw can still make it. Cards are held in the hand and tokens moved directly, so others can join at a glance and propose on the spot “how about changing it this way?”
  • Attention goes to rules, not art: a prototype is obviously a draft, so playtesters naturally comment on “are the rules clear, is it fun,” not on whether the colors look nice. The designer, too, is less likely to hesitate to change something because it looks polished. This is the common thinking behind a low-fidelity prototype.

The boundary: paper suits checking rules and choices, and can’t check control feel, visual presentation or real-time rhythm; those have to be tried in a digital prototype.

Source of the principle:
Marc Rettig · 1994 and Carolyn Snyder · 2003 · represent the low-fidelity prototype approach, which follows the idea of iteration.
“A rough prototype focuses feedback on the design itself” is a common claim in this field; no quantitative experimental data was found, so no numbers are cited.

Why it holdsTwo more basic ideas: play, and the “curse of knowledge”

Remember kittens pouncing on balls of yarn and lion cubs wrestling? Play looks like wasted effort, and biologists have long wanted to work out what it is for.

In 2001 Špinka, Newberry and Bekoff put forward an influential hypothesis: play is “training for the unexpected.” When animals play they deliberately put themselves out of control: a lion cub rolls over to bare its belly, the chaser and the chased swap roles. In an environment with no consequences, they can practice again and again recovering from sudden loss of control.

A paper prototype is the designer’s “play.” On a tabletop with no consequences, deliberately put the rules where they might go wrong, and let them stumble a few times. A fall on paper doesn’t hurt; a fall after release does.

Principle one: play is “training for the unexpected”

The earlier discussion of play covered the “practice” account, in which play is practice of one fixed skill. This hypothesis is its upgrade: what is practiced is dealing with what you didn’t foresee. This is exactly where a paper prototype is valuable: the designer doesn’t know what playtesters will do, and the prototype lets the surprise happen in a cheap place.

Controversy: this is an influential hypothesis, and it has no settled conclusion. Later reviews note that many of its specific predictions have not been adequately tested. Play may also have more than one function; the “play that doesn’t quite fit” mentioned earlier is one example.

Source of the principle:
Špinka, Newberry and Bekoff · 2001 · proposed that play is training for dealing with the unexpected (Mammalian play: training for the unexpected. Quarterly Review of Biology, 76(2), 141–168. PubMed).

Play as Training for the Unexpected

Play as training for the unexpected

Marek Špinka, Ruth C. Newberry, Marc Bekoff · 2001, Mammalian play: training for the unexpected

When animals play they deliberately put themselves out of control and then recover, practicing in an environment with no consequences how to deal with what they didn’t foresee.

ExampleA lion cub rolls over to bare its belly while chaser and chased swap roles. Putting the rules on purpose where they might go wrong in a paper prototype is the designer’s version of the same practice.

Another trouble lies in the designer: “I think it’s fun” doesn’t count, because the designer knows the game too well. When reading the rules, the mind automatically fills in everything left unwritten.

This has a classic experimental version. In 1990 Elizabeth Newton, a Stanford graduate student, had some people “tap on a table” the rhythm of a song everyone knows, and others guess it. The tappers predicted beforehand that about half of the listeners would get it. In the result only 3 of 120 songs were guessed, about 2.5%.

The tapper hears the whole song in their head, while the listener hears only a string of thuds. Between designer and playtester lies just such a gap.

Principle two: the curse of knowledge, so someone else must play

This is the curse of knowledge covered earlier. The designer is not careless; once you know, it is hard to “forget.” So to test whether the rules really can be “written on one page and explained in three minutes,” don’t read them once yourself; have someone who doesn’t know read them once.

The numbers come from Newton’s doctoral research and were spread widely by Made to Stick (the Heath brothers, 2007). The sample is small; the experiment shows a direction and should not be taken as an exact ratio.

Check it yourself (5 minutes): find a friend, tap on a table the rhythms of three songs you both know, and have them guess. Before tapping, write down how many you expect them to get, then compare the result. Then test a game the same way: show them nothing, explain the rules only by speech, and count how many rules they missed hearing.

Sources:
Elizabeth Newton · 1990 · Stanford doctoral dissertation; some people tapped out song rhythms and others guessed, with a hit rate of about 2.5%; retold in Heath & Heath, Made to Stick (2007).
Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber · 1989 · the term “curse of knowledge” first appears in their paper, whose experiments show that people who know something find it hard to ignore what they know.

Common misconceptions

    
    
      
  • “A prototype has to look good”: looking good distracts, and also makes you reluctant to change. Get the rules and choices right first.
    • 
      
  • “Start by making the full game”: make the smallest playable game first. If the rules don’t fit on one page, they are too complicated.
    • 
      
  • “It’s enough that I find it fun”: the designer knows the rules best, so can’t see where beginners get stuck (the curse of knowledge). Get others to play it.
    • 
      
  • “Changing many things at once makes faster progress”: change too much and you won’t know which change did the work.
    • 
      
  • “A paper prototype can verify everything”: it can’t verify feel or real-time rhythm.
    • 
    

  


  

    
Something to think about

    
In your prototype, which part do you least want to change? Why? If a playtester says it isn’t fun, do you doubt the rules first, or the playtester?

    
    
A contested question: does low fidelity always get more “honest feedback” than high fidelity? Some researchers and practitioners think paper prototypes make testers bolder in criticizing; others think that being too rough makes it hard for testers to imagine the real experience. It depends on what you want to verify.

  


  

    
Key points

    
    
      
  • A prototype exists for learning, and the rougher the better: paper, dice and sticky notes are enough; the benefit of roughness is that changes are affordable and judgment lands on the rules.
    • 
      
  • The paper prototype comes from the handmade practice of board games and the lo-fi prototypes of software interfaces: trading time for more rounds of playtests.
    • 
      
  • Play is practicing, in a safe environment, how to deal with the unexpected (Špinka et al., 2001), and the paper prototype lets the design’s “surprises” happen in a cheap place.
    • 
      
  • The designer is trapped by the “curse of knowledge,” so play alone first, then find people who don’t know. In playtests, shut up, only watch and record, and change one thing at a time.
    • 
    

  

  
A paper prototype moves the design’s “falls” to a place where they don’t hurt, at the lowest cost. It isn’t pretty, which is exactly why it works.
