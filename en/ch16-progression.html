Chapter 16

Progression & Growth Systems

Why will a player play one more hour? Because they can see themselves getting stronger.

Learning goals

  • Tell apart the five kinds of growth in a progression system (numbers, abilities, gear and collection, player skill, meta-progression) and say what problem each is good for.
  • Explain why the experience needed per level usually keeps growing, use a curve to spot a “wall,” “too fast” and “number inflation,” and say what problems experience points and skill trees were invented to solve.
  • Design an experience curve and a decent skill tree, point out the must-pick nodes and trap options in it, and watch out for “experience points becoming the goal themselves.”
about 55 min
1

What it isProgression: five ways a player “gets stronger”

When a player stays another hour in a game, it is often because of “one more monster and I level up,” and not because “this round is especially fun.” That pull is progression.

Chapter 2 covered the core loop: do something, get something, do better things. A progression system keeps the “get” from evaporating with every lap of the loop, and lets it pile up layer by layer.

“Getting stronger” covers five different things, and mixing them together muddies the design.

Stat growth (the character gets stronger)

When experience points fill up, the level goes up by one and health and attack rise. It is the easiest to make, and the easiest to turn a game into “numbers chasing numbers.”

Ability growth (the character learns new things)

Unlock a double jump, a grappling hook, new skills. It changes what the player can do, and not only how well. When “you can only get past after you get the hook,” the ability becomes a key.

Gear and collection

Swapping weapons, collecting sets, completing a codex. Growth comes from “finding,” not “accumulating,” so the surprise is stronger, but it depends on drops, and drops are random.

Player skill growth

The character’s numbers haven’t changed at all, but the player has learned to read the boss’s wind-up. Soulslikes, fighting games and speedrunning all rely on it. This kind of growth is not diluted by number inflation, but it is invisible, and design has to “light it up.”

Meta-progression (growth outside a run)

A run fails, but the player takes something away: a new character unlocked, a permanent bonus, more options. “Losing” is no longer pure loss.

Prestige / reset

Zero out on purpose, take a permanent bonus, and climb again. A staple trick of idle games.

Diablo-style loot grinding: character level is only an entry ticket, and the real pursuit is the dropped gear. Growth comes mainly from “gear and collection,” plus a little stat growth.
Hollow Knight and other metroidvanias: health goes up only a little, but every new ability suddenly opens a place on the map you couldn’t get past before. Growth comes mainly from “ability growth,” and the numbers hardly matter.
Learning to ride a bike (daily life): your “gear” hasn’t changed and there are no “numbers,” but today you can ride 100 meters without falling. This is pure player-skill growth, and one of the oldest and most lasting sources of joy for humans.
Counterexample: leveling up is just “bigger numbers.” Monster health grows in step with the player’s level, so from level 1 to level 50 every monster takes 10 hits. Leveling up changed only the numbers on the screen, not how you play. It looks like progress and is not.
2

What it isThe experience curve: how much to reach the next level?

At the heart of stat growth is a table: how much experience it takes to go from level n to level n+1. Draw that table out and you have the experience curve. Three shapes are the most common.

Illustrative: all three curves are generated from the same “growth rate” parameter (r=0.10), and the vertical axis is the experience needed for the next level. Linear is “a steady increase,” polynomial (here a square) speeds up as it goes, and exponential is “each level is a fixed multiple of the last”: slower than polynomial at first, then it overtakes and keeps shooting up.

Linear is easy to grasp and steady in rhythm, but late in the game it feels “weightless.” Polynomial (square, cube) climbs faster and faster, more gently than exponential, and is a common compromise. Real games often hand-edit the table on top of it.

Exponential is very quick for the first few levels, then hits a wall that keeps getting higher. It is mostly used in idle games with “endless growth,” and needs resets alongside it.

Beyond the curve there are three more knobs.

The first is the level cap: a hard cap is plain and clear, while a soft cap means “you can still grow, but it pays less and less.” That suits keeping deep players busy without breaking balance.

The second is that the rewards grow too: monsters in higher-level zones give more experience. The curve is the denominator and the reward is the numerator, so how fast leveling feels has to be judged with both together.

The third is the power fantasy: go back to the starting village and cut down in one blow a monster that once made you scramble, and the thrill depends on the old content not having grown stronger along with the player.

Many Japanese role-playing games: in the early game each level takes just a few battles, and later a level takes a long grind; the curve clearly bends, and players get through on “the next level is right in front of me” (a general description of the experience, not any one game’s numbers).
The “streak levels” of a language-learning app (daily life): the first few levels arrive in days, and later ones take longer and longer; newcomers get quick wins and veterans get a long-term goal.
Counterexample: level scaling. Every enemy grows stronger in step with the player, which looks like “always a challenge” but cancels the power fantasy: you gain 20 levels and the small fry are still a headache. Many players dislike it for exactly this reason, that growth is cancelled out.
3

What it isMaking progress visible, and how “zeroing out” can also be progress

However clever the numbers, if the player can’t see them they might as well not exist. Progress has to be shown in layers, with feedback at every scale from seconds to hours.

Illustrative: the shorter the feedback, the more “fine-grained”; the longer, the more “ceremonial.” A good progression system has something to see at every time scale, and if any layer is missing, players may “feel no progress” during that stretch.

The magic of a progress bar is that being “only 5% from leveling up” makes it hard to put the phone down, which is tied to the goal gradient: the closer to the goal, the harder one pushes. When a new feature unlocks, tell the player. In quite a few games players unlock a skill and never notice, and the progress is wasted. A level-up should also give more than a number: let the player feel the difference at once, with a flourish for the new move or an enemy knocked flying in one hit.

Progression has a natural enemy: reaching the end. The numbers grow huge, then either hit a wall or become “hundreds of millions” everywhere, and the player feels nothing. Designers came up with two ways to turn “resetting to zero” into a reward: prestige lets players reset voluntarily in exchange for a permanent multiplier, and meta-progression lets even a failed run buy something. The key is that every round must be faster than the last, or the reset is only a punishment.

The “ascension” of idle games: numbers climb from single digits into scientific notation, and each ascension takes away a permanent bonus; restarting takes only a few minutes to catch up with what a whole day used to give, and the player experiences it as “faster and faster.”
Meta-unlocks in roguelikes: the player lost this run, but took a key away, and next run can unlock a new character or new options. Losing is no longer pure loss but groundwork for the next run (a general description of this kind of design, not any one game’s numbers).
Counterexample: slower after a reset. If the early game after a reset is even slower than the last round, or the permanent bonus is too small to notice, players feel “that was for nothing” and won’t reset again. The next round must be visibly faster.
4

How it was inventedHow were experience points and levels “invented”?

When a battle ends, the story isn’t over

In the late 1960s and early 1970s, the “war games” hobbyists played put little figures on a table. One battle ended and that was it; next time you set them up again. But somebody thought: what if each figure were a “character” that had to persist across many games? How would that character “get stronger”? Measured by what? When does it count as having “gone up a step”?

Try it yourself first. Suppose a player earns an average of 100 experience points per play session, and the designer wants the leveling rhythm from level 2 to level 12 to carry “some anticipation but not too much grind.” Drag the slider to decide “how much more each level asks than the one before,” and see about how many sessions each level takes.

1971 Chainmail (medieval wargame rules by Gary Gygax and Jeff Perren) is published. Its “fantasy supplement” already has stronger units like “Hero,” “Superhero” and “Wizard,” but when a battle ends, the pieces go back in the box.
About 1971–73 In the Blackmoor campaign based on Chainmail, Dave Arneson let a single character persist across many games. According to the player Greg Svenson’s recollection, early on a monster’s hit points were the experience points earned for killing it, players did not keep the tally themselves, and Arneson announced when it was time to level up (accounts differ somewhat between sources).
1974 Dungeons & Dragons (Gygax and Arneson, TSR) is published and writes “experience” into explicit rules: kill monsters and take treasure to gain experience, and level up when the total reaches a threshold. According to the rule tables compiled by fans, the thresholds of the first few levels roughly double each level and get harder higher up. And according to Jon Peterson’s research, in the original game treasure brought in far more experience than killing monsters.
1974–75 The earliest computer role-playing games appear on large mainframes (such as dnd and pedit5 on the PLATO system; which came first and the details vary by source), and they carry the whole “experience and levels” scheme over. The computer keeps the books for the player, and from then on the experience curve can be written as a formula.

Squeezing “what has been done” into a number that can be compared

Points are used because without a number there is only the referee saying “you leveled up.” Points tell players how far they are from the goal, and make the rules public, so they can be argued about and designed. They are then cut into “levels” because continuous points are flat. Once cut into steps, each step brings a concentrated reward: new stats, new abilities.

The threshold rises because, if every level were equally easy, high-level characters would soon become “gods.” How to set the thresholds has been a parameter under constant revision from the start. The trade-off you just made on the slider is one the designers of the time made too.

Note: the usual account of the “origin” of experience points and levels is Arneson’s Blackmoor campaign, which then led into the 1974 D&D. Scholars and hobbyists dispute “who deserves the credit” (Arneson and TSR later had a royalty lawsuit), so only the parts on which the sources agree are written here.

With levels, a new trouble appeared: if leveling up just raises stats automatically, then all characters of the same class and level are exactly alike. Designers wanted each level-up to bring a choice. The earlier approach was a fixed level-up table, where you automatically learned certain things at certain levels. The newer idea was to give players a few points and let them decide where to put them, with “learn A before B” prerequisites between skills, which drawn out became a tree.

Strategy games already had the prerequisite structure of a “tech tree,” and a character’s skill tree is a relative of it. The 1996 Diablo gave each class a page of skills unlocked by level. The 2000 Diablo II is often called the forerunner of the modern skill tree. Later online games developed it into the “talent tree,” letting the same class follow different builds. There is no agreed answer to “who made the first skill tree.”

Why would anyone think of making players “reset to zero”?

This one has no reliable single origin, so only the motivation is told. The numbers in an idle game swell along an exponential curve, progress gets slower and slower, and the player eventually meets a wall. The designers did not tear down the wall. They gave the player a choice: zero yourself out in exchange for a permanent multiplier, then climb the same mountain at greater speed. That gives back the thrill of “fast early growth,” lifts the scale of the numbers to a new level, and still keeps the main thread of “the more you play, the stronger you get.”

Why it worksWhy do people love to “get stronger”: competence and visible progress

Before designing an experience curve, it helps to know what it is catching in the player. The first thing is the sense of competence.

Competence: I am becoming “capable”

In their 2000 self-determination theory, Ryan and Deci hold that people have three basic psychological needs: autonomy, competence and relatedness. Visible growth is the most direct source of competence: you did something, and the world answers “you are stronger now.” So the basic job of a progression system is to make progress visible.

Source: Ryan, R. M., & Deci, E. L. (2000). Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being. American Psychologist, 55(1), 68–78. Applying it to game progression is an analogy.

The second is that the closer people get to a goal, the harder they push, which is the goal gradient. The psychologist Hull (1932) had already proposed that animals run faster the closer they get to the goal. Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng (2006), using real data from coffee-shop punch cards, found that the closer a customer got to “a free cup once the card is full,” the shorter the gap between visits.

There is an even neater experiment. Nunes and Drèze (2006) randomly handed out two kinds of punch cards at a car wash: one with 8 blank spaces, one with 10 spaces but with 2 already stamped for the customer. Either way another 8 washes were needed, yet after nine months only 19% of the first kind were completed against 34% of the second. The feeling of “already having progress” itself pushes people forward.

The goal gradient and “endowed progress”

A progress bar is there to manufacture this visible closeness: give the player a little progress at the very start (a tutorial that gifts a few levels), and give plenty of “almost there” on-screen moments before a level-up. The effect is clear in the car-wash experiment. How much it does inside a game still has to be checked by playtesting.

Sources: Kivetz, R., Urminsky, O., & Zheng, Y. (2006). The goal-gradient hypothesis resurrected. Journal of Marketing Research, 43(1), 39–58; Nunes, J. C., & Drèze, X. (2006). The endowed progress effect. Journal of Consumer Research, 32(4), 504–512 (a field experiment, about 300 cards).

Growth should rise together with challenge

Flow requires challenge and ability to roughly match, and the zone of proximal development says that tasks just out of reach but within a stretch are best for learning. The job of a growth system is this: each time the player’s ability goes up a step, the challenge rises with it, so the two advance alternately inside the “flow channel.”

Sources: Csikszentmihalyi, M. (1990). Flow; Vygotsky, L. S. (1978). Mind in Society. Merging them into “progress in step with challenge” is one common reading in game design.

Why it worksWhy does the thrill of leveling fade? And why do experience points “lead players astray”?

Chapter 2 covered Schultz’s monkeys: once they learned that “light on means juice,” the juice itself no longer excited the dopamine neurons, and only an unexpected extra portion did. Carried over to leveling, something painful follows: when a level-up becomes fully predictable, the 30th ding is like the 30th sip of juice.

Progression systems therefore often “add a little surprise”: occasionally an extra good drop, occasionally a crit, so the prediction misses a little. This is also a double-edged sword, and the same mechanism is how slot machines hold people.

Reward prediction error, and the other side of hedonic adaptation

Schultz, Dayan and Montague (1997) concluded that the monkeys’ dopamine neurons report a “prediction error”: they fire more when things are better than expected, barely respond when things are as expected, and fall below baseline when things are worse. So a fully predictable reward gets duller and duller, which is the same thing as hedonic adaptation in the previous chapter told two ways: the baseline is moving up. Conversely, only when each level teaches the player something new can the “above expectation” part keep being produced.

Converting neuroscience findings directly into design rules is common in the design world, and the two do not correspond one to one in any agreed way.

Sources: Schultz, W., Dayan, P., & Montague, P. R. (1997). A neural substrate of prediction and reward. Science, 275(5306), 1593–1599. For hedonic adaptation see Brickman & Campbell (1971).

Number inflation also brings up a perception problem. How a change feels depends roughly on its relative size: going from 10 attack to 15 and going from 1000 to 1005 feel completely different. To make every level “felt,” growth has to be proportional. Proportional growth has an exponential shape, so the numbers double their way up, and that is where stat inflation comes from.

The last trouble is more hidden. You give the player experience points meaning to reflect “how well you are playing.” But once experience becomes the goal, players look for the least-effort way to farm it: swinging at the same rat in a hole again and again, numbers shooting up, while the chests and signposts go completely unseen.

The number isn’t lying. It just no longer stands for what the designer wanted it to stand for.

Goodhart’s law: when a measure becomes a target

The British economist Charles Goodhart pointed out in 1975, while discussing monetary policy, that a statistical regularity breaks down once it is used as a basis for control. The anthropologist Marilyn Strathern in 1997 boiled it down to the widely repeated version: “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.” Experience points are a proxy for “how invested and how skilled the player is.” Once players play directly for them, they and the real way of playing part ways. The remedy is to let experience come from the behavior the designer actually wants to encourage, and to give diminishing returns for grinding the same thing over and over.

Verification experiment: is growth actually “cancelled out”? A number-inflation comparison

Below is an illustrative model: attack grows by g per level, and the health of enemies in the current zone grows by gz per level. Watch three things: how many hits it takes to kill a mob back in the starter zone (power fantasy); how many hits to kill an enemy in the current zone (sense of challenge); and how long the attack number gets (inflation).

In the illustrative model, set gz equal to g and you get “level scaling”: fighting in the current zone always takes the same number of hits.

Advanced: how the model is computed

Attack = 10×(1+g)^(level−1); newbie-zone enemy HP is fixed at 100; current-zone enemy HP = 100×(1+gz)^(level−1). For example, with g = 15% at level 20: attack ≈ 10×1.1519 ≈ 143.

Old content has to “fall behind” for there to be a power fantasy, and current content has to “keep up” for there to be a challenge. Both are needed, so gz should be slightly below g, but then the numbers inevitably swell; try pulling the level up to 60.

Advanced: how the three curves are computed (illustrative)

Linear: need = base×(1+r×(n−1)); polynomial: need = base×(1+r×(n−1))²; exponential: need = base×(1+r)^(n−1). Time for level n = need(n) ÷ (daily experience × (1+bonus)^(n−1)).

A good skill tree: the melee, ranged and support lines all have strong builds, merge nodes encourage mixing, and no node is “you lose if you skip it.” You have to weigh things before clicking.
Prerequisites for university courses (daily life): you study calculus before linear algebra and probability, and finish both before you can take machine learning. It is a “skill tree” with a merge node, and students really do have choices.
A bad skill tree: one entry node (such as “+20% health”) is cheap and strong, and everyone takes it; some ultimate node costs an absurd amount and is not strong, and nobody takes it. It looks like twenty-some choices, but there is really one best path, which is a “false choice.”

Common misconceptions

  • “The more progression, the better”: if everything carries growth, players are flooded. And the more progression there is, the easier it is for players to “play only for the numbers,” whether or not the gameplay in their hands is fun.
  • “Bigger numbers feel great”: when numbers swell to astronomical sizes, each level’s change is just one more digit and nobody feels anything. The real thrill comes from changes in play (new abilities, new ways of fighting).
  • “Max level is the end”: if there is no new goal after max level, players leave. Either have soft caps, meta-progression or resets, or let “player skill growth” be the long-term goal.
  • “Level scaling keeps the game always challenging”: it also wipes out the power fantasy and cancels the sense of growth (see the number-inflation comparison above).
  • “Variable drops are free stickiness”: random rewards are very attractive, but they cut both ways. Once tied to spending, they come close to the edge of ethics.

Reflection and debate

Can an outside reward “crowd out” inner enjoyment? This is the long-debated “overjustification” question in psychology: adding a reward to something already fun can sometimes weaken the interest a person had. Does a progress bar in a game amplify the fun, or turn the game into clocking in? The research conditions and conclusions are not consistent, so designers had better playtest themselves.

Are “grinding” and “the grind” good design? Some think a moderate “numbers chase” gives casual players something to do; others think it uses “time” to pass for “content.” Both positions hold up, and it depends on the target player.

Progression is not the same as leveling. It is a system that lets the player’s investment be seen, kept and amplified, and whether the numbers are the star is a design choice.

Key points

  • Five kinds of growth: stats, abilities, gear and collection, player skill, meta-progression, plus prestige resets. Don’t understand “progression” as only “levels.”
  • The experience curve sets the pace: linear, polynomial and exponential each have their character; look at both experience needed and income, or you can’t see the “wall.”
  • The logic of invention: a character must persist across many plays, hence points and levels. Leveling must offer choices, hence skill points and skill trees. Numbers run out, hence resets and meta-progression.
  • Why it works: competence, the goal gradient and endowed progress, prediction error and hedonic adaptation, relative perception. So growth must be visible and keep supplying new stimulation.
  • Don’t let a measure lead the player astray: once experience points become the goal, players will farm them. Have experience come from the behavior you want to encourage.
  • Design check: Is there a wall? Will players hit max level before finishing? Are there must-pick nodes and trap options? Have the numbers swollen until they mean nothing?
A progression system turns “what you have done” into “getting stronger that you can see.” A good design lets growth and challenge rise together, instead of numbers chasing numbers.
