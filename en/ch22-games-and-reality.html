Chapter 22

Games & Reality

Where is the edge of a game? Why do people argue over “what a game is”?

Learning goals

  • Say who proposed each of “the magic circle,” “gamification” and “finite and infinite games,” and why it was proposed.
  • Explain why gamification sometimes works and sometimes backfires, and use a checklist of questions to evaluate a gamification scheme.
  • Point out that the border between “game” and “reality” thins in these four directions (money, identity, relationships, irreversibility), and form your own view.

What it isThe magic circle: a “temporary world” marked out inside reality

A child pulls on a monster mask and chases a little brother around the yard, and the brother screams and runs, laughing until he can hardly breathe. Suddenly a real stray dog darts in at the gate, and the brother’s scream changes pitch: this time he is truly afraid.

Two screams sound about the same, one of a game and one not. What separates them? An agreement nobody spoke aloud and everybody understands: right now, we’re playing. Once the agreement is broken, the same chase becomes a real fright.

In Homo Ludens (1938, first written in Dutch), the Dutch historian Johan Huizinga saw arenas, chessboards, stages, temples and courtrooms as one kind of place: circled off, temporary, with rules of their own. Such a place is the magic circle. Inside the circle there are the circle’s own rules: “capturing” the opponent’s piece is not violence, and losing one game is not failing at life.

Concept card

Magic Circle

Magic circle

Johan Huizinga · Homo Ludens, 1938; Salen and Zimmerman brought it into game design in 2003

The temporary space with its own rules that a game marks out within reality. Wins and losses inside the circle count only inside the circle.

ExampleYou “capture” a friend’s queen in chess, he laughs and curses, and a minute later the pieces are set up again.

In 2003 Salen and Zimmerman’s Rules of Play put the term to use in game design. So the “magic circle” designers speak of today is largely the version they retold and reworked, narrower in range than what Huizinga originally discussed.

The dashed circle is the magic circle. The double-headed arrows mean: money, identity and time from outside can walk into the circle, and wins and losses inside can also walk out.

A game of chess: you “capture” a friend’s queen, he laughs and curses, and a minute later the pieces are set up again. The consequences inside the circle stay inside it, and the circle is intact.
A boxing ring: the same punch is a match in the ring and a crime on the street. The ropes and the referee are an agreement everyone accepts, and the agreement makes that punch lawful.
Playing the villain: an actor snarls on stage and shakes hands with the opponent after stepping down. Nobody takes the harsh words spoken on stage as his own thoughts.
Online-game gear that sells for real money: once the gear has a price outside the game, the “wall” of the magic circle gets a hole: stolen accounts, scams, and the losses are real. It is still a game, but the magic circle is no longer intact.

What it isGamification: carrying game elements to non-game places

Open a vocabulary app and the most prominent thing on the home screen is a flame and “37-day streak.” The user is not playing a game, yet opens the app because they can’t bear to break that number. Points, badges, leaderboards, levels, quests and streaks are common elements in games. Using them in occasions that are not games, such as learning, fitness and work, is called gamification.

Concept card

Gamification

Gamification

Deterding, Dixon, Khaled and Nacke · gave the commonly used definition in 2011

Using game design elements, such as points, badges and leaderboards, in occasions that are not games.

ExampleThe “37-day streak” on a vocabulary app’s home screen.

Around 2010 the word suddenly became popular in internet product circles. But people used it loosely: is making a whole educational game gamification? Does adding points to a weekly report count? In a 2011 MindTrek conference paper, four researchers, Deterding, Dixon, Khaled and Nacke, gave a definition that has been cited again and again since: using game design elements in non-game settings.

The use of this definition is to separate gamification from several neighboring concepts. The chart below crosses “play” and “game,” “whole” and “parts,” with one kind of thing in each cell.

Illustration: the idea comes from the “playing / gaming × whole / parts” division in the paper by Deterding et al.; the wording in the cells is rewritten for explanation. A flight simulator for training pilots is a complete game that just has a serious purpose, called a “serious game,” and goes in the top-right cell. “Points plus badges” goes in the bottom-right cell.

A vocabulary app’s streak: putting a “game element” (the number of days in a row) into study is typical gamification.
A flight simulator: the whole thing is itself a complete game, only used for training. This is a serious game, not gamification.
The piano stairs: in 2009 Volkswagen’s “Fun Theory” campaign turned the steps of a Stockholm subway station into piano keys, right beside the escalator. The organizers said clearly more people took the stairs (this is the campaign’s own number, not peer reviewed). It has no points or ranking and only puts the flavor of “play” into the stairs, and is closer to playful design.
A customer-service ticket leaderboard: there are points and rankings, everyone competes only on how many tickets they handle regardless of quality, and closes tickets in a rush. A typical case of gamification gone wrong.

What it isExtension: finite and infinite games

Since the circle is drawn by everyone’s agreement, the game inside it can be played in two ways: to win, and break up once someone wins; or to keep the game going. The scholar of religion James P. Carse divided them this way in Finite and Infinite Games (Free Press, 1986). The finite game aims at winning, with clear participants, boundaries and an end time. The infinite game aims at keeping the game going; nobody wants it to end, and the rules can change in order to continue. This is Carse’s classification, often borrowed in discussions of business, politics and education, some of which borrowing is disputed.

Concept card

Finite and Infinite Games

Finite and infinite games

James P. Carse · 1986

A finite game is played to win and has an end. An infinite game is played to keep the game going and has no end.

ExampleThe World Cup final is a finite game; children’s “playing house” is an infinite game.

A World Cup final: it has an end and a winner and loser, a typical finite game. Precisely because the match will end, every minute is so tense.
Children playing house: no winner, and the rules change any time for “keeping the game going” (“now you be the dad”).
The slogan “a company should play an infinite game”: many business books simplify Carse’s classification into “finite is bad, infinite is good,” which ends up as “never stop working.” This is a simplification of the original book: competition itself is not bad.
4

How it was inventedWhy did the argument over “what a game is” arise?

A word everyone understands and nobody can pin down

At the start of the twentieth century scholars began to study “play” seriously. Play seemed to show up in war, law, poetry and sport, yet was not the proper business of any of them. To study it, one must first answer: what counts as a game? Whoever offers a definition, someone produces a counterexample. This question has practical consequences: a definition decides what goes into a “game design” course, and also whether games can be brought into schools and companies.

Guess first: who first put forward the “magic circle” that designers often speak of?

Pick one and see if it’s right.

Below we go through it in time order. Each stop answers the question left by the one before: where does the circle come from, can there be one unified definition, what is the core of a game, can a game have no end, can the circle be taken outside.

1938 Huizinga, Homo Ludens. War, law, poetry, ritual… why do they all seem like “playing”? His answer: games are a basic ingredient of culture, and people live temporarily by the rules of a “circled-off place.” How strict the circle is and where its rules come from, he did not make clear.
1953 Wittgenstein, Philosophical Investigations. Board games, card games, ball games, the Olympics… can one find an essence they all share? He laid them out one by one, found none, and saw only family resemblance: they resemble one another in different respects, with no single line running through the whole family. But without a unified essence, does the designer still have a tool that is good enough?
1978 Suits’s The Grasshopper answers: playing a game is voluntarily overcoming unnecessary obstacles. The rules deliberately block the easy road, and the player voluntarily accepts them: that is a game. He also imagined that in a utopia where all needs are met, people would still play games. This is Suits’s view. Critics ask: do love and art count as “overcoming obstacles” too?
1986 Carse, Finite and Infinite Games. A match ends, but a community and a culture don’t, so how to tell them apart? Carse’s answer: a finite game is for winning, an infinite game is for continuing. Later business books simplified this into a slogan, and the meaning changed.
2003 Salen and Zimmerman, Rules of Play. Designers needed a common word for talking about “the border between game and reality,” so they reworked Huizinga’s “circle” into a design tool, the “magic circle.” But in digital games, with money and identity flooding in, does the circle still stand?
2011 Deterding et al., “Gamification: Toward a Definition.” Companies and apps were stuffing points and badges into products, so what exactly was that? Their answer: gamification is using game design elements in non-game settings, which differs from making a whole game. But why does it work sometimes and not others?

Each step answers the question left by the previous one.

Why it holdsWhy does gamification sometimes backfire?

The first trouble in moving game elements outside the circle is rewards. Remember the experiment about certificates? In 1973 Lepper, Greene and Nisbett let nursery-school children who already loved drawing know beforehand that “drawing earns a certificate.” Afterward, in free play, these children drew for less time than children who had not been promised a certificate. This is called the overjustification effect. Gamification is handing out “certificates” for things, so it meets the same problem.

Amplifying: while memorizing words you see “30 more than last week,” and want to go on. The reward says “you’ve improved,” which is feedback.
Replacing: a child who loved drawing, once promised a certificate, draws only for the certificate, and the interest has been “bought away.” Here the reward is saying “do as I want,” which is control.
Gaming the number: to keep the “streak” going you open the app for one second every day and close it, and take in nothing. The number wins and the purpose loses.

Three principles, one reminder

The overjustification effect and self-determination theory (covered earlier in the chapter on player motivation): Deci and Ryan’s self-determination theory holds that if a reward is felt as control (“I’m doing this to get the prize”), it weakens the sense of autonomy and lowers intrinsic motivation. If it is felt as information (“I’m doing well”), it actually strengthens the sense of competence. In 1999 Deci, Koestner and Ryan pooled 128 experiments and held that tangible, announced-in-advance external rewards generally weaken intrinsic motivation. Cameron and Pierce and others analyzed it a different way, with much milder conclusions. The two sides still hold their ground.

Goodhart’s law (covered earlier in the chapter on metrics): once a metric is taken as a target, people find loopholes in it. The “check-in count” on a leaderboard gets gamed, because people are finding ways to make the number bigger, not doing the thing behind the number.

Sources:
Lepper, Greene & Nisbett · 1973 · had nursery-school children draw, with certificates announced beforehand · these children drew less afterward (Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 28(1): 129–137).
Deci, Koestner & Ryan · 1999 · pooled 128 experiments · concluded that announced tangible rewards weaken intrinsic motivation (Psychological Bulletin 125(6): 627–668).
Deci & Ryan · self-determination theory.
Goodhart · 1975 · proposed this regularity; Strathern · 1997 · made the current wording popular.

The leaderboard is the most commonly used part of gamification, and it works through another principle: social comparison. Leon Festinger proposed in 1954 that people compare themselves with others in order to know how they are doing, and prefer to compare with people close to themselves.

So the person in 38th place will fight hard looking at 37th, but feels nothing about 1st. A server-wide board often motivates only those near the top and makes those in the middle and bottom want to leave. How the board is divided into tiers and who is compared with whom matter far more than whether there is a board.

Source: Festinger · 1954 · proposed social comparison theory · people evaluate themselves by comparing with similar others (the paper A Theory of Social Comparison Processes, Human Relations 7(2): 117–140).

Illustration: whether gamification succeeds depends on whether the game elements are “amplifying” the original motivation or “replacing” it.

The real effect does depend on the case. In 2014 Hamari, Koivisto and Sarsa reviewed the existing empirical studies and found that gamification has positive effects most of the time, but the effect depends strongly on the specific setting and users.

Why does the magic circle “leak”?

The magic circle is an ideal: everything inside the circle has consequences only inside. But in reality the circle is often not closed. Edward Castronova, who studies virtual-world economies, in Synthetic Worlds (2005) called the border of virtual worlds an “almost-magic circle,” meaning the wall has many small holes and leaks.

The magic circle leaks in four directions. Money: casino chips are real money; Diablo III launched in 2012 with an auction house that let players buy and sell gear for real money, and Blizzard closed it in March 2014, saying it harmed the core fun of “killing monsters for loot.” Identity: a professional player’s skill at the game is their career and status. Social consequences: betrayal in a game can make friends in real life drift away. Irreversibility: money lost gambling and years spent can’t be redone.

Not everything that leaks out is bad: esports gives people who love games a career path, and friendships made in games are real too. The problem lies in these two cases: the consequences are borne by one side only (the player bears the losses, the operator profits), or the participants don’t notice that the circle has broken. How to use itWhat suits gamification?

Turn the principles above into judgments at design time. The six questions below are a checklist drawn up from the earlier chapters, and you can add or remove items. Read through once, then go and turn the knobs in the evaluator:

① Intrinsic interest

With all the points removed, would anyone still be willing to do it?

② A hard-to-game measure

Progress can be measured, and the numbers are not easily gamed.

③ Voluntary

Voluntariness is an element of play: a forced “game” is only an assessment.

④ Low cost of failure

The good of a magic circle is daring to make mistakes, so the cost of failure must be low.

⑤ Rewards support, not replace

A reward should be like a coach’s affirmation, not money “buying behavior.”

⑥ Participants accept the measure

Participants don’t feel watched or controlled.

Using games to “make an argument”

Gamification takes games “outside”; the other direction is letting the game speak. The game scholar Ian Bogost in Persuasive Games (2007) put forward procedural rhetoric: an essay persuades by argument, a game persuades by rules. His criticism of gamification is sharp too: pasting on only “points and badges” without changing the experience the rules bring grabs only the most superficial thing about games. In 2011 he half-ironically called this kind of practice “exploitationware.” This is his view.

The Landlord’s Game: Elizabeth Magie’s The Landlord’s Game was granted a US patent in 1904. She meant to show how land monopoly makes landlords richer and tenants poorer, and to promote a land tax. The later Monopoly (1935) evolved from it. The viewpoint is not in the lines but in the rules, and one round makes it clear.
Writing the viewpoint into dialogue boxes: the game pops up a long sermon and the player’s reaction is to press “skip.” What you wanted to say changed none of the player’s choices, so it was never played out.
The point to make→ What “system” lies behind it→ Turn the system into rules→ Let the player choose within the rules→ Experience the consequences

Note: the rules themselves carry the designer’s stance. Players may reach the opposite conclusion, or even learn “how to exploit loopholes.”

Source: Magie · 1904 · US patent 748,626 (granted January 5, 1904). Parker Brothers · 1935 · published Monopoly. Who “invented” Monopoly is reported differently in different sources; all that is said here is that The Landlord’s Game is one of the earliest known versions.

Common misconceptions

  • “Adding points, badges and a leaderboard is gamification.” These are only the most superficial elements of games. Without the overall design of goals, choices and feedback, superficial elements can’t hold up for long.
  • “The magic circle is a wall, and what happens inside has nothing to do with reality.” The magic circle is more a layer of “consensus” that needs constant upkeep: participants must agree to it and designers must respect it. Once money and identity flow in in quantity, the circle thins.
  • “A game must have a strict definition or it can’t be studied.” Wittgenstein pointed out that concepts can work through “family resemblance.” A definition is a tool, not a fence.
  • “Huizinga invented the magic circle of game design.” He talked about the broader places of “play”; the magic-circle concept commonly used in design was reworked by later writers.

Reflection and debate

Suits’s utopia: he held that in a world where all needs are met, games would still be a meaningful activity. Critics ask: does this shrink a “meaningful life” into “overcoming artificial obstacles”? Can raising children, art and love count as “games”? On the other hand, some say: if games matter so much, much of the labor of reality should also be more game-like.

Carse’s classification: many examples lie between the two. A community is an infinite game that also contains plenty of small finite games.

On gamification: Bogost’s criticism is his own view. Some researchers and practitioners also hold that well-designed gamification helps in fitness and learning. The effect depends on setting, population and duration, and research conclusions are not uniform.

One question to leave you with: if you had a child, would you want their homework, chores and exercise gamified? Which parts of your answer are considerations of “whether it works” and which of “values”?

Key points

  • The magic circle (Huizinga’s “circled-off place,” brought into design by Salen and Zimmerman): a game marks out a temporary, rule-governed world within reality. It is held up by everyone’s consensus, and it “leaks” in four directions: money, identity, relationships and irreversibility.
  • Gamification (Deterding et al., 2011): using game design elements in non-game settings, which differs from serious games and playful design. Success depends on whether the game elements amplify or replace the original motivation (self-determination theory, Goodhart’s law, social comparison).
  • Carse: a finite game is for winning, an infinite game is for continuing; Suits: playing a game is voluntarily overcoming unnecessary obstacles. Both are views of their own, to be used and also argued with.
  • Why they were proposed: everyone uses the word “game,” yet it is hard to pin down. These concepts appeared one after another, each answer leaving a new question. Only when games were brought into reality did the new problems of “the circle leaks, metrics get gamed” appear.
  • Procedural rhetoric: games speak through rules. A good “game with a viewpoint” writes the viewpoint into the rules, not into the lines.
A game is a voluntary, replayable enclosure within reality. Before taking game elements into reality, ask whether they amplify motivation or buy it away.
