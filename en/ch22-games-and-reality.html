Chapter 22

Games & Reality

Where is the edge of a game? Why do people argue over “what a game is”?

Learning goals

  • Explain who each of the “magic circle,” “gamification” and “finite and infinite games” came from, and say why people proposed them in the first place.
  • Explain why gamification sometimes works and sometimes backfires, and evaluate a gamification plan with a checklist of questions.
  • Point out that the boundary between “game” and “reality” grows thin in four directions (money, identity, relationships, irreversibility), and form your own view.

1

What it isThe magic circle: a “temporary world” marked out inside reality

A child pulls on a monster mask and chases a little brother around the yard, and the brother screams and runs, laughing until he can hardly breathe. Suddenly a real stray dog darts in at the gate, and the brother’s scream changes pitch: this time he is truly afraid.

The two screams sound much alike, yet one is a game and the other is not. What separates them? An agreement nobody said aloud and everybody understands: right now, we are playing. Once the agreement breaks, the same chase becomes a real fright.

In Homo Ludens (1938, first written in Dutch), the Dutch historian Johan Huizinga treated arenas, chessboards, stages, temples and courtrooms as one kind of place: marked off, temporary, with rules of their own. He called such a place a magic circle. Inside the circle are the circle’s rules: “capturing” the other’s piece is not violence, and losing a game is not failing at life.

In 2003 Salen and Zimmerman’s Rules of Play brought the term into game design. So the “magic circle” designers mean today is largely their retold and reworked version, narrower than what Huizinga originally discussed.

A game of chess: you “capture” a friend’s queen, he laughs and scolds you, and a minute later the pieces are set up again. The consequences inside the circle stay inside, and the circle is intact.
A boxing ring: the same punch is a match in the ring and a crime in the street. It is the “agreements everyone accepts,” like the ropes and the referee, that make it legal in the ring.
Playing the villain: an actor snarls on stage and shakes hands with the opponent after stepping down. Nobody takes the harsh words spoken on stage as his own thoughts.
Online-game gear that sells for real money: once gear has a price outside the game, the wall of the circle is punctured: account theft and fraud make the loss real. It is still a game, but no longer a pure circle.

What it isGamification: carrying game elements to non-game places

Open a vocabulary app, and the most prominent thing on the home page is a flame and “37-day streak.” You aren’t playing a game, yet you open it because you can’t bear to break that number. Using game elements like points, badges, leaderboards, levels, quest lines and streaks in non-game settings such as study, fitness and work is called gamification.

Around 2010 the word suddenly became fashionable among internet product people. But it was used loosely: is making a full teaching game gamification? Does adding points to a weekly report count? In their 2011 MindTrek paper, the four researchers Deterding, Dixon, Khaled and Nacke gave a definition that has been cited again and again: the use of game design elements in non-game contexts. (Deterding himself later said he had grown more and more doubtful about “what counts as a game design element.”)

The key to this definition is to separate it from several neighboring concepts. The picture below crosses “playing” and “gaming,” “whole” and “parts,” and each cell houses one kind of thing.

A vocabulary app’s streak: putting a “game element” (the number of days in a row) into study is typical gamification.
A flight simulator: the whole thing is itself a complete game, only used for training. This is a serious game, not gamification.
The piano stairs: in Volkswagen’s 2009 “fun theory” campaign, a Stockholm subway station turned its steps into piano keys, beside an escalator. The organizers said noticeably more people took the stairs (this is the campaign’s own figure and has not been peer reviewed). It has no points or ranking, and only puts the flavor of “play” into the stairs, closer to playful design.
A customer-service ticket leaderboard: it has points and ranking, the number handled outweighs quality, and everyone closes tickets in a rush. This is gamification gone wrong.

What it isExtension: finite and infinite games

Since the circle is drawn by agreement, a game inside it can live in two ways: to win, and break up once someone has won; or to keep the game going. The scholar of religion James P. Carse made exactly this split in Finite and Infinite Games (Free Press, 1986). A finite game aims to win, with clear participants, boundaries and an ending; an infinite game aims to keep the game going, nobody wants it to end, and the rules may change in order to continue. This is Carse’s classification, often borrowed into talk of business, politics and education, and some of those borrowings are quite disputed.

A World Cup final: it has an end and a winner and loser, a typical finite game. Precisely because the match will end, every minute is so tense.
Children playing house: no winner, and the rules change any time for “keeping the game going” (“now you be the dad”).
The slogan “our company will play an infinite game”: many business books simplify Carse’s classification into “finite is bad, infinite is good,” and it ends up as “never off work.” Competition in itself is not evil; this is a simplification of the original book.

How it was inventedWhy did the argument over “what a game is” arise?

The problem: a word everyone understands and nobody can pin down

In the early 20th century scholars began to study “play” seriously: it seemed to appear in war, law, poetry and sport, yet nowhere seemed to be its home. But to study it you must first answer: what counts as a game? Whoever offered a definition, someone raised a counterexample. This argument is not wordplay. The definition decides what you put into a “game design” course, and whether you dare bring games into schools and companies.

Guess first: who first proposed the “magic circle” that designers always talk about?

Below is a walk through in time. Each stop answers the gap the last one left: where the circle comes from, whether one definition can unify things, what the core of a game is, whether a game can have no end, and whether the circle can be carried out.

1938 Huizinga, Homo Ludens. The question: war, law, poetry, ritual… why do they all seem like “play”? His answer: games are a basic ingredient of culture, and people live temporarily by their own rules in a “marked-off place.” But how strict the circle is and where the rules come from, he did not pin down.
1953 Wittgenstein, Philosophical Investigations. The question: chess, cards, ball games, the Olympics… can a shared essence be found? He laid them out one by one, could not find it, and found only “family resemblance”: they resemble each other in different ways, with no single line running through the whole family. The new problem: with no unified essence, can a designer still have a tool that is good enough?
1978 Suits, The Grasshopper. His answer: playing a game is voluntarily attempting to overcome unnecessary obstacles. The rules deliberately block the easy path, the player accepts them freely, and that is a game. He also imagined that in a utopia where all needs are met, people would still play. This is Suits’s view; critics ask whether love and art can also be called “overcoming obstacles.”
1986 Carse, Finite and Infinite Games. The question: a match ends, but communities and cultures don’t, so how to tell them apart? The answer: a finite game is for winning, an infinite game for continuing. Later simplified by business books into slogans, it was distorted.
2003 Salen and Zimmerman, Rules of Play. Designers needed a common word for “the boundary between game and reality,” and so reworked Huizinga’s “circle” into a design tool, the “magic circle.” The new problem: when money and identity pour into digital games, does the circle still hold?
2011 Deterding et al., “Gamification: Toward a Definition.” Companies and apps were stuffing points and badges into products, and what was this? The answer: gamification is the use of game design elements in non-game contexts, different from making a whole game. The new problem: why are the results sometimes good and sometimes bad?

Each step answers the gap the last one left.

Why it worksWhy does gamification backfire?

The first trouble with carrying things out of the circle is rewards. Remember the experiment that gave children certificates? In 1973 Lepper, Greene and Nisbett had kindergarteners who loved drawing be told beforehand that “drawing earns a certificate,” and afterward, in free play, they drew less than the children who had been promised nothing. This is called the overjustification effect. Gamification is exactly handing out “certificates” for things, so it faces the same risk.

Amplifying: while memorizing words you see “30 more than last week,” and you want to go on. The reward says “you’ve improved,” which is feedback.
Replacing: a child who loved drawing, once promised a certificate, draws only for the certificate, and the interest is “bought off.” The reward says “do it the way I want,” which is control.
Gaming the number: to keep the “streak” going you open the app for one second every day and close it, and take in nothing. The number wins and the purpose loses.

Three principles, one reminder

Overjustification and self-determination theory (covered in the discussion of player motivation): Deci and Ryan’s self-determination theory holds that if a reward is perceived as control (“I do it to get the prize”), it weakens the sense of autonomy and lowers intrinsic motivation; if perceived as information (“I’m doing well”), it can strengthen the sense of competence. In 1999 Deci, Koestner and Ryan pooled 128 experiments and held that tangible, announced-in-advance external rewards generally weaken intrinsic motivation; Cameron and Pierce and others analyzed with another method and reached much milder conclusions, and the two sides still disagree.

Goodhart’s law (covered in the discussion of metrics): the “number of check-ins” on a leaderboard will be gamed, because people optimize the number and not what lies behind it. This is the mechanism behind the “gaming the number” example.

Sources: Lepper, Greene & Nisbett (1973), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 28(1): 129–137; Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999), Psychological Bulletin 125(6): 627–668; Deci & Ryan’s self-determination theory; Goodhart (1975), the wording popularized via Strathern (1997). The overjustification effect is disputed, and its size varies by situation: it is a “risk to watch for,” not an iron law.

The leaderboard is the most commonly used part in gamification, and it works through another principle: social comparison. Leon Festinger proposed in 1954 that people need to compare themselves with others to know how they are doing, and prefer to compare with those similar to themselves.

This explains why the person at rank 38 strives hard looking at rank 37, and feels nothing about rank 1; it also explains why a server-wide leaderboard often motivates only the people at the top and makes those in the middle and lower ranks want to quit. How the board is divided and who is compared with whom matter far more than “whether there is a board.”

Source: Leon Festinger, “A Theory of Social Comparison Processes,” Human Relations 7(2): 117–140 (1954).

Illustrative: whether gamification succeeds often depends on whether the game elements are “amplifying” the existing motivation or “replacing” it.

The real effects do “depend,” too. A 2014 review of empirical studies by Hamari, Koivisto and Sarsa found that gamification mostly has positive effects, but depends strongly on the context and the users.

Why does the magic circle “leak”?

The magic circle is a lovely ideal: all consequences inside the circle count only inside the circle. But real circles are often not closed. Edward Castronova, who studies virtual-world economies, in Synthetic Worlds (2005) called the boundary of a virtual world “almost-magic circle,” meaning the wall is porous.

It leaks in four directions: money (casino chips are real money; Diablo III launched in 2012 with an auction house where players could trade gear for real money, and Blizzard closed it in March 2014, saying it hurt the core fun of “killing monsters for loot”), identity (a pro player’s game skill is their profession and status), social consequences (betrayal in a game can make friends in real life drift away), and irreversibility (money lost gambling, years spent, cannot be redone).

Leaks are not all bad: esports gives people who love games a career path, and friendships made in games are real. The trouble is that when the consequences are borne by one side (the player bears the loss and the operator profits), or participants don’t realize the circle has been broken, “it’s just a game” becomes an excuse. How to use itWhat suits gamification?

Turn the principles above into judgments for design. The six questions below are a checklist put together from the earlier chapters (not an academic standard, and you may add or delete). Read them through, then go “turn the knobs” in the evaluator:

① Intrinsic interest

With all the points removed, would anyone still be willing to do it?

② A hard-to-game measure

Progress can be measured, and is not easy to game.

③ Voluntary

Voluntariness is an element of play: a forced “game” is only an assessment.

④ Low cost of failure

The virtue of the magic circle is the courage to experiment.

⑤ Rewards support, not replace

Like a coach’s affirmation, not “buying the behavior.”

⑥ Participants accept the measure

They don’t feel watched or controlled.

Using games to “make an argument”

Gamification carries games “out”; the other direction is letting the game speak. In Persuasive Games (2007) the game scholar Ian Bogost proposed procedural rhetoric: an essay persuades through argument, and a game persuades through rules. His criticism of gamification is sharp as well: just attaching “points and badges” without changing the experience of the rules grabs only the most superficial part of games, and in 2011 he half-mockingly called this “exploitationware.” This is his view, not an accepted conclusion.

The Landlord’s Game: Elizabeth Magie’s The Landlord’s Game won a US patent in 1904, meant to show how land monopoly makes landlords richer and tenants poorer, and to promote a land tax. The later Monopoly (1935) evolved from it. The argument isn’t in the lines but in the rules: play one round and you understand.
Putting the argument into a dialog box: the game pops up a long sermon, and the player’s reaction is to hit “skip.” If what you want to say never changes any of the player’s choices, it has not been played out.
The point to make→ What “system” lies behind it→ Turn the system into rules→ Let the player choose within the rules→ Experience the consequences

Also beware: the rules themselves carry the designer’s stance, and a player may reach the opposite conclusion to yours, or even learn “how to exploit loopholes” from them.

Sources: Elizabeth Magie’s patent is US Patent 748,626 (granted January 5, 1904); Parker Brothers published Monopoly in 1935. The details of who “invented” Monopoly differ across sources; here we say only that The Landlord’s Game is one of the earliest known versions.

Common misconceptions

  • “Adding points, badges and a leaderboard is gamification.” These are only the most superficial elements of games. Without a whole design of goals, choices and feedback, surface elements usually don’t last long.
  • “The magic circle is a wall, and what happens inside has nothing to do with reality.” The magic circle is more like a “consensus” that needs constant upkeep: participants must agree to it, designers must respect it, and once money and identity flow in heavily, the circle thins.
  • “A game must have a strict definition or it can’t be studied.” Wittgenstein reminds us that a concept can work through “family resemblance.” A definition is a tool, not a fence.
  • “Huizinga invented the magic circle of game design.” He talked about the broader places of “play”; the magic-circle concept commonly used in design was reworked by later writers.

Reflection and debate

Suits’s utopia: he held that in a world where needs are met, games remain a meaningful activity. Critics ask: does this shrink “a meaningful life” down to “overcoming artificial obstacles”? Can caring for children, art and love be seen as “games”? On the other hand, some say that if games matter so much, much of the labor in reality could be made more “playful,” which is the philosophical root of gamification.

Carse’s classification: many examples lie between the two; many healthy infinite games (a community, for instance) also contain many small finite games.

On gamification: Bogost’s criticism is one view; some researchers and practitioners also hold that, well designed, gamification really helps in fitness and learning. The actual effect depends on context, population and duration, and research conclusions are not uniform.

A question for you: if you had a child, would you want their homework, chores and exercise gamified? In your answer, which parts are considerations of “effectiveness” and which of “values”?

Key points

  • The magic circle (Huizinga’s “marked-off place,” brought into design by Salen and Zimmerman): a game marks out a temporary, rule-bound world within reality; it is held by consensus, and “leaks” in four directions: money, identity, relationships and irreversibility.
  • Gamification (Deterding et al., 2011): using game design elements in non-game contexts, different from serious games and playful design. Success depends on whether game elements amplify or replace existing motivation (self-determination theory, Goodhart’s law, social comparison).
  • Carse: finite games are for winning, infinite games for continuing; Suits: playing a game is voluntarily overcoming unnecessary obstacles. Both are “one view,” usable but arguable.
  • Why they were invented: these concepts arrived in a relay because “game” is a word everyone uses and nobody can pin down, each answer left a new gap, and only when games were carried into reality did new problems like “the circle leaks, measures get gamed” appear.
  • Procedural rhetoric: games speak through rules; a good “game with a point” writes the point into the rules and not into the lines.
A game is a voluntary, restartable fence in reality; before carrying it into reality, ask whether it is amplifying motivation or buying it off.
