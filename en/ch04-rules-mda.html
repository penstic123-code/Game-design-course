Chapter 4

Rules & MDA

What you write is rules; what players feel is an experience.

Learning goals

about 35 min
  • Say why the MDA framework was proposed, and place a design element in one of the three layers: mechanics, dynamics or aesthetics.
  • Explain three things: why one set of rules can run out into widely different experiences, why you should look at a “distribution” and not “one game,” and why “writing the rules right” does not equal “getting the behavior you want.”
  • Write a design hypothesis that can be taken to playtests for verification: the feeling you want → rules → the predicted dynamics.
1

What it isMDA: from a number to a mood

The designer says: “I changed the cat’s speed from 2.2 to 2.5.” The player says: “This game got so oppressive.”

The two sentences say one thing, yet a river runs between them. The designer stands on the left bank with only numbers and rules in hand. The player stands on the right bank with only a pounding heart and sweaty palms. There is no bridge in the river, only a few stepping stones: the rules must first be played out and become some behavior, and only then does behavior become feeling.

The MDA framework simply gives these stepping stones names. The one on the left bank is called mechanics, the one mid-river dynamics, and the one on the right bank aesthetics.

Concept card

The MDA framework

MDA framework: Mechanics–Dynamics–Aesthetics

Robin Hunicke, Marc LeBlanc and Robert Zubek · the 2004 paper “MDA: A Formal Approach to Game Design and Game Research”

A game is split into three layers: mechanics (rules and numbers), dynamics (the behavior that appears when the rules run) and aesthetics (what the player feels). Designers think from mechanics toward aesthetics, and players feel their way from aesthetics back toward mechanics.

ExampleThe cat’s speed is 2.5 (mechanics), the mouse finds that circling a pillar is safest (dynamics), and the player’s heart speeds up (aesthetics).

The third layer is the one people mix up most. Here “aesthetics” is not about whether the graphics look good; it is the feelings the player is stirred to: tension, curiosity, achievement, a knowing smile.

Click M, D and A above to see what each one is in the cat-and-mouse game.
Tension can’t be “designed” directly; you can only adjust the rules and then see how it feels to play. So playtesting is a must.

The same structure in three settings

Cat chasing mouse. Mechanics: cat speed 2.5, mouse 3.5. Dynamics: the mouse finds that running around a pillar keeps the cat always a step behind. Aesthetics: heart in the throat, yet you can’t help laughing.
Hide-and-seek. Mechanics: the seeker counts to 30 before opening their eyes, and can’t run once they do. Dynamics: hiders sprint far away first, and swap spots as soon as the seeker turns. Aesthetics: the tension of holding your breath, the shrieks and laughter when you’re found.
A subway station at rush hour. Mechanics: the gate lets through one person at a time, with queue lines painted on the floor. Dynamics: the crowd piles up in front of the gate, then spontaneously splits into two lines after it. Aesthetics: irritation, or the reassurance of “everyone follows the rules.”
Writing aesthetics as mechanics. A design document says “Mechanics: make the player feel tense.” That is a goal, not a rule, and it can’t be written straight into code. It has to be translated into a countdown, a pursuer, limited ammo, and then you predict what behavior those will produce.
Treat player complaints as editing instructions. A player says “this level is annoying”: that is a symptom at the aesthetics layer, not a prescription at the mechanics layer. Are the monsters too tough, the map too winding, or must you walk a long stretch again after dying? You have to trace back along A→D→M, and not simply halve some number.
2

What it is“Fun” isn’t one word: eight aesthetics

But “feeling” is too vague a word. Two people both say “fun”: one means laughing in a heap with friends, the other means finally beating a level, and these two kinds of fun have almost nothing in common.

The MDA paper gives “aesthetics” a vocabulary that splits “fun” into eight kinds. It is a taxonomy the authors put together. With it, people can say concretely “which kind our game mainly wants to give.”

Concept card

The eight aesthetics

The eight kinds of fun: sensation, fantasy, narrative, challenge, fellowship, discovery, expression, submission

Hunicke, LeBlanc and Zubek · the 2004 MDA paper (from LeBlanc’s “eight kinds of fun”)

“Fun” is split into sensation, fantasy, narrative, challenge, fellowship, discovery, expression and submission.

ExampleAnimal Crossing mainly offers fantasy, expression and submission and hardly relies on challenge; chess mainly offers challenge.

An Animal Crossing-style game. There is almost no winning or losing, yet people play for hundreds of hours: its main aesthetics lean toward fantasy, expression and submission, with almost no reliance on challenge.
Dodgeball, chess, the Rubik’s Cube. All called “fun”: one is about friendship and the senses, one about challenge, one about discovery. With the single word “fun,” the three can’t be told apart at all.
Treating the eight kinds as a single-choice question. To ask “which aesthetic does Minecraft really belong to?” is to ask wrongly: builders value expression, cave explorers value discovery, and online players value fellowship. The same game has a different dominant aesthetic for different players.
3

How it was inventedWhy MDA exists: game makers, coders and researchers talking past each other

What does “it isn’t fun” actually mean?

MDA took shape bit by bit at the Game Design and Tuning Workshop. From 2001 to 2004 the workshop ran every year at the Game Developers Conference (GDC) in San Jose, and its speakers included Marc LeBlanc and Robin Hunicke.

The paper opens by naming what gave them headaches: people who make games, people who write game criticism and people who do technical research each have their own language, with a gulf between them. Programmers stare at data structures and algorithms, designers say “this isn’t fun,” and critics talk about “experience.” The paper also reminds us that even for those working on game AI, a seemingly trivial algorithmic decision climbs all the way up and affects the final play.

Nobody could say clearly how a change in rules becomes the player’s feeling. The authors wanted to move “from words like ‘fun’ and ‘gameplay’ toward more directed vocabulary,” building a bridge among design and development, game criticism and technical research.

They also made an observation: when a book or film is finished, its content is fixed. A game’s content is behavior, which happens only when played, and no one can fully foresee it when it is done.

Try it first: without the word “fun,” can you say what separates them?

Pick a game and tick the aesthetics you think it mainly offers (several allowed), then click “Reveal the paper’s labels.” The paper’s own four games are each labeled with several items, and the four lists all differ. With only the one word “fun,” none of these differences could be said.

Their approach was to fill in the missing piece between “rules” and “feelings”:

  • Early attempt: only “fun / not fun.” When the team argued, nobody could tell whether people were disputing numbers, player behavior or feelings.
  • Key insight: between rules and feelings a word was missing, and it is the behavior that appears once the rules run, which is to say the dynamics. With it, rules can connect to feelings.
  • MDA’s three lenses: the designer sees the order M→D→A, and the player exactly the reverse: first feeling A, then slowly working out D and M.
2001–2004 The GDC “Game Design and Tuning Workshop” runs every year, and MDA takes shape step by step in teaching and discussion.
April 2004 LeBlanc gives a talk at Northwestern University, “Mechanics, Dynamics, Aesthetics” (listed in the paper’s references).
July 2004 The paper is presented at the AAAI-04 workshop on Challenges in Game AI (San Jose, USA) and collected in AAAI Technical Report WS-04-04.
Since then It has become one of the most cited frameworks in teaching and industry conversation, and has also been criticized again and again as “too simple,” prompting many extended versions.

Source: Robin Hunicke, Marc LeBlanc, Robert Zubek, “MDA: A Formal Approach to Game Design and Game Research,” 2004; for dates and workshop information see the AAAI-04 workshop list.

4

Why it worksWhy does one set of rules give a thousand different faces?

Designers of course want to get it right in one go: work out a number that makes every player tense. But in the very same “cat chases mouse,” one person learns to circle a pillar and another, greedy for cheese, is cornered against a wall. Not one word of the rules changed, yet the behavior differs, and the mood differs even more.

Why can’t rules govern behavior? Because the step from mechanics to dynamics hides two layers of uncertainty.

First uncertainty: rules “fight” each other, and a small change can cause a qualitative shift

Rules affect one another, so the effect of changing one number is hard to work out in your head. Such a relation is nonlinear.

The linked chart ahead shows it. Speed the cat up bit by bit from 1.7 and “tense” only slowly gives way to “frustrating,” a very gentle change. But from 3.4 to 3.5 the cat chases as fast as the mouse, and every game suddenly falls into “hopeless.” The same +0.1 is barely noticeable the first time and completely different the second.

There is another common amplifier. In Monopoly, the one with more property collects more rent, and with the rent can buy even more property. This causal loop of “the result strengthens the cause” is a feedback loop, and one that widens gaps is positive feedback.

A slight lead at the start becomes an overwhelming advantage after dozens of turns, yet no rule says “let the leader win.” This phenomenon of “present in the whole, absent from any single rule” is emergence.

Sources:
Donella Meadows, 2008, Thinking in Systems, on feedback loops and system behavior.
Elizabeth Magie, 1904, patented The Landlord’s Game, the predecessor of Monopoly; she meant to show how “ground rent makes landlords richer and richer” (who invented Monopoly is disputed). The MDA paper also takes Monopoly as one of its subjects of analysis.

Second uncertainty: players differ, and so does their luck

Skills, preferences and opening luck all differ, so the same rules always give a pile of results, not one result. A designer’s habit is to look at the distribution of results: let many games run and see where most people land. In engineering a Monte Carlo-style approach is common: have automated players run hundreds of games, then count. For real people, we rely on playtesting.

Verification experiment: the linked chart lets you verify it by hand. Turn one knob and mechanics, dynamics and aesthetics change together. The dynamics layer is the distribution of 30 games, not one game.

Source: nonlinearity and randomness are general concepts of systems science and statistics; running many simulations to see the distribution of results is common practice in game balancing. “A game’s content is behavior” comes from the MDA paper (2004).

Monopoly drags on. The leader gets richer and richer, the trailer has almost no way back, and in the second half everyone knows who will win and the tension is gone. The problem is in the dynamics (the gap is amplified), and what needs changing is the mechanics.
The same exam. With one paper, some find it “just challenging enough,” some “all freebies,” some “can’t do any of it.” The setter should look at the distribution of the whole class’s scores, not at a single student.
Looking only at the average. “Average user rating of 3 stars” sounds like “okay,” but it could be half the people giving 5 stars and half giving 1 star, with nobody at the average. The average hides the distribution, and hides that “half the people are leaving.”

Why it worksThe rules are written correctly, so why is the behavior still wrong?

There is a more awkward case still: the rules are right to the letter, yet the behavior you get is completely unwanted. Tell a robot to “get the room clean,” judged by “no things visible on the floor,” and it stuffs everything under the sofa. It broke none of the rules.

This is another pit between mechanics and dynamics: rules are what the designer wrote down, the intent is what was in mind, and between the two there is always some loss in translation. A player or program that seriously “maximizes” will mercilessly find that gap.

Specification gaming: exploiting the written word, not the intent

AI research calls this specification gaming. In late 2016, OpenAI researchers used reinforcement learning to train a program to play the boat-racing game CoastRunners, meaning it to win the race. The game’s score came from hitting targets along the way.

The program found a small lagoon: the targets there respawned repeatedly, and circling to hit them scored more than finishing the whole course. By OpenAI’s account, its average score was about 20% above human players, yet it never went to the finish line.

Victoria Krakovna and colleagues at DeepMind gathered dozens of such examples in 2020. They pointed out that the exploit arises because the reward function was written wrong, and the program is not “bad.” When a designer treats rules as the player’s goal, the player may really optimize to the letter.

Sources:
OpenAI, December 2016, training a program to play CoastRunners: it circled hitting targets, scored about 20% above human players, and never went to the finish (“Faulty Reward Functions in the Wild”).
Krakovna et al., April 2020, a DeepMind blog post gathering dozens of similar cases: exploits come from a badly written reward function (“Specification gaming: the flip side of AI ingenuity”).

Concept card

Specification gaming

Specification gaming (also called reward hacking)

OpenAI · the 2016 CoastRunners experiment; Krakovna et al. at DeepMind · cases gathered in 2020

A system earns a high score by the literal wording of the goal, without doing what the designer really wanted.

ExampleA boat-racing program circles in a lagoon hitting targets that keep reappearing, scores more than by finishing the course, and never goes to the finish line.

Boat circling. The spec is “hit targets for points,” the intent is “win the race.” Targets reappear in the lagoon, circling pays better than finishing the course, so the program circles without a qualm.
Imagine a customer-service center: the review looks only at “the shorter the average call time the better.” The operators soon find that hanging up is the best deal. The numbers look good and not one problem is solved. (This is an imagined case for illustration, not a real event.)
Pushing the blame onto “cheaters”. Crying “it cheats” whenever a player or program exploits a loophole often looks in the wrong place. The player is only playing the given rules seriously, and what should be fixed is the specification.
🚤 Adjust the reward: make the AI choose to finish the course

A program that only pursues “highest total score” faces two routes: finish the 45-second course, or circle in the lagoon (hitting 3 targets every 6 seconds). The race is limited to 60 seconds. There are also 3 targets along the course. The numbers are illustrative, not real game data.

Turn it into a workflow: three steps to a design hypothesis

  1. Write the aesthetics (A) first: “I want most players to feel tense, but not desperate.”
  2. Then write the rules (M) and predict the dynamics (D): “The cat is a bit slower than the mouse, and adding cheese makes players willing to take risks; expect some players to circle and some to be cornered.”
  3. Take it to a playtest and look at the distribution: the goal is “most people land in the area you want,” not “everyone is tense.” If not, go back to step 2 and adjust the rules.

Common misconceptions

  • “Dynamics” = on-screen motion effects. No: dynamics are the patterns of behavior that appear when the rules run (circling, camping a spot), not animation.
  • “One playtest is enough.” One game is only a point in a distribution, and you need to look at many people and many games.
  • “MDA can compute the experience.” It helps people discuss experience and can’t predict it for them.
  • “Aesthetics = looks nice.” Here aesthetics means the player’s emotional response, not art style.
  • “Just write the rules right.” Rules are the letter, the intent is in the designer’s mind, and a serious player will find the behavior in the gap.

Reflection and debate

MDA has spread widely and is often criticized: it treats players as passive “receivers” and ignores context such as culture, community and narrative. The eight-way division is not an accepted, complete classification either: even in the paper’s own labeling for Quake, the word “competition,” which is outside the eight kinds, appears.

Some also point out that much of what is called “mechanics” itself carries aesthetic coloring. Several extensions or alternatives have appeared since, such as DPE (Design, Play, Experience) for serious games, MDAO, and the DDE (Design, Dynamics, Experience) some have proposed. The field has not taken MDA as the final draft.

“A designer cannot design the experience directly, only influence it indirectly” is the view of the MDA authors. Some designers also stress that in narrative-driven games the designer’s control over the experience is far stronger. The “machine players” in the linked chart are crude, and real people behave far more intricately; simulation can only help find “places worth playtesting.”

Think about it: if two people feel completely opposite things about the same round, is it a problem with the rules, or with the players?

Key points

  • Mechanics can be written out; dynamics appear only when it runs; aesthetics are what the player feels.
  • MDA was proposed because designers, programmers and researchers lacked a shared language for saying “how rules become feelings.” It fills in the “dynamics” layer and splits “fun” into eight aesthetics.
  • Designers think from M to A, and players feel their way from A back to M.
  • Rules affect one another nonlinearly and feedback loops amplify gaps, so changing one number can transform the experience, which only playtesting can confirm.
  • One set of rules produces a distribution of experiences, not a single point. A written specification isn’t the intent either, and a serious player will exploit the gap.
Rules are written by the designer, and the experience is “run out” by the players. One set of rules runs out a whole distribution, so look at the distribution in playtests to calibrate the feeling you want.
