Chapter 25 · Case study

The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild

Anywhere you can see, you can go; the rules hold everywhere; and the play grows on its own.

about 40 min

Learning goals

  • Explain the problem the team faced back then (traditional Zelda was “passive”), and what “sightline guidance,” “multiplicative gameplay” and “breakable weapons” were each meant to solve.
  • Use emergence, autonomy, information gaps, opportunity cost and feedback loops to explain “why this design works,” and tell which parts are what the developers said and which are inference.
  • Tell a successful case apart from “copy it and it will work,” and write down what a small team should cut first when borrowing from it.
1

What it isA mountain, a tower, and you want to go look

Many people climbed some little hill near home as kids. Nobody asked them to go up; they just saw something on the top and wanted to know what was on the other side.

The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild (released on Wii U and Switch on March 3, 2017, by Nintendo) wants to keep the player in this state all the time. Starting from a high platform, the player can walk almost straight to any place in sight, and nobody is rushing them to do quests. The director was Hidemaro Fujibayashi and the producer Eiji Aonuma.

Its design rests on three ideas: draw the player to explore with sight lines and terrain; keep the physics and “chemistry” rules consistent across the whole world, so players come up with plays the designers didn’t write out one by one; and make weapons break, forcing trade-offs. In public talks the developers called the second “multiplicative gameplay.”

String the player’s ordinary state together and you get a core loop: see a landmark in the distance → head there → interact with the world’s rules along the way → find something → climb high and look again for the next place you want to go.

The loop starts from the player’s own curiosity, not from a quest list.

2

What it isLine-of-sight guidance: the mountain is the cover, the tower tip is the invitation

Open worlds have an old problem: when you can go anywhere, you don’t know where to go. So the designers have to make the world “speak” for itself. Breath of the Wild does it with terrain.

Nintendo developers described a “triangle rule” at CEDEC in Japan in 2017 (according to Matt Walker’s English translation and several retellings): think of obstacles as triangles, and the player always has two choices facing them: climb over, or go around. At the same time a triangle blocks the view, so what is behind it can be kept as a surprise.

Think of obstacles as triangles: the player can always climb over or go around, and a triangle blocks the view, so what is behind it can be kept as a surprise.

ExampleA mountain hides half the view and a tower tip pokes over the crest: the player sees from afar a place they want to go, without knowing what is there.

The division of the three sizes is according to Matt Walker’s English retelling: the biggest is landmarks, the medium blocks the view, and the smallest regulates walking pace. The figure is illustrative.

The terrain rises and falls: it hides the whole answer and leaves a goal tall enough to see.

A tower tip poking over a hill (in the game)The hill hides half of it, the tower tip rises above the crest, and the player sees from afar a place they want to go, without knowing what is there. The goal is chosen by the player, and no quest is pushing.
A winding path in a park (daily life)A good garden path doesn’t let you see the end at a glance: it bends, a corner of a roof shows behind the trees, and you want to go on. The principle is the same as the tower tip.
Counterexample: seeing it all at once If the terrain is completely flat and every landmark is visible with nothing blocking, there is no suspense and no reason to “go and see.” The triangle exists to avoid exactly this kind of terrain.
Counterexample: visible but unreachable A landmark placed far away yet stopped by an invisible wall, and the player quickly realizes the world’s “promise” doesn’t count. A distant goal must have a way to get there, and only then does “see it, go there” hold.
3

What it isMultiplicative gameplay: few rules, many combinations

The second idea has nothing to do with terrain; it is about “how objects react to each other.” If a game has N kinds of elements (fire, wind, water, electricity…) and M kinds of objects (grass, wood, metal…), and the rules hold everywhere for them, about N×M interactions arise, while the designer needs to make only N+M pieces, with no need to hand-write a contraption for every scene.

Concept card

Multiplicative Gameplay

Multiplicative gameplay

Hidemaro Fujibayashi, Satoru Takizawa, Takuhiro Dohta · GDC talk, 2017

A few rules that hold everywhere multiply with one another and produce far more interactions than the designers could list.

ExampleFire burns the grass, hot air rises, and opening the paraglider lifts the player into the sky: no special level was built for it, the rules multiplied and it “grew.”

Fire × grass → updraft (in the game)Fire burns the grass, hot air rises, and opening the paraglider lets you fly up. The designers didn’t build a level for this; it “grew” out of rules multiplying.
LEGO bricks (daily life) With just a few basic bricks and one rule, “they snap together,” a child builds towers, bridges and houses, none of them prescribed in the instructions.
Counterexample: a one-off contraption “The key opens this door” is a hand-made single puzzle: only one solution, no value once used, so a hundred puzzles take a hundred pieces of work. The narrower the solution, the less room the player has to invent.
Counterexample: inconsistent rules The same grass lights with a touch here and won’t burn however you try there. The player’s guesses then have no value, and so they dare not experiment. Consistent rules are the precondition of this design.
4

What it isWeapons break: make every blow worth a thought

The third idea is the most controversial: weapons have durability and break when used up. If you use one to cut ordinary monsters, there is less left for tough enemies. Every swing is a trade-off between “use it now” and “use it later,” so choices carry weight.

The weapon nearly gone (game) The sword in your hand has a little durability left, and ahead is a stronger enemy. Keep chopping the small fry in front, or save it? No standard answer, and the player must weigh it.
A backpack that holds only three things (daily life)Heading out to climb a hill with a limited pack, you must decide whether to bring water, an umbrella or snacks. Putting one in means giving up another.
Counterexample: an unbreakable artifact If there were a strongest weapon that never broke, the player would of course use only it, and the choices would vanish. Using the same move against every enemy, after a while only repetition is left.

The developers’ own explanation comes from interviews in late 2017. On the Nintendo Power Podcast, Fujibayashi said the team wanted players to be drawn into adventuring in this open world. If players are left to do as they please, they often don’t know what to do, so different enemies drop different weapons to steer players into looking for them.

Aonuma said durability makes combat more strategic, so players can’t rely only on their favorite move. Players’ reactions were split: some liked this trade-off, some found it annoying, and Game Informer ran a piece criticizing it at the time.

5

How it was inventedThe problem the team faced back then, and how they changed it

From “passive” to “active”

Development of Breath of the Wild started after Skyward Sword (2011). At GDC (the Game Developers Conference) in March 2017, Fujibayashi, with art director Satoru Takizawa and technical director Takuhiro Dohta, explained the team’s starting point: the Zelda series was a very “passive” game, with the order of events fixed in advance and walls on the way you couldn’t pass; the player seemed free but was really walking a predetermined route. They wanted to turn it into an “active” game.

Guess first. To get a team of a few dozen people to quickly grasp what is fun about “objects reacting to each other,” what did they do first?

The starting point Traditional Zelda has “order” and “walls,” so the player is always led by the hand. Fujibayashi said this is the illusion of freedom. So they made walls climbable and the sky a way to glide back down, and only then did “where to go” begin to be the player’s decision.
The prototype Fujibayashi went back and played the original The Legend of Zelda, and what impressed him most was: “every time the screen scrolls, there is something new to discover.” They made a small 2D prototype that looked like the old 8-bit Zelda, with the aim of quickly showing colleagues that “objects react to each other.” The team later explained that this “2D” prototype actually ran on a 3D engine, with the physics and chemistry systems the game finally used behind it.
Multiplication Past puzzles were mostly separate contraptions, made one by one at great labor. What they wanted was a few mechanisms multiplied over and over across the whole world: objects react to the player, and objects react to one another. In the prototype there was just one simple rule: “there is a goal; can you get to it?”
Art Consistent rules weren’t enough; the player had to “understand them at a glance.” Art director Takizawa changed the style on the basis of The Wind Waker HD, feeling that the original overly exaggerated cartoon look made it harder to think of physics and chemistry play. Some presentations are deliberately not realistic: a log cut through instantly turns into a tied bundle of firewood; food bounces in the pot like a rubber ball. He later said that on seeing such “absurdity” he felt it was fun.
The world The last problem to solve was how to lead the player into the vast world. According to Japanese press reports of the CEDEC 2017 talk, the developers used “gravity” to explain how the player is guided: a structure that matters to the story has high gravity, and players naturally want to head there. Placing landmarks and shaping terrain is a way of giving different parts of the world pulls of different strength.

Taken together, these steps show what the team had worked out: freedom means letting each of the player’s wishes land, and opening all the doors is not enough, nor is a bigger world. A visible goal must be reachable, an experiment you’ve done must give a consistent result, and what you use must have weight. The three designs answer the same question.

Why it worksWhy is this design effective?

The five explanations below each cite a source. They explain it afterward through principles; they are not the designers’ words, nor a guarantee that “doing the same will work.”

① Emergence and the consistency of rules

Ants are the most common example of emergence: a single ant can only walk, leave a scent and follow scents, and no ant knows the whole foraging route. Yet a colony together “grows” a road to the food. The physicist P. W. Anderson wrote a very short article in 1972 titled “More Is Different”: at each higher level, new properties appear that cannot simply be derived from the level below.

It is the same in games: fire, grass, wind and metal each have a few simple rules, and when the rules multiply, plays like “riding an updraft up” pop out that nobody designed specially. But emergence has a precondition: the rules must be consistent. If fire burns here and not there, the player’s guesses have no value. The game scholar Jesper Juul divides game structure into “emergence” and “progression,” and this game is often taken as a representative of the former.

Sources of the principle: P. W. Anderson · 1972 · “More Is Different” · at each higher level, new properties appear that cannot simply be derived from the level below (Science 177(4047): 393–396). Jesper Juul · 2005 · Half-Real (MIT Press) · distinguishes games of emergence from games of progression.

Concept card

Games of Emergence and Games of Progression

Games of emergence and games of progression

Jesper Juul · The Open and the Closed (2002), Half-Real (2005)

Games of emergence get a large amount of variety from the combination of a few rules; games of progression move forward on content the designer has arranged level by level.

ExampleChess and Breath of the Wild are often taken as representatives of emergence; an adventure game that advances through its story level by level is closer to progression.

② Self-determination theory: a sense of autonomy

Chapter 3 covered three basic needs, and one of them is autonomy: feeling “I chose this myself” makes intrinsic motivation stronger. Seeing a tower, deciding yourself where to go, with no quest list pushing, gives exactly the sense of autonomy. This theory explains motivation itself; using it to infer a game’s sales goes beyond what it can account for.

Source of the principle: Deci & Ryan, Intrinsic Motivation and Self-Determination in Human Behavior (1985).

③ Curiosity’s information gap: the tower tip behind the mountain

Information-gap theory holds that curiosity comes from “I know a little, but not everything.” The mountain hides half and the tower tip shows half, which is a just-right gap: too small and there is no interest, too big and you don’t know where to begin asking. This theory has fairly good experimental support, though applied to the layout of a particular level it is only one interpretation.

Source of the principle: George Loewenstein, “The psychology of curiosity: A review and reinterpretation,” Psychological Bulletin 116(1): 75–98 (1994).

④ Opportunity cost: weapons break

Weapons have durability: using one on ordinary monsters leaves less for strong enemies. Each strike makes the player weigh “use it now” against “use it later,” and so choices carry weight. As said earlier, players’ reactions to this design split widely, so “effective” depends on the person.

Source of the principle: the economic concept of opportunity cost; the developers’ explanation is in the late-2017 Nintendo Power Podcast interview with Fujibayashi and Aonuma (per Zelda Dungeon’s retelling).

⑤ Feedback loop: calibrating the world with heat maps

Towers guide the crowd, the crowd is recorded by heat maps, the team moves towers and adds small structures accordingly, and the new crowd is recorded again. This is a balancing loop: deviation (the crowd too squeezed into one line) is pulled back again and again. It is the same structure as the player’s small “guess → try → see the result” loop in the game: output first, then observed, then fed back to change the input.

Source of the principle: Donella Meadows, Thinking in Systems (Chelsea Green, 2008), on the distinction between balancing and reinforcing loops; the term “gravity” is per Japanese press reports of the CEDEC 2017 talk.

Design tricks you can borrow

① “If you can see it, you can reach it”

Put goals in conspicuous places far away, and give the player a way to get there (climb, glide, run). A promise must be kept for players to trust the world.

② Few, consistent rules

Rather than writing a hundred special cases, make ten rules that hold everywhere. Consistency lets players dare to “guess and verify.”

③ Let players be the “inventors”

Designers don’t need to write out every solution, only to guarantee that “a way exists.” Multiple solutions make each person’s story different.

④ Calibrate “gravity” with data

Don’t place landmarks by feel: look at the heat map, find where players squeeze into a single line, then adjust and test again.

Key points

  • The problem: traditional Zelda was “passive,” with fixed order and walls in the way. The team wanted an “active” game where the player decides where to go and what to do.
  • Line-of-sight guidance: with terrain hiding and revealing at different heights, players set goals for themselves, matching the explorer motive, autonomy and the information gap.
  • Systemic rules produce emergence: the same mechanisms combined in different situations give far more dynamics than the designers’ list; consistent rules are the precondition for players to dare to experiment.
  • Breakable weapons give choices weight, but players’ reactions are polarized; heat maps and “gravity” spread the crowd across the world, which is a feedback loop.
  • Guidance is not the same as coercion: the designers “lay the road, light the lamps”; they don’t drag the player along.

Common misconceptions

  • “Systemic means anything goes”: consistent rules take great discipline; every rule added multiplies the interactions, and the testing multiplies too.
  • “Open world = nothing to design”: behind the freedom is a great deal of “guidance” (sight lines, terrain, object placement).
  • “The case shows the one right way”: it is one direction; linear narrative and strongly guided games can be just as excellent.
  • “The chemistry rule table above is the game’s real rules”: it is only a simplified version for teaching.

Think about it: survivorship bias

What you read is the success story of Breath of the Wild (it won Game of the Year at The Game Awards 2017). Countless games that tried “open world + physics and chemistry systems” did not get the same result, and few people take them as cases. So “it was designed this way, therefore it succeeded” may carry survivorship bias: team size, timing, brand and overall polish matter just as much.

Write one sentence: if a small team wanted to borrow its “systemic rules,” what most needs to be cut or simplified in advance?

Breath of the Wild breeds emergence from “a few consistent rules,” sparks curiosity with sightlines and information gaps, calibrates the world with heat maps, and gives choices weight with autonomy and trade-offs. It is a much-discussed success case, not a universal recipe.
