Chapter 5

Emergence & Systems Thinking

What you write down is rules, and what the player gets is another world.

Learning goals

about 40 min
  • Explain emergence in your own words, tell positive from negative feedback, and say what questions the Game of Life and the “predator–prey model” were originally meant to answer.
  • Explain two things: why negative feedback with a delay swings back and forth, and why someone who only “climbs uphill” gets stuck on a small hill.
  • Judge whether a system problem calls for “changing the numbers” or “changing the feedback structure,” and design rules more likely to grow interesting play.
1

What it isEmergence: simple rules, a complex whole

At dusk a great flock of starlings wheels across the sky like a flowing black curtain. Who is directing them?

The answer often given is: nobody. Each bird does just three things: don’t bump the birds beside you, fly in about the same direction, don’t stray too far from the flock. All three rules care only about the few birds nearby, and none mentions “the shape of the whole flock.” Put thousands of such birds together and a huge pattern that no single bird “knows” appears.

The whole has behavior that the parts lack, and this behavior comes from the interactions between the parts. This is emergence.

ExampleEach starling only avoids bumping into neighbors, flies the same way as neighbors and stays with the flock. Put thousands together and they fly a huge wheeling pattern.

Designed

What the designer writes directly: rules, numbers, assets. They can be counted one by one.

Emerged

What “grows” out of rules interacting: tactics, schools of play, economic bubbles, unexpected play. They can’t be counted, only observed.

Emergence in games

Board games. The rules of chess fit on one page, yet they grew opening theory, endgame studies and schools of play. No rule says “control the center”; players “discovered” it by running into the rules again and again.
Redstone in Minecraft. Redstone is a material that carries signals, with plain rules, yet players built logic gates, calculators and even complete computers with it. None of this was on the design list.
Combos in fighting games. Noritaka Funamizu, producer of Street Fighter II, found while checking for bugs that another hit could be chained at a certain timing. The team later relaxed the detection and made it an official feature. An interaction nobody expected became the cornerstone of a whole genre (sources differ somewhat on the details).

Emergence beyond games

The computer version of a flock: Boids. Craig Reynolds introduced it in his 1987 SIGGRAPH paper “Flocks, Herds, and Schools.” Each “boid” obeys just three local rules: separation (don’t get too close), alignment (head the same way as neighbors), cohesion (move toward the neighbors’ center). There is no leader, and a lifelike flock comes out.
Phantom traffic jams. In 2008 Sugiyama and colleagues ran an experiment in Japan: twenty-odd cars drove at about the same speed on a ring road with no obstacle. A few minutes later a jam wave appeared on its own and traveled against the direction of the traffic. The jam is the result of the cars’ interaction, and a roadblock is only a trigger.
Counterexample: a pile of things is not emergence. A level holds 50 monsters, each patrolling on its own script and ignoring one another. That is just “a lot of them.” Emergence needs interaction: monsters, monsters and player, player and environment must be able to pull on each other.
Counterexample: “random” is not emergence. The chaos of rolling dice has no “new overall structure.” The Game of Life later has no randomness at all, yet grows gliders that walk. That is emergence.

What it isThe first tool for making the whole “come alive”: feedback loops

In Monopoly, collecting rent lets you buy more property, and more property collects more rent. A result that circles back to affect its cause is a structure called a feedback loop. It has two basic forms:

  • Positive feedback (a reinforcing loop): change is amplified. More money, better gear, more wins, even more money. The result is a snowball, winner-takes-all, or a crash.
  • Negative feedback (a balancing loop): change is cancelled out and the system is pulled toward a target. Body too hot, you sweat; temperature falls, sweating stops.
Concept card

Positive and negative feedback

Positive feedback and negative feedback

Norbert Wiener · Cybernetics, 1948, which set out feedback as a general concept

A result comes back to affect its cause: one that amplifies change is positive feedback, and one that pulls change back toward a target is negative feedback.

ExampleIn a competitive game, kills buy gear and gear brings more kills, which is positive feedback. A thermostat that turns the heating off when it is hot and on when it is cold is negative feedback.

A + beside an arrow means “when the first goes up, the second goes up too,” and − means “when the first goes up, the second goes down.” The whole loop on the left is all +, so it grows ever larger; the loop on the right has one −, which pulls the change back a bit on each lap.

Snowballing in a competitive game. Kills give gold, gold buys gear, and gear brings more kills. The first lead is small, but a few minutes later it is an overwhelming advantage.
Recommendations on a video platform. Videos with high view counts are pushed to more people, and more viewers push the count higher. Nobody decides “this video should go viral”; the loop lifts it by itself.
A thermostat. When the room gets hot the heating goes off, when it gets cold the heating comes on, and the temperature is always pulled back to the setting. This is the classic negative feedback.
The catch-up items in Mario Kart. Players further behind are likelier to get a powerful item, pulling the gap back (the official weights aren’t public and differ by title). Those behind therefore have a chance to stay in the race.
Counterexample: negative feedback overdone. If the catch-up mechanic is too strong, every bit of the leader’s effort is flattened, and players feel “leading means nothing.” This is where “rubber-band” mechanics are often criticized. Negative feedback is for helping those failing stay in the race, not for erasing success for the successful.
3

What it isStocks and flows: why big systems turn slowly

Start with a bank balance. Pay is the faucet, a little flowing in each month. Rent and meals are the drain, a little flowing out each month. The total lying in the account at this moment is the real money.

The total in the account is the stock, and the speed at which money comes in and goes out each month is the flow. Health, gold and monster counts in a game are all stocks.

A stock can change only through flows, bit by bit, and the water level in a bathtub can’t double in an instant. So a system with a big stock reacts slowly and isn’t easily knocked down by a single shock.

4

What it isDelay and oscillation: why well-meant adjustments swing wilder and wilder

Put negative feedback together with a stock and a counterintuitive phenomenon pops out. Think of taking a shower: the water is too cold, so you turn the tap toward hot. The pipe still holds cold water, which hasn’t warmed, so you turn it again. When the hot water finally arrives, it is scalding, and you hurry to turn back, and the water slowly turns cold again…

The reason is that there is a delay between action and result. This lag keeps negative feedback always half a beat late, so the system swings back and forth around its target, which is oscillation.

Shower temperature. There is a delay of several seconds between turning the tap and the hot water actually arriving. People adjust by the water temperature “now,” yet affect the temperature several seconds later, and so swing back and forth between cold, scalding, cold.
Tides in a game economy. Monsters are wiped out, drops decrease, players move elsewhere, monsters pile up at the respawn points, and the spawn points fill up with players again. Prices, dungeon popularity and class strength on a server often rise and fall like this.
The Beer Game. This is a teaching game from MIT in which participants play four stations, retailer, wholesaler, distributor and factory, placing orders and waiting for shipments with delays in between. John Sterman of MIT ran experiments with it in 1989 and found the swings in orders grew larger from one station to the next, because people often overlook that their ordering decisions in turn affect the system.
Counterexample: no lag, no swinging. If the water temperature changed the instant the tap was turned, you could soon hold it steady where you want. The culprit of the swings is reacting too slowly, and negative feedback itself is not at fault.

How it was inventedThe Game of Life: how far can the simplest rules go?

A game “nobody would actually play,” and why it became the emblem of emergence

In the 1940s John von Neumann and Stanisław Ulam were studying “cellular automata”: cut the world into squares, each square interacting with its neighbors by rules. Von Neumann wanted to know whether a machine could build itself. The cellular machine he designed had frighteningly complicated rules.

Twenty-some years later, the Cambridge mathematician John Conway asked it another way: with only extremely simple rules, can we still get surprising behavior? Over the following years he tried all sorts of two-dimensional rules with pen and paper. In October 1970 Martin Gardner introduced it in his “Mathematical Games” column in Scientific American. Letters from readers poured in, and everyone began to study the patterns.

The word “move” is nowhere in the rules, yet by generation 4 you will find that the whole pattern has shifted one cell toward the lower right. This walking pattern is what people later called the glider.

Conway then conjectured that no pattern can grow without limit, and offered a 50-dollar prize for a proof. In November the same year, Bill Gosper’s group at MIT found a “glider gun” that spits out a glider every 30 generations, and the conjecture was overturned.

The 1982 book Winning Ways then gave an argument that the Game of Life can build a universal computer.

How it was inventedThe puzzle of the Adriatic catch

With no fishing, why were there more big fish?

During the First World War (around 1915–1918), fishermen on the Adriatic could hardly go out to fish. After the war the Italian marine biologist Umberto D’Ancona went through the fish-market data and found something odd: with less fishing, the share of fish that eat other fish (mainly sharks and rays) in the catch actually grew. Isn’t not fishing good for all fish?

He gave the problem to a mathematician, his father-in-law Vito Volterra. Volterra built the simplest two-species equations, published in Nature in 1926. The American Alfred Lotka had already written the same equations in his 1925 book, so it is called the “Lotka–Volterra model.”

Before seeing how he explained it, guess: if rabbits and foxes are both removed in proportion (standing for “fishing”) and then fishing stops, will the foxes’ share of all the animals go up, go down, or stay the same?

Advanced: how the model computes

A discrete-time simulation. In each small step, rabbits increase by (birth rate − fishing) × rabbits and decrease by predation efficiency × rabbits × foxes. Foxes increase by 0.5 × predation efficiency × rabbits × foxes (0.5 is the share of eaten rabbits turned into new foxes) and decrease by (death rate + fishing) × foxes.

The pattern Volterra’s model gives is: fishing presses down both populations at once, but hurts the predator more. Once fishing stops, the foxes’ average rises, the rabbits’ average falls, and the predators’ share goes up. Ecology calls this conclusion the “Volterra principle.”

1925 Lotka sets out the predator–prey equations in Elements of Physical Biology.
1926 To explain D’Ancona’s catch data, Volterra independently builds a model, published in Nature; Lotka later points out his priority, and the model gets its name.
1942 Elton and Nicholson compile the Hudson’s Bay Company’s fur purchase records, in which the lynx–snowshoe hare cycle is widely cited, with the lynx peak always a little after the hare’s.
1995 Krebs and colleagues ran an eight-year field experiment at Kluane in the Yukon, giving snowshoe hares extra food and fencing out predators, and found that the hares’ cycle can’t be put down to predation alone: food has a part too.

Why it worksKnowing the rules, why can’t you work out the result?

The 4 rules of the Game of Life can be read in a minute. But a “zoo” grew out of these four rules.

Still lifes, such as the 2×2 “block,” where every cell has exactly 3 neighbors and never changes. Oscillators, such as the “blinker,” three in a row, flipping between horizontal and vertical each generation.

Then there is the walking glider, 5 cells, shifting 1 cell diagonally every 4 generations. The “R-pentomino,” with only 5 cells, takes 1103 generations on an infinite board to settle down.

Knowing all the rules doesn’t mean you can foresee the result

This is the most unsettling thing about emergence: to know what generation 200 looks like, you mostly have to run it one generation at a time, with no shortcut. A designer writing rules is like releasing a seed, and what it grows into you have to watch grow.

Source: the “Conway’s Game of Life” entry (Wikipedia / LifeWiki). The R-pentomino’s 1103 generations, 116 live cells and 6 escaping gliders are results on an infinite grid; this page’s board has a ring border, so the result will differ. Extending it to game design is only an analogy.

Negative feedback plus delay equals oscillation

Imagine an island with rabbits and foxes. Rabbits eat grass (unlimited) and breed. Foxes eat only rabbits. The causal chain goes like this: more rabbits, foxes eat well and breed more; more foxes, more rabbits eaten; fewer rabbits, foxes starve again; fewer foxes, rabbits breed again…

This is negative feedback with a lag: the foxes rise slowly only after the rabbits become many, so the foxes always reach their peak later than the rabbits, and when the foxes overshoot, the rabbits have already begun to fall. Two stocks chasing each other draw two staggered waves.

The Lotka–Volterra model: what it shows and what it doesn’t

In the simplest model the populations cycle forever around an equilibrium point and never settle quietly on it. Verification experiment: go back to the simulation above, open “Other parameters,” raise the foxes’ death rate and see the period shorten. Then tick “Food limit,” which amounts to adding an extra negative feedback, and the oscillation gradually dies away.

This is a deliberately simplified model. Real field data (such as the lynx–hare record) is not so clean: the hares’ cycle is thought to be tied to both predation and food. The model is used to explain why oscillation appears, not to predict precisely.

Why it worksWhy does walking uphill get you stuck on a small hilltop?

After every patch, the player community soon “solves” a strongest build, and then almost everyone uses it and the game becomes uniform. Some designers complain that players are “not creative enough.”

Think of a climber in thick fog: he can’t see far, only what is underfoot, so each step goes to higher ground. He climbs the small hill beside him and everything around slopes down. He hasn’t gone wrong; he just can’t know there is a higher mountain on the other side.

Local optimum: every step is better, but it isn’t the best

This image comes from evolutionary biology. In 1932 the geneticist Sewall Wright proposed the “adaptive landscape.” He imagined each possible gene combination as a point on a terrain: the better adapted, the higher the elevation.

Natural selection is like a climber who only climbs uphill, easily trapped at a local optimum, the hill that is highest nearby but not highest overall.

To get out, you must now and then break the “go uphill” rule and try somewhere unfamiliar. Reinforcement learning discusses exactly this trade-off, exploration and exploitation: exploiting is to keep using the best known approach; exploring is to pay a little to try what hasn’t been tried. All exploiting easily traps you on a small hill; all exploring never banks any gain.

Why it worksMeadows’s “leverage points”: where is intervention most effective?

If the whole grows out of loops, which knob should you turn to change the whole? Donella Meadows, the systems-dynamics author, found in “Leverage Points: Places to Intervene in a System” that there are many “places” to intervene in a system, with very different effects.

One view of Meadows: the closer to structure, the bigger the leverage

Meadows first drew up a short list of 9 points and later expanded it to 12 kinds (the 1999 version). The gist:

  • The most inconspicuous and most often adjusted are parameters / numbers (tax rates, health, drop rates), which often change the system’s behavior very little.
  • More powerful are the strength of feedback loops, the way information flows, and the rules of the system, that is, changing the “structure.”
  • Most powerful are the goals of the system and the mindset (paradigm) behind it.

Her 12 kinds (from weakest to strongest) are: constants and parameters, buffers, stock-and-flow structure, delays, strength of negative feedback, gain of positive feedback, information flows, rules, self-organization, goals, paradigms, and transcending paradigms. You should see: turning numbers only changes “how fast the ending arrives,” not the ending itself; changing the structure changes the ending. In the snowball, as long as the “lead bonus” stays positive, even if tiny, the leader eventually wins. In inflation, as long as production exceeds fixed consumption, the total keeps rising. Only negative feedback (catch-up, proportional consumption) pulls the system toward a target.

Some problems need only a change of numbers. The boss has too little health and the player beats it in 10 seconds: that is a number too small, and adjusting health directly is cheapest and also right. Structural changes cost more and should be considered when the numbers are already reasonable and it is still dull.

Designing soil that can “grow” good play

Emergence brings a new role: the designer is more like a gardener. Choose seeds, prepare the soil, set up the fence, then see what grows, and prune.

Soil that easily grows good things has three traits: few rules but deep, systems that can interact, and rules that are consistent.

🎯

Few but deep rules

There are only a few rules, but each one pulls deeply on the whole. The more rules and exceptions, the harder it is for players to “reason and predict,” and emergence turns into noise.

🔗

Systems that can interact

Fire, water, wind and electricity are each simple, but let them act on each other (water conducts electricity, fire ignites grass, wind spreads fire) and combination play grows.

⚖️

Consistent physics and chemistry

Only when the same rule holds everywhere will players dare to “extend by analogy.” The fewer the exceptions, the easier it is to verify a player’s guesses.

Few but deep rules: Go. The rules take a few lines to write, yet the variations of play are so many that nobody can calculate them all.
Few but deep rules: LEGO. A few shapes and one uniform way of connecting can build endless things.
Few but deep rules: language. A limited set of words plus a grammar can say sentences nobody has ever said.
Interacting systems: Breath of the Wild. Metal conducts, flame ignites grass, and wind carries fire. Each system is simple, and when they meet, solutions the player works out grow from it.
Counterexample: lots of content, each isolated. A game has 30 items, each working only in its own level, with no interaction among them. That is “breadth,” not “depth,” and no new play will grow.

Two more “brake” principles.

First, leave an exit for positive feedback: a snowball mechanic is exciting, but there must be ways to reverse it, such as catch-up mechanics, resources that can be seized, and extra conditions needed to win.

Second, close the economic loop: where there is production there must be consumption, and the “faucet” and “drain” must be designed together, or the stock only climbs out of control.

Surprise can’t be eliminated. The design job is to raise the odds of good surprises and fit brakes to bad ones.

Common misconceptions

  • “Emergence = randomness / chaos”: the Game of Life has no randomness at all, and every step is deterministic. Emergence is hard to predict because the interactions among rules are complicated, which has nothing to do with rolling dice.
  • “The more rules, the deeper the game”: depth comes from the interactions among rules, regardless of how many rules there are. Many “content-rich” games in fact have systems isolated from one another, with only breadth and no emergence.
  • “When there’s an exploit, nerf the numbers”: nerfing numbers only treats the symptom. If the exploit comes from positive feedback or a missing closed loop, however much you nerf, players will take another path around it (already verified in the leverage testbed).
  • “Players all use the same build because they lack creativity”: more likely everyone is reasonably going uphill and they are stuck together in the same local optimum.

Reflection and debate

Limits. The Game of Life and the Lotka–Volterra model are both extremely simplified models. They show “why some kind of phenomenon appears,” and can’t directly tell a designer “what to set it to” in their own game. Real players learn, imitate and team up, which is far harder than rabbits and foxes.

Debate. “Emergence” itself is disputed in philosophy and science: some think it is just another way of saying “not yet worked out” (weak emergence), and others that irreducible new properties really exist at the level of the whole (strong emergence). Game design usually uses only the “weak” sense: “hard to predict, but the rules are explicit.”

Meadows’s ranking of leverage points is only her view, and in games “changing the numbers” is sometimes the right answer.

Think about it: in a game you’ve played, what play did “the designers didn’t write and the players grew themselves”?

Key points

  • Emergence: the behavior of the whole comes from interactions between the parts, and is not written by any single rule.
  • Positive feedback amplifies and negative feedback stabilizes. Stocks change slowly through flows, so feedback often has lag, and negative feedback plus lag oscillates.
  • The Game of Life uses 4 rules to show “knowing the rules ≠ knowing the result.” Volterra used equations to answer “why, with less fishing, predators actually became more.” Both models appeared to answer a specific puzzle.
  • Only walking uphill easily gets you stuck at a local optimum; getting out takes a little exploration.
  • Design points: rules few but deep, systems able to interact, physics consistent. For a problem, first look at the feedback structure, then consider changing numbers (Meadows’s view).
What you write is rules, and what the player gets is the world that grows when the rules interact. So design the feedback structure like a gardener, and don’t just turn the numbers knob.
