Chapter 5

Emergence & Systems Thinking

What you write down is rules; what players get is another world.

Learning goals

about 40 min
  • Explain emergence in your own words, tell positive feedback from negative feedback, and say what questions the Game of Life and the predator–prey model were originally meant to answer.
  • Explain why negative feedback with a delay swings back and forth, and why someone who only “walks uphill” gets stuck on a small hilltop.
  • Judge whether a systems problem calls for “changing the numbers” or “changing the feedback structure,” and design rules that grow interesting play more easily.
1

What it isEmergence: simple rules, a complex whole

At dusk a huge flock of starlings churns across the sky like a flowing black curtain. You are bound to wonder: who is directing them?

The answer often given is: nobody. Each bird does just three things: don’t bump into the birds near you, fly in about the same direction, and don’t stray too far from the flock. All three rules care only about the few birds beside it, and none mentions “the shape of the whole flock.” Yet put thousands of such birds together and an enormous pattern that no single bird “knows” appears.

This is emergence: the whole has behavior that the parts lack, and this behavior comes from the interactions between the parts.

Designed

What the designer writes down directly: rules, numbers, assets. You can count them one by one.

Emerged

What “grows” once the rules interact: tactics, schools of play, economic bubbles, unexpected ways to play. You can’t count them; you can only observe.

Emergence in games

Board games. The rules of chess fit on one page, yet they grew opening theory, endgame studies and schools of play. No rule says “control the center”; players “discovered” it by running into the rules again and again.
Redstone in Minecraft. Redstone was added around July 2010, in a “secret update” during testing, as a material that carries signals. Its rules are plain, yet players built logic gates, calculators and even whole computers with it. None of that was on the design list.
Combos in fighting games. Noritaka Funamizu, the producer of Street Fighter II, found while checking for bugs that with the right timing another hit could be chained on. At first he thought the timing too hard and left it as a hidden trick; later the team loosened the timing window and made it an official feature. An interaction nobody had foreseen became the cornerstone of a whole genre (sources differ a little on the details).

Emergence beyond games

The computer version of a flock: Boids. Craig Reynolds made an animation program around 1986, introduced in his 1987 SIGGRAPH paper “Flocks, Herds, and Schools”: each “boid” follows just three local rules, separation (don’t get too close), alignment (head the same way as neighbors) and cohesion (steer toward the center of neighbors). There is no leader, yet a convincing flock appears.
Phantom traffic jams. In 2008, Sugiyama and colleagues ran an experiment in Japan: twenty-odd cars drove on a circular road at similar speeds with no obstacles. After a few minutes a jam wave appeared by itself and propagated backward against the flow of traffic. Their conclusion: a traffic jam is a collective phenomenon of the cars’ interactions, and an obstacle is only a trigger.
A pile of things is not emergence. A level holds 50 monsters, each patrolling on its own script and ignoring one another. That is just “a lot of them.” Emergence needs interaction: monsters, monsters and player, player and environment must be able to pull on each other.
“Random” is not emergence. The chaos from rolling dice has no “new structure of the whole.” The Game of Life you will play shortly has no randomness at all, yet grows a walking glider. That is emergence.
2

What it isThe first tool for making the whole “come alive”: feedback loops

Remember the circle of cause in Monopoly: “collect rent, buy more property, collect more rent”? A structure in which a result goes around and affects its own cause is called a feedback loop. It has two basic forms:

  • Positive feedback (a reinforcing loop): change is amplified. More money, better gear, more wins, even more money. The result is a snowball, winner-takes-all, or a crash.
  • Negative feedback (a balancing loop): change is cancelled out and the system is pulled toward a target. Body too hot, you sweat; temperature falls, sweating stops.
Snowballing in a competitive game. Kills give gold, gold buys gear, and gear brings more kills. The first lead is small, but a few minutes later it is an overwhelming advantage.
Recommendations on a video platform. Videos with high view counts are pushed to more people, and more viewers push the count higher. Nobody decides “this video should go viral”; the loop lifts it by itself.
A thermostat. When the room gets hot the heating goes off, when it gets cold the heating comes on, and the temperature is always pulled back to the setting. This is the classic negative feedback.
Catch-up items in Mario Kart. Players who are behind are more likely to get powerful items, which pull the gap back (the exact weights aren’t public and differ between entries). It gives those trailing a chance to stay in the race.
Negative feedback overdone. If the catch-up mechanism is too strong, every bit of effort by the leader gets flattened and players feel “being ahead is meaningless.” This is what the “rubber band” mechanic is often criticized for. Negative feedback is meant to help the losing stay in the game, not to erase the success of the winning.
3

What it isStocks and flows: why big systems turn slowly

Look at your bank balance. Your salary is a tap, a little flowing in every month; rent and food are drains, a little flowing out every month; and the total lying in the account right now is your money.

These are a stock and a flow. A stock is the total saved up at a moment, and a flow is the speed at which something enters or leaves per unit of time. Health, gold and monster counts in a game are all stocks.

The key intuition: a stock can only be changed bit by bit by its flows, and you can’t make a bathtub’s water level double in an instant. So a system with a large stock reacts slowly and is not easily knocked over by a single shock.

4

What it isDelay and oscillation: why well-meant adjustments swing wilder and wilder

Put negative feedback and stocks together and a counterintuitive phenomenon pops up. Think of taking a shower: the water is too cold, so you turn the tap toward hot; but the pipe still holds cold water that hasn’t warmed up, so you turn it again; by the time the hot water finally arrives it’s scalding, you hurriedly turn it back, and the water slowly turns cold again…

You aren’t stupid. The problem is the delay between the action and its result. This time gap is called delay. Add a delay to negative feedback and the system swings back and forth around its target, which is called oscillation.

Shower temperature. Between turning the tap and the hot water actually arriving there are a few seconds of delay; you adjust to the temperature “now” while affecting the temperature a few seconds from now, so it swings cold, scalding, cold.
Tides in a game economy. Monsters are wiped out, drops decrease, players move elsewhere, monsters pile up at the respawn points, and the spawn points fill up with players again. Prices, dungeon popularity and class strength on a server often rise and fall like this.
The Beer Game. A teaching game from MIT in which participants play the four stations of retailer, wholesaler, distributor and factory, placing orders and waiting for shipments, with delays in between. John Sterman of MIT used it in experiments in 1989 and found that people often overlook how their own ordering decisions feed back into the system, and the swings in orders grow larger at each station.
No delay, no swing. If the water temperature changed the instant you turned the tap, you could quickly settle it at the spot you wanted. So the culprit behind the swinging is not negative feedback itself but that it reacts too slowly.

How it was inventedThe Game of Life: how far can the simplest rules go?

A game “nobody would actually play,” and why it became the emblem of emergence

In the 1940s, John von Neumann and Stanisław Ulam were studying “cellular automata”: cut the world into squares and let each square interact with its neighbors by rules. Von Neumann wanted to know one thing: could a machine build itself? The cellular machine he designed had frighteningly intricate rules.

More than twenty years later, the Cambridge mathematician John Conway took up the question and asked it differently: could extremely simple rules also produce unexpected behavior? From 1968 he tried various two-dimensional rules with pencil and paper (there were no personal computers then). In October 1970, Martin Gardner introduced it in his “Mathematical Games” column in Scientific American, under the title “The fantastic combinations of John Conway’s new solitaire game ‘life.’” Letters from readers poured in, and everyone started studying patterns.

Now you be Conway: here are 4 rules and a small 5-cell pattern. Don’t look at the answer yet; work it out yourself.

There is no word “move” in the rules, but by generation 4 you will find that the whole pattern has shifted one square to the lower right. It was later called the glider. Conway conjectured that no pattern can grow without limit, and offered a $50 prize for a proof. How it was inventedThe puzzle of the Adriatic catch

With no fishing, why were there more big fish?

During World War I (around 1915–1918), fishermen in the Adriatic Sea could hardly put to sea. After the war, the Italian marine biologist Umberto D’Ancona went through fish-market data and found something strange: with less fishing, the share of the catch made up of fish that eat other fish (mainly sharks and rays) had actually gone up. He couldn’t understand it: wouldn’t not fishing be good for all the fish?

He handed the problem to a mathematician, his father-in-law Vito Volterra. Volterra built a minimal two-species equation, published in Nature in 1926. The American Alfred Lotka had written down the same equations in his 1925 book, which is where the name “Lotka–Volterra model” comes from.

Before seeing how he explained it, take a guess: if rabbits and foxes are removed in equal proportion (standing in for “fishing”), then fishing stops, will the foxes’ share among all animals go up, go down, or stay the same?

Why? The pattern Volterra’s model gives is: fishing pushes down both populations, but hurts the predators more. The average number of foxes is proportional to (rabbit birth rate − fishing), and the average number of rabbits is proportional to (fox death rate + fishing). When fishing stops, the fox average rises, the rabbit average falls, and the predator share goes up. This is the conclusion later called “Volterra’s principle.”

1942 Elton and Nicholson compile the Hudson’s Bay Company’s fur purchase records, in which the lynx–snowshoe hare cycle is widely cited, with the lynx peak always a little after the hare’s.
1995 Krebs and colleagues run an eight-year field experiment at Kluane in the Yukon, adding food and keeping out predators, and find that the hare cycle can’t be put down to predation alone, since food plays a part too; when both are manipulated together, hare density rises about 11-fold.

Why it worksKnowing the rules, why can’t you work out the result?

Anyone can read the 4 rules of the Game of Life in under a minute. But a whole “zoo” grows out of those four.

Stable states, such as the 2×2 “block,” where every square has exactly 3 neighbors and nothing ever changes. Oscillators, such as the “blinker,” three in a row that flips between horizontal and vertical each generation. And the walking glider, 5 squares that shift 1 square along the diagonal every 4 generations. The “R-pentomino” has only 5 squares, yet on an infinite board it takes 1,103 generations to settle down, ending with 116 live cells, of which 6 are gliders that have escaped.

Knowing all the rules doesn’t mean you can foresee the result

This is the most unsettling thing about emergence: to know what generation 200 looks like, most of the time you can only run it honestly, one generation at a time. The simpler the rules, the less you can tell the ending by “looking at the rules.” A designer who writes rules is like someone releasing a seed: what it grows into, you have to watch it grow.

Sources: the “Conway’s Game of Life” entries (Wikipedia / LifeWiki: R-pentomino 1,103 generations, 116 live cells, 6 escaped gliders, are results on an infinite grid; the board on this page wraps around, so the outcome differs). Extending it to game design is an analogy borrowed here, not a theorem.

Negative feedback plus delay equals oscillation

Picture an island with rabbits and foxes. Rabbits eat grass (unlimited) and breed; foxes eat only rabbits. The chain of cause goes like this: with lots of rabbits, the foxes eat well and breed a lot; with lots of foxes, many rabbits get eaten; with few rabbits left, the foxes starve; with few foxes, the rabbits breed again…

This is negative feedback with a delay: the stock of foxes has to wait for the rabbits to become plentiful before it slowly climbs, so the foxes always peak later than the rabbits, and by the time the foxes overshoot, the rabbits have already started to fall. Two stocks chasing each other draw two waves offset in time.

The Lotka–Volterra model: what it shows and what it doesn’t

In the simplest model, the populations cycle around an equilibrium point forever instead of settling quietly onto it. For small amplitudes the period is about 2π/√((birth rate − fishing) × (death rate + fishing)). Verification experiment: go back to the simulation above, expand “other parameters,” raise the fox death rate and watch the period get shorter; then tick “food cap,” which adds an extra negative feedback, and the oscillation gradually dies away.

This is a deliberately simplified model. Real field data (such as the often-cited “lynx–snowshoe hare” records) are not this clean: the hare cycle is thought to involve both predation and food, and scholars are still debating it. The safest use of the model is “to explain why oscillation appears,” not to predict precisely.

Why it worksWhy does walking uphill get you stuck on a small hilltop?

After every patch, a player community “solves” the strongest build very quickly, and then nearly everyone uses it and the game becomes monotonous. Designers often complain that players “aren’t creative enough.” But think of a climber in thick fog: he can’t see far, only his feet, so every step goes to higher ground. He climbs the small hill next to him and finds it slopes down on every side. He did nothing wrong, and has no way of knowing there is a higher mountain on the other side.

Local optimum: every step is better, but it isn’t the best

This image comes from evolutionary biology. In 1932, the geneticist Sewall Wright proposed the “adaptive landscape” at the International Congress of Genetics: picture each possible combination of genes as a point on a landscape, where the better adapted, the higher the elevation. Natural selection is like a climber who can only go uphill, easily trapped on a local optimum, a peak that is the highest nearby but not the highest overall.

To get out takes something more: now and then not following the rule of “walk uphill” and trying somewhere unfamiliar. Reinforcement learning calls this exploration versus exploitation: to exploit is to keep using the best-known approach, and to explore is to pay a little to try what you haven’t tried. Relying only on exploitation, you get trapped on a small hill; relying only on exploration, you never save up any gains.

Why it worksMeadows’s “leverage points”: where is intervention most effective?

If the whole grows out of loops, which knob should you turn to change the whole? Donella Meadows, the systems-dynamics author, found in “Leverage Points: Places to Intervene in a System” that there are many “places” to intervene in a system, with very different effects.

One view of Meadows: the closer to structure, the bigger the leverage

Meadows first drew up a short list of 9 points and later expanded it to 12 kinds (the 1999 version). The gist:

  • The least conspicuous and most often adjusted are parameters / numbers (tax rates, health, drop rates), which often change system behavior very little;
  • More powerful are the strength of feedback loops, how information flows, and the rules of the system, which is to say changing the “structure”;
  • Most powerful are the goals of the system and the mindset (paradigm) behind it.

Her 12 kinds (from weakest to strongest) are: constants and parameters, buffers, stock-and-flow structure, delays, strength of negative feedback, gain of positive feedback, information flows, rules, self-organization, goals, paradigms, and transcending paradigms. Tap the steps below to see what each level corresponds to in a game.

This is an empirical view and classification of Meadows, not a strict theorem; some think its ordering doesn’t fit every situation. But “don’t just turn the knobs, look at the loops first” is a very useful design habit.

You should have seen: turning the numbers changes only “how fast the ending arrives,” not the ending itself; changing the structure changes the ending. This is no coincidence. In the snowball, as long as the “lead bonus” stays positive, however small, the leader eventually wins; in the inflation, as long as output exceeds fixed spending, the total keeps rising. Only negative feedback (catch-up, proportional spending) pulls the system toward a target.

Of course, not every problem needs a structural change. If the boss has so little health that players beat it in 10 seconds, the number is simply too small, and adjusting the health directly is cheapest and also right; structural changes cost more and should be considered when the numbers are already reasonable but it is still dull. Meadows also admitted that parameters are the “lowest-leverage” point, but not “useless.”

Designing soil that can “grow” good play

If you used to think “design means writing out every detail,” emergence invites you to change posture: you are more like a gardener than an architect. You pick the seeds, prepare the soil, set up the fence, then watch what grows, and prune.

What kind of soil grows good things easily? Three traits: few but deep rules, systems that can interact, and consistent rules.

🎯

Few but deep rules

There are only a few rules, but each one pulls deeply on the whole. The more rules and exceptions, the harder it is for players to “reason and predict,” and emergence turns into noise.

🔗

Systems that can interact

Fire, water, wind and electricity are each simple, but let them act on each other (water conducts electricity, fire ignites grass, wind spreads fire) and combination play grows.

⚖️

Consistent physics and chemistry

Only when the same rule holds everywhere will players dare to “extend by analogy.” The fewer the exceptions, the easier it is to verify a player’s guesses.

Few but deep rules: Go. The rules take a few lines to write, yet the variations of play are so many that nobody can calculate them all.
Few but deep rules: LEGO. A few shapes and one uniform way of connecting can build endless things.
Few but deep rules: language. A limited set of words plus a grammar can say sentences nobody has ever said.
Interacting systems: Breath of the Wild. Metal conducts electricity, fire ignites grassland, wind carries flames. Each system is simple, but where they meet, solutions that players work out for themselves grow.
Lots of content, but each isolated. A game has 30 kinds of items, each working only in its own level, with no interaction between them. That is “breadth,” not “depth,” and it will not grow new play.

Two more “brake” principles. First, give positive feedback an outlet: snowballing mechanics are thrilling, but there should be ways to reverse them, such as catch-up mechanics, resources that can be seized, and extra conditions needed to win. Second, close the economic loop: where there is output there should be spending, and the “tap” and the “drain” should be designed together, otherwise the stock only rises out of control. Some emergence lengthens a game’s life (tactical depth, player creation) and some wrecks it (infinite-money exploits, a snowball ending the match early). The designer’s job is not to eliminate surprise but to raise the odds of good surprises and put brakes on bad ones.

Common misconceptions

  • “Emergence = randomness / chaos”: the Game of Life has no randomness at all, and every step is deterministic. The unpredictability of emergence comes from the complexity of interactions between rules, not from rolling dice.
  • “The more rules, the deeper the game”: depth comes from the interactions between rules, not the number of rules. Many “content-rich” games have systems that are isolated from each other, with breadth but no emergence.
  • “Find an exploit and cut the numbers”: cutting numbers only treats the symptom. If the exploit comes from positive feedback or a missing closed loop, however much you cut, players will take another path around it (you already verified this on the leverage test bench).
  • “Everyone using the same build means players lack creativity”: more likely everyone is walking uphill reasonably, and together they are stuck in the same local optimum.

Reflection and debate

Limits. The Game of Life and the Lotka–Volterra model are both extremely simplified. They show “why a certain kind of phenomenon appears,” but can’t tell you directly “what to set it to” in your own game. Real players learn, imitate and band together, and the “feedback” the rules give them keeps changing, which is far harder than rabbits and foxes.

Debate. “Emergence” itself is disputed in philosophy and science: some think it is just another way of saying “we haven’t worked it out yet” (weak emergence), while others think irreducible new properties really exist at the level of the whole (strong emergence). Game design usually uses only the “weak” sense, meaning “hard to predict, but the rules are explicit.” Also, Meadows’s ranking of leverage points is one view of Meadows, and in games “changing the numbers” is sometimes the right answer.

Think about it: in a game you have played, which kinds of play were “not written by the designer but grown by the players themselves”?

Key points

  • Emergence: the behavior of the whole comes from interactions between the parts, and is not written by any single rule.
  • Positive feedback amplifies, negative feedback stabilizes; stocks change slowly through flows, so feedback often comes with delay, and negative feedback plus delay oscillates.
  • The Game of Life uses 4 rules to show that “knowing the rules ≠ knowing the result”; Volterra used equations to answer “why do predators increase when fishing decreases?” Both models appeared to answer a specific puzzle.
  • Only walking uphill easily gets you stuck at a local optimum; getting out takes a little exploration.
  • Design points: rules that are few but deep, systems that can interact, physics that is consistent; for problems, look at the feedback structure first, then consider changing numbers (Meadows’s view).
What you write down is rules, and what players get is the world that grows from the rules’ interactions; so design feedback structures like a gardener, instead of just turning the number knobs.
